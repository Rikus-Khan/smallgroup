Acts 7

The man who forgave

Stephen tells Israel's story to the men about to kill him, then prays for them as the stones fall.

Part one

The God of glory

Verses 1–8

Asked a yes-or-no question, Stephen starts at the beginning, with one man who owned nothing.

A man on trial

Stephen was one of the seven men the church chose to serve and look after the widows (Acts 6). Now he stands before the same council that condemned Jesus. Liars said he spoke against the temple and the law of Moses (6:13–14). The high priest asks: “Are these things so?”

Stephen doesn't say yes or no. He tells them their own story, the one every man in the room knew by heart. He's going to show them, from their own Scriptures, that God has never been tied to one building, and that Israel has a habit of pushing away the men God sends.

He isn't saying this to win. He's talking to men who are about to kill him, and every word is meant to turn them around. (Luke says his face looked like an angel's, 6:15.)

Stephen's sermon in one line

  1. about 2000 BCAbraham is called7:2–8

That's about two thousand years in one sermon. Watch for one thing that keeps repeating: God sends a man to save His people, they refuse him, and God raises him up anyway.

Dates here are approximate. Scholars place Abraham and Moses a little earlier or later. What matters is the order.

Called to walk away

Abraham's family lived in Ur of the Chaldees in Mesopotamia (“between the rivers”), far to the east, past the edge of this map. Stephen says the God of glory appeared to him there. He had no Bible, no church, no promise yet. God spoke first.

Charran is Haran, in the north. The family stopped and settled until Abraham's father died. Then he moved on, into the land where these very men were standing. It was a walk of hundreds of miles on God's word alone.

What Abraham owned

0acres. Not enough to set his foot on
0children, “when as yet he had no child”
1promise from the God of glory

He had nothing to show for it, and God told him it would be four hundred years before his children owned the land. Abraham died a stranger in it. He lived on the promise anyway.

“And he believed in the LORD; and he counted it to him for righteousness.”

Genesis 15:6

That's grace. God didn't count Abraham righteous for what he did. He counted it because he believed. A man in recovery with nothing in his hands is in good company.

Believing first, the sign after

Verse 6 sums up four hundred years in a sentence: a strange land, then bondage. God said it before it happened, and He also said He would judge the nation that enslaved them, and bring them out (verse 7).

First

He believed

“He believed in the LORD; and he counted it to him for righteousness” (Genesis 15:6).

Then

The sign

The covenant of circumcision. A mark on the body showing he belonged to God. It was given after he believed, not to make him believe.

Baptism works the same way for us. It doesn't make anyone right with God. It's the step a believer takes to show it. We'll see that again in Acts 8.

And notice what's in verse 8: Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, the twelve sons. God works through ordinary, flawed families, one generation at a time.

Part two

God was with him

Verses 9–16

A boy sold by his own brothers ends up running Egypt, and feeds the men who did it.

Sold, and not alone

his brothersmoved with envy, sold himJoseph“God was with him”governor over Egypt

Envy is an ugly thing, and Stephen names it. Joseph's brothers weren't monsters. They were jealous of their father's favourite, and it grew until they sold him. Envy and resentment have wrecked more homes than most of us can count.

Joseph's afflictions were real: the pit, slavery, prison. The text doesn't skip them. It says “but God was with him,” in the pit too. Years later Joseph looked back on it and said:

“But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive.”

Genesis 50:20

God doesn't cause evil, and He doesn't call it good. But He can take the worst thing done to you, and the worst thing you did, and still bring something out of it.

Down to Egypt for bread

A dearth is a famine. Chanaan is Canaan, the land God promised. Egypt had grain because Joseph had stored it up. Egypt was the place with food.

The brothers came the first time and didn't know him (verse 12). The second time, “Joseph was made known to his brethren.” The brother they had sold was holding the bread, and he fed them instead of getting even.

Seventy-five people

“threescore and fifteen souls”

Threescore is three times twenty: sixty, plus fifteen. A small family went down. Four hundred years later they were a nation of hundreds of thousands. God keeps His promise even when it takes generations.

Verse 16 is a hard detail. Genesis says Abraham bought a burial cave at Hebron, and Jacob bought land at Shechem (Genesis 23 and 33; Joshua 24:32). Stephen is giving the family's story fast, and he joins the two purchases. A summary can do that. It doesn't touch his point: even in death, they wanted to lie in the land God promised.

Part three

Who made thee a ruler?

Verses 17–43

Moses tries to save Israel his own way and fails. God sends him back forty years later, and they refuse him again.

Forgotten, and then hunted

  1. a new king“knew not Joseph” (7:18)
  2. dealt subtillycrafty, slippery (7:19)
  3. evil entreatedtreated cruelly (7:19)
  4. their babies cast out(7:19)

Everything Joseph did was forgotten in a generation. That's how it goes: a good man's name fades and the next king doesn't care. Subtilly means with craft, with clever lies and small steps. Cruelty usually starts that way.

It looked like the end of the story. It's exactly when God's promise “drew nigh” (verse 17). The darkest hour of the story is when He moves.

A baby in the right house

a baby cast out → raised in the palace The very king who ordered the babies killed paid for the baby's upbringing. His mother's family kept him three months, and then the daughter of Pharaoh “nourished him for her own son.”

Moses was “learned in all the wisdom of the Egyptians,” and “mighty in words and in deeds.” He had the best education in the world. And God was going to use him only after he had lost every bit of it.

Forty, forty, forty

40 yearsa prince in Egypt (7:23)
40 yearsa stranger in Madian, with sheep (7:29–30)
40 yearsleading them through the wilderness (7:36)

Moses was sure he was the one to deliver Israel, and he went about it his own way: at forty, he killed an Egyptian. “He supposed his brethren would have understood”, but they didn't, and he ran. A man who thought he was somebody ended up a refugee.

Plenty of us have a chapter like that: something done in anger that can't be undone, and the years of hiding that came after it. Moses spent forty years with it. And then God called him anyway, at eighty, from a bush. The worst thing he ever did was not the end of his story.

A bush that burned and didn't burn up

“I have seen, I have seen”

Sina is Sinai, the mountain in the desert. After forty years in hiding, Moses sees a bush on fire that doesn't burn up, and he's afraid to look.

“Put off thy shoes from thy feet: for the place where thou standest is holy ground” (verse 33). A patch of desert, holy because God was there. And His first words: I've been watching all along, and I heard the groaning.

If you've ever groaned at the bottom of something where nobody seemed to hear, this is for you. He sees, and He hears.

Refused, then sent

“Who made thee a ruler and a judge?” → “the same did God send” They shoved him away at forty. God sent “this Moses whom they refused” back at eighty, as “a ruler and a deliverer.” The man they rejected became the man who saved them.

Stephen is not just telling a Bible story. He is showing his judges what's about to happen again, because there's another man Israel pushed away: Jesus. Hold that thought. It comes back in verse 52.

From Egypt to the wilderness

The first line is Jacob's family going down for bread. Then watch what Moses does.

Moses runs from Egypt to Madian (Midian) and Sinai, which are just south of this map. He spends forty years there with his sheep.

Then he leads the people out through wonders and signs, through the Red sea, and into the wilderness forty years. The people he led out would not obey him.

“Him shall ye hear”

Moses told Israel that God would raise up a prophet like unto me. Moses was pointing ahead to Jesus. The council is about to hear Stephen say they've killed the very One Moses told them to hear.

“The church in the wilderness” is not a building. The Greek word means an assembly, a gathered people. The church was always people, and in the wilderness there wasn't even a roof.

“Lively oracles” means living words: God's words that give life. Israel received them. Having them in your hands is not the same as doing them. They didn't obey.

Free, and still looking back

They were out of Egypt, but “in their hearts turned back again into Egypt.” Rescued from slavery, and they still missed it. Every man who's been clean a while knows that pull.

While Moses was up the mountain they panicked: “Make us gods to go before us.” They “rejoiced in the works of their own hands.”

“If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness.”

1 John 1:9

“God turned, and gave them up”

This is a hard verse. God didn't give them up the first time they turned back. He put up with it for forty years in the wilderness and sent prophet after prophet. Stephen is quoting Amos (5:25–27). In the end God let them have what they kept choosing: the host of heaven (the stars), Moloch and Remphan, until Babylon carried them off.

He gave them up to their own gods. A man picks the thing over God again and again, until God lets him have it, and he finds out what it costs.

It's a warning, not a sentence on a man who turns. In this very chapter God keeps pointing the way: “him shall ye hear.” The door is open today.

Part four

Not made with hands

Verses 44–53

The temple was the charge against Stephen. He answers it, then turns on his judges.

A tent, then a house

The tabernacle

a tent

Built “according to the fashion that he had seen.” It moved with them through the wilderness for centuries, even into the land with Joshua (“Jesus” in verse 45 is Joshua).

Solomon's house

stone

David “found favour before God” and wanted to build a house for God. Solomon actually built it, about 960 BC.

For most of their history, God's presence travelled with His people in a tent. The temple came later. Stephen's point is that the building was never the heart of it.

A God too big for a building

Stephen isn't mocking the temple. Solomon himself said the heaven of heavens couldn't contain God. The sin was treating the building as if it held Him, and caring more about the house than about the One who lived in it.

“For all those things hath mine hand made, and all those things have been, saith the LORD: but to this man will I look, even to him that is poor and of a contrite spirit, and trembleth at my word.”

Isaiah 66:2

So where does He live?

In His people

“What? know ye not that your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost which is in you, which ye have of God, and ye are not your own?”

1 Corinthians 6:19

The stone temple in Jerusalem was torn down by Rome in about AD 70, and not one stone was left on another. A believer can't be torn down.

If you belong to Jesus, God's Spirit lives in you, wherever you are, today. You don't have to get to a special place to find Him, and no failure has driven Him out.

Hard words, said in love

“Ye do always resist the Holy Ghost”

Stiffnecked is an ox that won't be turned. Uncircumcised in heart and ears means they had the outward mark but never let God deal with the heart. They heard everything and listened to none of it.

Every prophet was pushed away, Stephen says, and now they've done it to “the Just One.” They had the law, “and have not kept it.” None of us has.

A man can resist the Spirit. When a hard truth lands and your teeth clench (verse 54), you can answer with rage or ask, is it true? Most turning around starts there.

The last column is why this matters. The men who shoved Joseph aside ended up fed by him. The men who shoved Moses aside were led out by him. And the One they shoved aside is still offering the same thing: “whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord shall be saved” (Romans 10:13).

Part five

Lord, lay not this sin

Verses 54–60

They grind their teeth. Stephen looks up, sees Jesus, and forgives them.

Heaven opened

Cut to the heart means furious, not sorry: they gnashed on him with their teeth. Stephen, full of the Holy Ghost, looked up, away from them, and saw the glory of God.

He saw Jesus standing. Usually Jesus is seated at God's right hand, His work finished. Here He's on His feet, as if rising to receive His man. Stephen was not alone in that room, and neither are you.

A young man named Saul

They stopped their ears, the same ears Stephen said were closed, and rushed him. They dragged him outside the city to stone him. The witnesses threw the first stones, so they took off their coats and piled them at the feet of a young man: Saul.

Saul didn't throw a stone. He stood there and watched, and agreed with it. Luke will say so in the next line: “Saul was consenting unto his death” (8:1). Sometimes the quiet part is the sin.

The man who held the coats

Acts 22:20

And when the blood of thy martyr Stephen was shed, I also was standing by, and consenting unto his death, and kept the raiment of them that slew him.

1 Timothy 1:13

Who was before a blasphemer, and a persecutor, and injurious: but I obtained mercy, because I did it ignorantly in unbelief.

Years later Saul, now Paul, told the story himself. He never forgot that day. And Jesus got hold of him anyway, in Acts 9. If God can reach the man holding the coats, He isn't finished with you.

He prayed like his Lord

Luke 23:34, Jesus on the cross

Then said Jesus, Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do.

Acts 7:60, Stephen under the stones

And he kneeled down, and cried with a loud voice, Lord, lay not this sin to their charge.

Luke 23:46, Jesus

…Father, into thy hands I commend my spirit: and having said thus, he gave up the ghost.

Acts 7:59, Stephen

…calling upon God, and saying, Lord Jesus, receive my spirit.

Stephen died the way Jesus did, forgiving the men doing it. And “he fell asleep”: for a believer, death is falling asleep in the arms of the One he'd just seen standing.

“Lord, lay not this sin to their charge”

Stephen wasn't saying what they did was fine. It was murder. He was handing it to God to deal with, instead of carrying it himself.

A lot of us are carrying someone: a father, an ex, a boss, a man who hurt us before we were old enough to fight back. Resentment is a heavy thing to hold, and it's the kind that takes a drink to carry. Forgiving doesn't make it okay. It puts the weight down.

Today: pick one name. Say, out loud if you can, Lord, I give You this one. Ask God to do for him what He did for Saul. You don't have to feel it yet. Stephen prayed it with stones in the air.

Question 1 of 6

God's promise

What did God promise Abraham when he had no land and no child?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

That He would give him the land he was standing in, and that his children would own it after him, even though Abraham didn't own a foot of it and had no child. Abraham believed, and God counted it as righteousness. God kept the promise, about four hundred years later.

Question 2 of 6

Refused

Whom did Israel push away, and then God sent to save them?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Moses. They shoved him away with “Who made thee a ruler and a judge?”, and God sent that same man to be their ruler and deliverer. Joseph went the same way, and so did Jesus, whom the council had just betrayed and killed.

Question 3 of 6

Turning back

Where did Israel's hearts turn back to in the wilderness?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

To Egypt, where they'd been slaves. They were free, but they missed the old life, and they made a golden calf and celebrated what their own hands had made. It's the pull every man in recovery knows. The way out is the same one Moses gave them: hear Him, and confess it.

Question 4 of 6

God's house

Does the most High live in houses made with hands?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

No. Heaven is His throne and earth is His footstool, and no house can hold Him. He made all of it. He lives in His people by the Holy Spirit, so you can talk to Him anywhere today.

Question 5 of 6

Heaven opened

What did Stephen see when he looked up?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

The glory of God, and Jesus standing at God's right hand. He saw it while a mob ground their teeth at him. Jesus was already there, on His feet for him.

Question 6 of 6

Forgiveness

What did Stephen pray as the stones hit him?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

“Lord Jesus, receive my spirit,” and “Lord, lay not this sin to their charge.” He forgave the men killing him, with a young man named Saul standing by. That young man later became Paul.
