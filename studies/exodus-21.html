Exodus 21

The first judgments

After the ten commandments, God gives the first rules for how a free people live together: servants who go free, a life valued, eye for eye, and make it good. Behind them is a ransom.

Part one

“If thou buy an Hebrew servant”

Verses 1–11

How a man who owed a debt could work it off, and how the law protected him and the women of the house.

From the ten words to the judgments

  1. chapter 20The ten wordsGod's holy standard
  2. chapters 21–23The judgmentshow a nation lives and settles disputes
  3. chapter 24The covenant sealedwith blood

A judgment is a ruling: how a court should settle a case. These chapters are civil law for Israel's courts, for a nation of freed slaves with no land, no police, and judges only just appointed (18:25–26).

Some of it is hard for us to read. We will take it plainly, as it was given: a law that limited revenge, protected the weak, and put a high price on a human life. Where it points to Jesus, we will say so.

Six years, then free

year 6serve
year 7free

This is not the slavery of Egypt. A Hebrew servant was usually a man who owed a debt, or had nothing, and worked it off in another man's house. The law set a limit: six years, and in the seventh he goes out free “for nothing”: without paying.

The law kept it fair: if he came in alone, he went out alone; if he came in married, his wife went out with him. If the master gave him a wife while he served, she and her children stayed with the master. That was a real cost, and the law does not hide it. It left the man a choice, in the next verses.

“And if thy brother, an Hebrew man, or an Hebrew woman, be sold unto thee, and serve thee six years; then in the seventh year thou shalt let him go free from thee. And when thou sendest him out free from thee, thou shalt not let him go away empty: Thou shalt furnish him liberally out of thy flock, and out of thy floor, and out of thy winepress: of that wherewith the LORD thy God hath blessed thee thou shalt give unto him. And thou shalt remember that thou wast a bondman in the land of Egypt, and the LORD thy God redeemed thee: therefore I command thee this thing to day.”

Deuteronomy 15:12–15

Moses repeats the rule later and adds what the master must do: do not send him away empty. And the reason: “remember that thou wast a bondman in the land of Egypt.” Israel was told to treat the man in debt the way God had treated them.

The servant who stays

  • aula small pointed tool for piercing: an awl
  • judgeshe goes before them: a public, open choice
  • door postthe ear pinned to the door of the house: his home for life

After six years he may go free. But if he says, “I love my master, my wife, and my children; I will not go out free,” he can stay, for good. It must be said plainly, before the judges: nobody could force it.

The mark in his ear says: I am here because I love him. It is the choice of a man who could have gone and did not.

The ear, and the Servant

“Sacrifice and offering thou didst not desire; mine ears hast thou opened: burnt offering and sin offering hast thou not required. Then said I, Lo, I come: in the volume of the book it is written of me, I delight to do thy will, O my God: yea, thy law is within my heart.”

Psalm 40:6–8

Many Bible teachers see a picture in the opened ear: the Servant who came to do his Father's will, because he loved. Jesus said of himself that he came “not to be ministered unto, but to minister” (Mark 10:45, which we will see again).

Notice what the story says to us: the best freedom is not leaving, it is being where you love to be. Some of you spent years as a slave to something you hated. The Lord is a master you can love.

“Jesus answered them, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Whosoever committeth sin is the servant of sin. And the servant abideth not in the house for ever: but the Son abideth ever. If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed.”

John 8:34–36

A daughter given into a household

Here a poor father gives his daughter to a household as a maidservant, to become the wife of the master or of his son. Betrothed means promised in marriage. The law is there to protect her, because she has no one else to speak for her.

  • No selling her on“to sell her unto a strange nation he shall have no power” (strange means foreign)
  • Treat her as a daughterif she is for his son, “after the manner of daughters”
  • Three things owedher food, her raiment (clothing), and her marriage rights

If he fails to give her those three things, she goes out free, “without money”: she owes nothing. The woman in the weakest place has a claim on the strongest man in the house.

Part two

“Surely put to death”

Verses 12–27

The law's weightiest words: a life is a life, and the weak get justice too.

An accident, or guile

He did not lie in wait

A place to flee

“God deliver him into his hand”: it happened, and he did not plan it. God says he will appoint “a place whither he shall flee.”

He came upon his neighbour

No shelter

“Presumptuously” means with arrogance, on purpose, “with guile”: with cunning. Not even God's altar protects him.

Life is the first thing God protects, and the law makes the difference between a death by accident and a death by plan. The place of refuge is named later, in Numbers 35.

“That by two immutable things, in which it was impossible for God to lie, we might have a strong consolation, who have fled for refuge to lay hold upon the hope set before us:”

Hebrews 6:18

Every man in this room knows what it is to need a place to flee to. The Lord is one. You do not have to be innocent to run to him, only to run.

What God counts as grave

  • Striking a parenta son who lifts his hand against his father or mother
  • Stealing a mankidnapping a person and selling him
  • Cursing a parentspeaking contempt for the people who gave him life

These are the penalties of Israel's courts, in a nation where the family held the whole life of the people together and where there was no police. We are not under those courts, but we can see what God valued.

Notice the middle one. A man who stole a human being and sold him (Joseph's own story began that way) was to die for it. A person is never merchandise. The New Testament still lists “menstealers” among the sins the law condemns (1 Timothy 1:9–10).

The cost of a fight

  • strivefight, quarrel
  • quitcleared of blame, let off
  • loss of his timethe wages he lost while he could not work
  • thoroughly healedpaid in full until he is well

If a man hurt another in a fight and the man lived, he was not put to death. But he paid for the days lost and the doctor until the man was well. Even a fight had a bill, and the man who started it had to pay it.

Real world: if your anger or your hands have hurt someone, the question is not only “will I be punished?” but “what do I owe this man?” Ask your pastor how to make it right.

The master's rod

This is one of the hard verses in the chapter, and we will not hurry past it. In Israel's courts, if a master struck his servant and the servant died under his hand, the master “shall be surely punished.” A servant's life counted. In the same age, a master's power over those under him was often without limit.

Notwithstanding means nevertheless. “He is his money” is a hard phrase. It says that if the servant lives a day or two, the master is not tried for murder: the servant is his man, and he has already lost his work. It does not say that beating a servant is good. It sets a floor, and men were never meant to stop there.

“Masters, give unto your servants that which is just and equal; knowing that ye also have a Master in heaven.”

Colossians 4:1

The New Testament sets the standard far higher: a servant is a brother (Philemon 15–16), and the master has a Master.

“Eye for eye”

Read the words as a judge would: “life for life, eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot, burning for burning, wound for wound, stripe for stripe.” Stripe is a lash or a bruise. Mischief (verse 23) means harm.

It is a measure, not a command to take revenge. It tells the court: the punishment may fit the harm, and no more. Without a rule like this, a hurt man took more: a life for an eye, a village for a man. “Eye for eye” put a ceiling on revenge and gave the same justice to rich and poor.

The case before it, a woman with child hurt in a fight, shows that the law protected her, and her child: the court looks at the harm done and the guilty man pays. (Teachers differ on the details of verse 22, but the point is plain.)

What Jesus did with it

In the court

Eye for eye

A judge measures out justice, no more than the harm. A limit on revenge.

In your own hand

Turn the other cheek

You do not take your own revenge. You leave it with God.

“Ye have heard that it hath been said, An eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth: But I say unto you, That ye resist not evil: but whosoever shall smite thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also.”

Matthew 5:38–39

Jesus did not cancel the court. He said what a man should do with a wrong done to him: not collect it himself. For men who have been hurt, and those who are angry about it, this is the hardest thing and the freest. Take it to the Lord, and to your brothers in Christ, and put the weapon down.

A servant hurt goes free

Back to the servants. If a master struck out his servant's eye or tooth, the servant went free for it. The same rule for a manservant and a maidservant.

  • Verse 20a servant killed: the master is punished
  • Verse 26an eye lost: the servant goes free
  • Verse 27a tooth lost: the servant goes free

The law says: the servant is a person with his own eyes and his own teeth. A master who maimed him lost his hold on him. God's justice runs down to the weakest man in the house.

Part three

“Make it good”

Verses 28–36

An ox that gores, and a pit left open: who pays when the harm was not planned.

The ox that was known to gore

  • wontused to, in the habit of
  • push with his horngore, butt
  • kept inshut up, fenced
  • quitcleared of blame

If a man's ox killed someone the first time, the ox was stoned, and the owner was not at fault. But if the owner knew his ox had a habit of goring, and had been warned, and did not shut it in, he was guilty too: “his owner also shall be put to death.”

The point: you answer for the danger you knew about and did nothing about. A man who knows his habit, and is warned by those who love him, and keeps doing nothing, is the man this law has in mind.

A ransom for a life

Even for a guilty owner, the law allows a way out: if the family agrees, he may “give for the ransom of his life whatsoever is laid upon him.” A ransom is a price paid to set a life free.

“For even the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many.”

Mark 10:45

A man whose life was forfeit could be set free if a price was laid and paid. That is exactly what Jesus came to do: not a fine, but his own life. “A ransom for many.” Every man whose life was forfeit by his own doing is in the picture.

If the ox killed a servant, the owner paid the master thirty shekels of silver: the price set for a slave's life. Hold on to that number.

Thirty pieces of silver

In the law

30 shekels

The price of a servant killed by an ox. What the law set as the value of a slave.

In Jerusalem

30 pieces

What the chief priests agreed to pay for the Lord Jesus.

“Then one of the twelve, called Judas Iscariot, went unto the chief priests, And said unto them, What will ye give me, and I will deliver him unto you? And they covenanted with him for thirty pieces of silver.”

Matthew 26:14–15

Many teachers notice that this is the same number: the price of a slave. They valued the Son of God at the price of a servant killed by an ox. And he paid the ransom for them, and for us.

The open pit

  • open a pittake the cover off a well or a cistern
  • make it goodpay for the loss: money to the owner, and the dead beast is his
  • ox for oxpay a like for a like

Nobody planned to hurt the ox. But the man who left the hole open owed for the harm. This is the law of carelessness: you are responsible for the damage you leave in other people's way.

If one man's ox hurt another's, they shared the loss. But if the owner knew about his ox and did nothing, he paid in full: “ox for ox.”

What this chapter gives you

  • Sin, honestlyHarm done by temper, by habit, by carelessness: all of it has a bill.
  • Regret“Make it good”: the way out of regret is to put right what you can.
  • GraceBehind the law is the Ransom: “to give his life a ransom for many.”
  • HopeThe Son makes free, and he is a master you may love.
  • TodayThink of one person you have wronged. Tell your pastor, and ask how to make it right.

If you have a debt you can pay, a thing you can give back, or an apology you can make, ask a brother in Christ or your pastor to walk with you. You do not have to carry it alone. And where you cannot pay, remember Who has.

Points to remember

  1. Law for living“Now these are the judgments which thou shalt set before them” 21:1The ten words show God's heart. The judgments show how a nation lives by it, in its courts.
  2. Freedom“six years he shall serve: and in the seventh he shall go out free for nothing” 21:2A man in debt did not stay a servant for ever. He went out free, and could choose to stay for love.
  3. Life“I will appoint thee a place whither he shall flee” 21:13A life is a life. An accident was not the same as murder, and God provided a place to run to.
  4. Justice“Eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot” 21:24It limited revenge in the court. Jesus told us what to do with a wrong done to us: not collect it ourselves.
  5. The weak“he shall let him go free for his eye’s sake” 21:26A servant hurt goes free, a daughter cannot be sold on, a wife must be cared for. God's justice reaches the weak.
  6. Ransom“he shall give for the ransom of his life whatsoever is laid upon him” 21:30A forfeited life could be bought back. Jesus gave his life a ransom for many.

Question 1 of 6

Law for living

Why does God follow the ten commandments with rules about servants, injuries and oxen?

What verses tell us about this?

“Jesus said unto him, Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind. This is the first and great commandment. And the second is like unto it, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself. On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets.”

Matthew 22:37–40

Answer

The ten commandments say what God is like and what he asks. The judgments apply it in the courts of a new nation: how to settle a death, an injury, a debt. They protect the weak and limit revenge.

Question 2 of 6

Freedom

What did God give a Hebrew servant after six years?

What verses tell us about this?

“Jesus answered them, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Whosoever committeth sin is the servant of sin. And the servant abideth not in the house for ever: but the Son abideth ever. If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed.”

John 8:34–36

Answer

Freedom, for nothing, in the seventh year. And if he loved his master and his house, he could choose to stay for ever. Jesus says the Son makes a man free indeed.

Question 3 of 6

Life

What did God say about a man's life, even when it was taken by accident?

What verses tell us about this?

“That by two immutable things, in which it was impossible for God to lie, we might have a strong consolation, who have fled for refuge to lay hold upon the hope set before us:”

Hebrews 6:18

Answer

That a life is a grave thing. A killing by accident was not a killing by plan, and God appointed a place to flee. For the man who plotted it, there was no shelter. We have a refuge to flee to in the Lord.

Question 4 of 6

Justice

What did “eye for eye, tooth for tooth” do, and what did Jesus say about it?

What verses tell us about this?

“Ye have heard that it hath been said, An eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth: But I say unto you, That ye resist not evil: but whosoever shall smite thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also.”

Matthew 5:38–39

Answer

It told the judge: no more than the harm done. It limited revenge and gave the same justice to rich and poor. Jesus did not cancel the court, but he told us not to take our own revenge: leave it with God.

Question 5 of 6

The weak

How did the law protect servants, women and the poor?

What verses tell us about this?

“Masters, give unto your servants that which is just and equal; knowing that ye also have a Master in heaven.”

Colossians 4:1

Answer

A servant who was maimed went free. A girl in a household could not be sold on, and was owed food, clothing and her marriage rights, or she went out free. The weakest person had a claim on the strongest. The New Testament goes further: a servant is a brother.

To a ransom: a price paid to set a forfeited life free. Jesus said he came “to give his life a ransom for many.” The price was not silver but his own life.
