Romans 9

Mercy on His own terms

Paul grieves for his own people, and shows that God has always given mercy His way: not by birth, not by works, but by faith.

Chapter 8 ended on top of the mountain: nothing “shall be able to separate us from the love of God.” Then Paul stops, and his voice drops. A question has been hanging over the whole letter.

God made promises to Israel. Jesus is Israel's own Messiah. Yet most of Paul's own people had turned Him down. So has God's word failed? And if God's promises to Israel can fail, how can we trust His promises to us?

Chapters 9, 10 and 11 answer that question. They're about God's plan for Israel and for the nations. Read them as one argument, because Paul doesn't finish his point until the end of chapter 11.

Part one

Great heaviness

Verses 1–5

Before Paul says a word about choosing, he weeps for his own people.

A broken heart

Paul swears to it three ways: in Christ, by his conscience, and by the Holy Ghost. He isn't putting on a show. Accursed means cut off and handed over to destruction. If it would save them, he'd trade places with them.

These were the people who had hunted him and tried to kill him. Paul, once their star persecutor, was now the man they hated most. He still calls them my brethren, my kinsmen. Moses prayed the same way after the golden calf:

“Yet now, if thou wilt forgive their sin—; and if not, blot me, I pray thee, out of thy book which thou hast written.”

Exodus 32:32

Whatever this chapter means, it was written with tears, by a man who wanted everyone saved. Read it that way.

Everything but Him

  1. The adoption“Israel is my son, even my firstborn” (Exodus 4:22)
  2. The gloryGod's presence in the cloud, the tabernacle and the temple
  3. The covenantswith Abraham, Moses and David
  4. The lawgiven at Sinai
  5. The service of Godthe priests, the sacrifices, the worship
  6. The promisesof a coming King and Saviour
  7. The fathersAbraham, Isaac and Jacob
  8. Christ“who is over all, God blessed for ever”

Israel had every advantage. The Messiah Himself was born a Jew. And Paul says plainly who He is: God blessed for ever. Jesus isn't just a good teacher. He is God.

Here's the warning: you can grow up around all of it, the church, the Bible, the praying grandmother, and still miss Him. Being close to the things of God isn't the same as knowing God.

Part two

The children of the promise

Verses 6–18

God's word hasn't failed. It never promised that being born into the family would be enough.

Not all Israel

Both Ishmael and Isaac were Abraham's sons. But God said, “In Isaac shall thy seed be called.” Isaac came by a promise, born to an old woman who couldn't have children. Ishmael came by Abraham's own plan.

So the family of God was never just a bloodline. It's the children of the promise, those who take God at His word, the way Abraham did (Romans 4). Your parents' faith can't save you. Neither can your church card. Only your own trust in His promise.

Two nations

Here good Bible teachers have wrestled for centuries, and humble men still disagree. Let's go slowly and let the verses Paul quotes tell us what they meant. He quotes two, and both are about peoples.

Genesis 25:23, before the twins were born

And the LORD said unto her, Two nations are in thy womb, and two manner of people shall be separated from thy bowels; and the one people shall be stronger than the other people; and the elder shall serve the younger.

Malachi 1:2–3, about 1,500 years later

Was not Esau Jacob’s brother? saith the LORD: yet I loved Jacob, And I hated Esau, and laid his mountains and his heritage waste for the dragons of the wilderness.

Jacob became the nation of Israel. Esau became the nation of Edom. God chose which nation would carry the promise, the line that would lead to Christ. And He chose before either boy had done anything, so no one could say it was earned: “not of works, but of him that calleth.”

How it played out

  1. about 2000 BCThe twins are born“the elder shall serve the younger”
  2. Genesis 25:34Esau sells his birthrightfor one meal
  3. Genesis 33:3Jacob bows to Esauseven times
  4. about 1000 BCEdom serves David2 Samuel 8:14
  5. about 430 BCMalachi: “I hated Esau”Edom lies waste
  6. about AD 57Paul quotes bothRomans 9:12–13

Notice: Esau the man never served Jacob. Jacob bowed to him. The words came true in the two nations, centuries later. And hated is the Bible's strong way of saying “chose the other, loved less.” Jacob “loved also Rachel more than Leah,” and the next verse says “Leah was hated” (Genesis 29:30–31).

So this isn't God creating one baby for heaven and one for hell. It's God choosing which people would carry His promise to the world, freely, by His own call.

One morsel of meat

Esau wasn't a puppet. Genesis tells what he did with what he had. He came in hungry, and traded his birthright, his whole future as the firstborn, for a bowl of lentil stew.

“Then Jacob gave Esau bread and pottage of lentiles; and he did eat and drink, and rose up, and went his way: thus Esau despised his birthright.”

Genesis 25:34

“Lest there be any fornicator, or profane person, as Esau, who for one morsel of meat sold his birthright.”

Hebrews 12:16

Some of us know that trade. Everything for one more hit, one more drink, one more night. “He did eat and drink, and rose up, and went his way”: like it was nothing. That's the sin. But the God of Romans 9 is the God who shows mercy, and your story isn't over at the bottom of the bowl.

Said after the golden calf

Is God unfair? God forbid: never! Paul answers with Exodus 33:19. God said it to Moses just after Israel had worshipped a golden calf. They deserved to be wiped out. God chose to show mercy instead. These words are God's freedom to forgive people who don't deserve it.

  • not of him that willethyou can't decide your way into it
  • nor of him that runnethyou can't work hard enough to earn it
  • but of God that sheweth mercyit's His gift, on His terms

Nobody can demand mercy. That's what makes it mercy. And on whom does He choose to have it? Paul tells us before he's done: on “whosoever believeth on him” (9:33).

Who hardened Pharaoh's heart?

God told Moses ahead of time, “I will harden his heart” (Exodus 4:21). He knew the man. Then Pharaoh gave his first answer: “Who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice?” (5:2). Now watch the order through the ten plagues.

  1. Blood7:22was hardened
  2. Frogs8:15Pharaoh
  3. Lice8:19was hardened
  4. Flies8:32Pharaoh
  5. Cattle9:7was hardened
  6. Boils9:12the LORD
  7. Hail9:34Pharaoh
  8. Locusts10:20the LORD
  9. Darkness10:27the LORD
  10. Firstborn11:10the LORD

Pharaoh hardened his hearthis heart “was hardened”the LORD hardened

Through the first five plagues, Exodus never says the LORD did it. “When Pharaoh saw that there was respite, he hardened his heart” (8:15). Only at the sixth does it say “the LORD hardened the heart of Pharaoh” (9:12). God gave Pharaoh what Pharaoh kept choosing, and used it to make His name known in all the earth.

Harden not your hearts

Every “no” to God makes the next “no” easier. Pharaoh promised to let the people go every time it hurt, and took it back every time the pressure let up. That's how a heart gets hard: respite, and then back to the old way.

We know that pattern. Promises made in the cell, in the hospital, on the bathroom floor, and broken once things calm down. God doesn't owe anyone another warning. Pharaoh had ten.

“While it is said, To day if ye will hear his voice, harden not your hearts, as in the provocation.”

Hebrews 3:15

If you can still hear Him, your heart isn't stone yet. Answer Him today.

Part three

The potter and the clay

Verses 19–29

Someone talks back to God. Paul answers with a picture every Jew knew from Jeremiah.

At the potter's house

Paul borrows the potter from Jeremiah 18. God sent Jeremiah to watch a potter at his wheel. The pot went wrong in his hands.

The potter didn't throw the clay away. He made it again into another vessel. Then God explained: the clay is a nation, and the potter responds to what the clay does.

The clay that turns

“O house of Israel, cannot I do with you as this potter? saith the LORD. Behold, as the clay is in the potter’s hand, so are ye in mine hand, O house of Israel.”

Jeremiah 18:6

“At what instant I shall speak concerning a nation, and concerning a kingdom, to pluck up, and to pull down, and to destroy it; If that nation, against whom I have pronounced, turn from their evil, I will repent of the evil that I thought to do unto them.”

Jeremiah 18:7–8

God has every right to shape the clay, and nobody gets to put Him on trial: “who art thou that repliest against God?” Paul is rebuking a man who blames God for his own sin, not a man who asks honest questions. But in the very passage Paul quotes, the Potter answers to clay that turns. A marred pot can be made again.

Much longsuffering

Verse 22

Vessels of wrath

“fitted to destruction”: ripe for judgment. And God endured them, with much longsuffering, as He did Pharaoh through ten plagues.

Verses 23–24

Vessels of mercy

“which he had afore prepared unto glory”: “Even us, whom he hath called, not of the Jews only, but also of the Gentiles.”

Why is God patient with men on the road to judgment? Peter tells us:

“The Lord is not slack concerning his promise, as some men count slackness; but is longsuffering to us-ward, not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance.”

2 Peter 3:9

A vessel unto honour

Paul uses the same picture again in his last letter, and there he shows which vessel a man can become:

“But in a great house there are not only vessels of gold and of silver, but also of wood and of earth; and some to honour, and some to dishonour.”

2 Timothy 2:20
Ifa man therefore purge himself from these,

thenhe shall be a vessel unto honour, sanctified, and meet for the master’s use (2:21).

Some of us have been used for the worst things a man can be used for. That's not the end of it. The Master of the house still takes old clay and makes vessels He can use, “prepared unto every good work.”

Not my people, my people

Osee is Hosea. God told Hosea to name his son Lo-ammi, “not my people,” a living sign of Israel's unfaithfulness. Then God promised to change the name.

Lo-ammi → Ammi Hebrew for “not my people”, then “my people” (Hosea 2:1)

“And I will have mercy upon her that had not obtained mercy; and I will say to them which were not my people, Thou art my people; and they shall say, Thou art my God.”

Hosea 2:23

Paul hears in it the Gentiles too, people who were never God's people at all. Think of what you've been called: junkie, felon, failure, “not one of us.” In the place where that was said, God calls a man His own.

As the sand of the sea

Esaias is Isaiah. Israel was as many as the sand, but only a remnant, a small part left over, turned to the Lord. So God's word hasn't failed. A believing remnant was always there, and Paul himself was one of them (11:1, 5).

The Lord of Sabaoth is the Lord of hosts, the Lord of heaven's armies. If He hadn't left a seed, Israel would have ended like Sodoma and Gomorrha, burned without a trace. Anyone left standing is left by mercy.

Part four

Not by faith

Verses 30–33

Paul asks, “What shall we say then?” and gives the reason Israel missed it.

The race nobody expected

The Gentiles

Weren't even running

They “followed not after righteousness,” and yet they attained it: “the righteousness which is of faith.”

Israel

Ran hard, and missed

They “followed after the law of righteousness,” chased it for centuries, and did not attain it.

The pagans got it and the religious people didn't. It's the same upside-down story as the publican and the Pharisee. The men who knew they had nothing walked away with everything.

Wherefore? Because…

Here is Paul's own answer to why Israel missed it. Not because God made them to miss it. Because they sought it not by faith. They tried to earn what can only be received.

Israel→stumbled at→that stumblingstoneA crucified Messiah, a gift you can't earn: they tripped over Him.
Whosoever→believeth on him→shall not be ashamedThe same Stone, trusted, holds a man up for ever.

“Therefore thus saith the Lord GOD, Behold, I lay in Zion for a foundation a stone, a tried stone, a precious corner stone, a sure foundation: he that believeth shall not make haste.”

Isaiah 28:16

Where Paul lands

This chapter is hard, and we should be humble with it. But don't stop reading in the middle of Paul's sentence. Here is where these three chapters land:

“For whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord shall be saved.”

Romans 10:13

“For God hath concluded them all in unbelief, that he might have mercy upon all.”

Romans 11:32

God has the right to show mercy on His own terms. And His terms are faith, open to whosoever. You can't earn it, and you can't be shut out of it if you come to Him.

Question 1 of 6

Heartbreak

What did Paul wish he could do for his people?

What verses tell us about this?

“Brethren, my heart’s desire and prayer to God for Israel is, that they might be saved.”

Romans 10:1

Answer

He wished he could be cut off from Christ himself, if it would bring them to Christ. He couldn't, but he grieved and prayed for them, the same people who had tried to kill him. That's the heart God gives for lost family.

Question 2 of 6

Promise

Who are the true children of Abraham?

What verses tell us about this?

“For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God:”

Ephesians 2:8

Answer

The children of the promise, not just the children of the flesh. Being born in Israel, or raised in church, never made anyone God's child. Those who take God at His word, as Abraham did, are counted as his family.

Question 3 of 6

Jacob and Esau

What was God choosing between Jacob and Esau?

What verses tell us about this?

“And the LORD said unto her, Two nations are in thy womb, and two manner of people shall be separated from thy bowels; and the one people shall be stronger than the other people; and the elder shall serve the younger.”

Genesis 25:23

Answer

Which nation would carry the promise. Jacob's people, Israel, would be the line that led to Christ. God chose before the twins were born, so it was by His call, not their works. It isn't one baby picked for heaven and one for hell.

Question 4 of 6

Pharaoh

Who hardened Pharaoh's heart first?

What verses tell us about this?

“But when Pharaoh saw that there was respite, he hardened his heart, and hearkened not unto them; as the LORD had said.”

Exodus 8:15

“And the LORD hardened the heart of Pharaoh, and he hearkened not unto them; as the LORD had spoken unto Moses.”

Exodus 9:12

Answer

Pharaoh did. God knew it was coming, but Pharaoh hardened his own heart every time the pressure let up. Only then did God confirm what Pharaoh had chosen.

Question 5 of 6

The potter

What do the potter and the clay teach us?

What verses tell us about this?

“And the vessel that he made of clay was marred in the hand of the potter: so he made it again another vessel, as seemed good to the potter to make it.”

Jeremiah 18:4

Answer

God is God, and the clay doesn't put Him on trial. But in Jeremiah the Potter answers clay that turns, and makes marred clay again. A man who turns to Him can become “a vessel unto honour” (2 Timothy 2:21).

Question 6 of 6

Stumbling

Why did Israel miss the righteousness the Gentiles found?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Because they sought it not by faith, but by the works of the law. They tried to earn it, and tripped over Christ, the Stone God laid. The Gentiles weren't even looking, and received it by faith.
