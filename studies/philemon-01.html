Philemon 1

A letter about one man

A runaway slave, the man he wronged, and a prisoner standing in the middle. The shortest letter Paul wrote to one person, and the gospel in miniature.

The letter to Philemon

“Paul, a prisoner of Jesus Christ, and Timothy our brother, unto Philemon our dearly beloved, and fellowlabourer”

Written from
Rome, where Paul was a prisoner in his own rented house, guarded day and night
When
about AD 60 to 62, the same time as Colossians and Ephesians
To whom
Philemon, a believer at Colosse, who hosted a church in his house (verse 2)
Carried by
Onesimus himself, walking back with Tychicus (Colossians 4:7–9)
Why
To ask a master to take back a slave who had run away, and to take him back as a brother.

The road Onesimus took

  1. A slavein Philemon's houseat Colosse
  2. He runsand wrongs his masterprobably to Rome, over a thousand miles
  3. He meets Paula prisoner, and believes“begotten in my bonds” (verse 10)
  4. Sent backwith this letter“Whom I have sent again” (verse 12)
  5. The doorat Philemon's housewe aren't told what happened

We aren't told exactly what Onesimus did. Verse 18 says “if he hath wronged thee, or oweth thee ought,” so he probably took something, or cost his master dearly. In Rome, a runaway slave could be branded, beaten or killed. Philemon was within his rights to do any of it.

We aren't told how he and Paul met, either. Rome was the biggest city in the world, a good place to disappear. Somehow he ended up in front of a prisoner who told him about Jesus.

Where Philemon fits in Paul's life

  1. about AD 53 to 56Paul at Ephesusthe word spreads across Asia
  2. about 57 to 59Held at CaesareaActs 21–26
  3. about 59 to 60Shipwreck, then RomeActs 27–28
  4. about 60 to 62Philemon, from Romewith Colossians and Ephesians
  5. about 62 to 64Paul set free1 Timothy and Titus

“With Onesimus, a faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you. They shall make known unto you all things which are done here.”

Colossians 4:9

Three letters left Rome together in one bag: Ephesians, Colossians, and this one. Tychicus carried the two for the churches. Onesimus carried the third in his own hand, and it was about him. The man who ran away carried, to the man he wronged, the letter that asked for him to be forgiven.

Part one

A prisoner writes to a friend

Verses 1–7

Before Paul asks for anything, he says what he sees in Philemon. Notice how he starts.

Who this letter is for

  1. Paulwriting as “a prisoner,” not “an apostle”
  2. Philemondearly beloved, a fellow worker, and the master
  3. Apphia, Archippusprobably family, and a fellow soldier
  4. The church in thy houseeveryone who meets there

Paul could have started with his title. He starts with his chains: “a prisoner of Jesus Christ.” He asks as a man who has nothing to pull rank with.

And notice he does not write only to Philemon. The letter is addressed to the whole church in his house, so a private matter between two men is going to be read out loud in front of the people who know them both. That is not to shame Philemon. It is the opposite of secrets: this is something the whole family will see him get right.

A man who refreshes people

The bowels of the saints are refreshed by thee.

The old word bowels meant the gut, the place where deep feeling lives, like our heart or gut feeling. Paul is saying: you have been the kind of man who leaves people lifted. In a rough world, that is the best thing anyone could say about a man.

Paul is also getting ready. He has just reminded Philemon what kind of man he is, in front of everyone. He will use it in verse 17. A good word about a man's character is a way of calling him up to it.

Part two

The runaway

Verses 8–16

Now Paul asks. He doesn't give an order, and he doesn't hide what he is asking for.

Asked, not ordered

  • enjoin theeorder it, as an apostle has the right to do
  • for love’s sake I rather beseech theeplead, as a friend, and as “Paul the aged”

Paul had the authority to say do this. He chose to ask. He wants Philemon to make the right choice himself, because a forgiveness that was ordered would not be real forgiveness. (Verse 14 says so.)

Notice he calls himself “Paul the aged” and “a prisoner.” The older man who has been through the fire does not bark. He leans in and asks.

Useless, and now useful

in time pastOnesimusunprofitable
→
nowOnesimusprofitable, truly “useful”

The name Onesimus means useful. Slaves were often given names like that. And Paul is making a pun: a man named Useful, who was useless, is now useful. That is Paul's way of saying: the name finally fits.

“If a man therefore purge himself from these, he shall be a vessel unto honour, sanctified, and meet for the master's use, and prepared unto every good work.”

2 Timothy 2:21

That is what God does with a life that has been wasted. He doesn't get a new man; He makes the one He has useful. Whatever the last years have been, the story isn't over: meet for the master's use.

Born in a prison

Whom I have begotten in my bonds.

Begotten means fathered: Paul had led him to Jesus, and so he calls this runaway “my son.” The new birth happened in a prison, to a man on the run, in the weeks he had probably given up on himself.

If you came to Jesus in a cell, a rehab bed or a bad apartment, you are in good company. There is no place too low for it to start.

Notice mine own bowels in verse 12. Paul says this man is my own heart. He feels about Onesimus the way a father feels about his boy. This is not a trade. It is a love.

A hard thing for Paul too

What Paul wanted

Keep him

Paul was in chains and needed help. Onesimus could have “ministered unto me in the bonds of the gospel.” A willing, useful helper at his side, and every reason to want him.

What Paul did

Send him back

Onesimus belonged to Philemon, and Paul would not keep another man's property without his say-so. “Without thy mind would I do nothing.” It cost Paul something to do right.

Paul sends Onesimus back, but he doesn't send him back to a death sentence. He sends him with a letter that stands between them. Going back is the right thing, and it is also scary. It is the hardest thing in the whole letter, and Paul doesn't pretend it isn't.

A brother, not just a servant

the masterthe slave“a brother beloved … both in the flesh, and in the Lord” (verse 16)
the man wrongedthe man who wronged him“receive him for ever” (verse 15)

“There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither bond nor free, there is neither male nor female: for ye are all one in Christ Jesus.”

Galatians 3:28

Paul doesn't write set him free, but he writes something that makes owning him hard to hold onto: how do you keep a brother in the Lord as your property? Later believers saw what that meant. (And notice “perhaps” in verse 15: God can bring good out of a runaway's road.)

Part three

Put it on my account

Verses 17–25

Paul puts his own name where Onesimus's debt was. It is the clearest picture of the gospel in the whole Bible, in two verses.

The bill, and who signs for it

What Onesimus owes (verse 18)

  • What he took, if he took anythingowed
  • The work he walked away fromowed
  • The trust he brokeowed
  • A master left to wonderowed
Balance duemore than a runaway can pay
PUT THAT ON MINE ACCOUNTPaul

“I Paul have written it with mine own hand, I will repay it.”

Philemon 1:19

Paul doesn't say he didn't really owe it. He says: I'll take it. And he signs it in his own handwriting. A signed promise is worth what the signer is worth.

Two trades at once

Onesimus's sidewelcomed

Paul's sidecharged

The debt“put that on mine account”
Paul's standing“receive him as myself”

Paul takes Onesimus's debt, and Onesimus gets Paul's place: “If thou count me therefore a partner, receive him as myself.” A trade in two directions at once.

The gospel in miniature

“For he hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him.”

2 Corinthians 5:21

Jesus took our sin onto His account, and we are given His standing before the Father. Everything we owed went on Him. Everything He is, is counted to us. The old word is imputed: put to someone's account.

“Blotting out the handwriting of ordinances that was against us, which was contrary to us, and took it out of the way, nailing it to his cross;”

Colossians 2:14

If you have a pile of what you owe, to God and to people, here is the gospel in one line: put that on mine account. He has already said it to you.

Going back to make it right

Onesimus's road has a low point, and a climb. The climb is not only believing. It is walking over a thousand miles to knock on the door of the man he wronged.

Many men here know that walk: the phone call to make, the money owed, the person hurt. It is one of the hardest things a man will ever do. But Paul does not send Onesimus alone. He sends a letter, a friend, and a promise to pay.

“Therefore if thou bring thy gift to the altar, and there rememberest that thy brother hath ought against thee; Leave there thy gift before the altar, and go thy way; first be reconciled to thy brother, and then come and offer thy gift.”

Matthew 5:23-24

Make it right, with a friend beside you

  1. Admit itto God first: verse 18 starts with “If he hath wronged thee”
  2. Take a friendPaul wrote, and stood beside him
  3. Offer to repaysay what you'll do, and then do it
  4. Let him choose“willingly,” not of necessity (verse 14)

Paul writes “Having confidence in thy obedience I wrote unto thee, knowing that thou wilt also do more than I say” (verse 21). He trusts Philemon to do the right thing. And in verse 22 he says he is coming to see for himself: “prepare me also a lodging.” There will be a face-to-face.

If you are carrying something you owe, pick one name. Tell someone you trust. Make a plan for how and when. Then go. (Not every amend should be made face to face, and some people are not safe to go back to, so ask a pastor or an older believer first.)

Paul's circle, and a second chance

The letter ends with the men around Paul: Epaphras (who started the church at Colosse), Marcus, Aristarchus, Demas and Lucas: Mark and Luke, writers of two Gospels.

“And Barnabas determined to take with them John, whose surname was Mark. But Paul thought not good to take him with them, who departed from them from Pamphylia, and went not with them to the work.”

Acts 15:37-38

Mark had once quit on Paul in the middle of the work. Now there he is on the list, a fellow labourer.

“Only Luke is with me. Take Mark, and bring him with thee: for he is profitable to me for the ministry.”

2 Timothy 4:11

Years later, Paul asks for him: “profitable to me for the ministry.” The same word he used for Onesimus. A man who failed is called useful.

A look back over Philemon

  1. The wrongHe ran, and owedverse 18
  2. The new birth“begotten in my bonds”verse 10
  3. The new nameuseless → usefulverse 11
  4. The hard road“sent again”verse 12
  5. The debt“on mine account”verses 18–19
  6. The welcome“a brother beloved”verses 16–17

We aren't told how Philemon answered. But the letter was kept and copied, and it is in your Bible. A letter that had gone badly would hardly have been saved. Some early writers say an Onesimus later led the church at Ephesus. We can't be sure it's the same man.

Whatever your own story is, it has these same pieces: a wrong, a Saviour who found you, a debt put on His account, and a road back where you are welcomed. Go and live today as a man who is no longer useless.

Points to remember

  1. Debt“If he hath wronged thee, or oweth thee ought, put that on mine account” 1:18Paul took the debt and signed for it himself. Jesus took ours.
  2. Changed“Which in time past was to thee unprofitable, but now profitable to thee and to me” 1:11A useless man made useful. God doesn't discard a wasted life; He makes it meet for the master's use.
  3. Brother“Not now as a servant, but above a servant, a brother beloved” 1:16In Christ, the man who wronged you, and the man you wronged, are brothers.
  4. Love“Yet for love’s sake I rather beseech thee” 1:9Paul asked rather than ordered. Real forgiveness has to be chosen.
  5. Making it right“Whom I have sent again” 1:12Onesimus went back. Making amends is hard, and he did it with a letter, a friend and a promise to pay.
  6. The picture“If thou count me therefore a partner, receive him as myself” 1:17Paul took the debt and gave his standing. That is the gospel in miniature.

Question 1 of 6

Debt

What did Onesimus owe, and who offered to pay?

What verses tell us about this?

“Blotting out the handwriting of ordinances that was against us, which was contrary to us, and took it out of the way, nailing it to his cross;”

Colossians 2:14

Answer

He had wronged his master and ran, and probably owed money and years of service besides. More than he could pay. Paul offered to take it all on his own account and wrote it down in his own hand. Jesus did the same for us, nailing the bill to His cross.

Question 2 of 6

Changed

How did God change a man whose name means “useful” but who was useless?

What verses tell us about this?

“If a man therefore purge himself from these, he shall be a vessel unto honour, sanctified, and meet for the master's use, and prepared unto every good work.”

2 Timothy 2:21

Answer

He met Paul in prison and was born again. The man who had been unprofitable became profitable, useful to Paul and to Philemon. God didn't give Onesimus a different life. He took the one he had and made it useful. He can do the same with a life that has been wasted.

Question 3 of 6

Brother

How should Philemon receive him?

What verses tell us about this?

“There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither bond nor free, there is neither male nor female: for ye are all one in Christ Jesus.”

Galatians 3:28

Answer

Not as a runaway slave, but as a brother beloved, and as Paul himself. Philemon was within his rights to punish, but Paul asks him to welcome. In Christ, the man who wronged you and the man he wronged are one family.

Question 4 of 6

Love

Why did Paul ask instead of giving an order?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Because a good deed that is forced is not a good deed. Paul could have used his authority, but he wanted Philemon to choose it freely, out of love. Forgiveness can be asked for and prayed for, but it cannot be ordered.

Question 5 of 6

Making it right

What did Onesimus have to do after he met Christ?

What verses tell us about this?

“Therefore if thou bring thy gift to the altar, and there rememberest that thy brother hath ought against thee; Leave there thy gift before the altar, and go thy way; first be reconciled to thy brother, and then come and offer thy gift.”

Matthew 5:23-24

Answer

Go back to the man he had wronged, with Paul's letter. Believing in Christ didn't cancel the need to face Philemon; it gave him the courage to do it. Making amends is hard. He didn't go alone, and he didn't go empty-handed: he had a letter, a friend and a promise to pay.

Question 6 of 6

The picture

How is this little letter a picture of what Jesus did for us?

What verses tell us about this?

“For he hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him.”

2 Corinthians 5:21

Answer

Onesimus owed a debt he could not pay. Paul put it on his own account, and gave Onesimus his own place: receive him as myself. Jesus took our sin and gives us His standing before the Father. Everything we owed went on Him, and everything He is, is counted to us.
