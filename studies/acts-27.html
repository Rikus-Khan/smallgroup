Acts 27

The storm

Paul sails for Rome as a prisoner. A warning goes unheard, a storm takes everything but hope, and God keeps a promise to the last man aboard.

Part one

A warning nobody wanted

Verses 1–12

A prisoner, a centurion, a late-season ship, and a harbor that was no good for winter.

A prisoner at sea

After two years in a cell at Caesarea, Paul is bound for Rome with a centurion named Julius. The “we” is Luke, writing as he goes.

At Sidon, Julius “courteously entreated Paul” and let him visit friends. Remember that kindness. It comes back.

Contrary winds push them “under Cyprus.” At Myra they change to a big grain ship out of Egypt, bound for Rome.

Then “sailed slowly many days,” curling around Crete to Fair Havens, a small bay on its south coast.

Late in the sailing year

“The fast” is the Day of Atonement, the Jewish fast that falls about the start of October. Luke uses it as a calendar: the safe season is over. Sailors of that day counted mid-September to mid-November as risky, and the winter as closed.

Paul was no landlubber. He'd already written, “thrice I suffered shipwreck, a night and a day I have been in the deep” (2 Corinthians 11:25). Three wrecks before this one. He knew what he was talking about.

Who do you believe?

Paul, a prisoner“Sirs, I perceive that this voyage will be with hurt and much damage… of our lives.”

The master and the owner, and most of the crowd“The haven was not commodious to winter in.”

Nobody here is a villain. The harbor really was poor for winter, the owner had money on the line, the pilot was the expert, and the man with the warning was a prisoner. He was right.

Then “the south wind blew softly” (v. 13), and they felt sure. A bad decision feels great for the first hour. If you've ever waved off the guy telling you the truth, you know how this goes.

Part two

Fourteen days with no sun

Verses 13–26

A wind off the mountains, a ship driven blind, and a word from God in the night.

Caught, and driven

Euroclydon is a northeaster, Greek for east wind plus billows. It tore down off Crete's mountains, and the ship “could not bear up into the wind.” “We let her drive.”

Behind little Clauda they hauled up the lifeboat and bound cables around the hull, “undergirding the ship.”

Their fear was the “quicksands,” the Syrtis shoals off Libya. So they slowed down and let the wind take them across the Adria, about 475 miles.

Over the side

Day two, they “lightened the ship,” and a fortune in grain and freight went over.

Day three, “with our own hands,” they threw out the ship's own tackling. Their lives were worth more than their gear.

Maybe you've been here. The thing you swore you needed (a phone number, the old crowd, the bottle in the glove box) has to go over the side or it takes you down. And notice whose hands did it: our own. It hurts. It's how you stay afloat.

No sun, no stars

Sailors steered by the sun by day and the stars by night. There was no compass. With both hidden, they didn't know where they were.

The storm took the cargo, then the gear, then the last thing: “all hope.” Luke writes “we.” He's in that sentence too.

If you've had a night like that, with no sign of morning, you're in good company. The Bible doesn't skip the hopeless part. It doesn't stop there either.

What the storm could take

It could take

The ship

The cargo, the gear, the whole vessel. “Ye should have hearkened unto me… and to have gained this harm and loss.” Paul says it once, plainly, and moves on.

It could not take

A single life

“There shall be no loss of any man's life among you.” Every one of the people who ignored the warning is still included.

Notice how Paul handles their mistake. He names it, because they need to know why they're here, and then he turns straight to hope: “And now I exhort you to be of good cheer.” He doesn't make them live in it.

That's a good way to treat your own regret too. Say what happened, honestly. Then ask what God says to do next.

Whose I am

“Whose I am, and whom I serve.”

A prisoner of Rome, on a doomed ship, in the worst night of his life, and Paul knows who owns him. Not the emperor, not the storm. Whose I am: I belong to God. Whom I serve: He's who I work for.

God had told him already, months before, in a Jerusalem cell:

“And the night following the Lord stood by him, and said, Be of good cheer, Paul: for as thou hast testified of me in Jerusalem, so must thou bear witness also at Rome.”

Acts 23:11

And notice what the angel does not say: no wreck. “We must be cast upon a certain island.” God promised Paul he'd get through, not that it would be smooth. “I believe God” means taking Him at His word before anything changes.

Part three

The fourteenth night

Verses 27–44

The sound of surf in the dark, four anchors, a loaf of bread, and a beach.

Four anchors

Midnight, fourteen days in. They sense land and “sounded” with a lead weight on a line.

Twenty fathoms: 120 feet of water (a fathom is six feet).

Then fifteen: 90 feet. The bottom is rising, and they can't see the rocks.

So “four anchors out of the stern.” They couldn't steer in the dark. All they could do was hold still.

And “wished for the day.” Just men waiting for light. It came.

What are your anchors today?

An anchor that holds

Sure and stedfast.

“Which hope we have as an anchor of the soul, both sure and stedfast, and which entereth into that within the veil;”

Hebrews 6:19

Those four anchors held because they caught on something solid. Hope in Jesus is like that. It holds you not because you're gripping it hard enough, but because it's fastened to Him, “within the veil,” in God's own presence. You can drag and slip and still be held.

Today's real-world anchors: open God's Word for five minutes, text one brother, eat something, and say one honest prayer. Put them down before the night, not after.

Stay in the ship

Some sailors decide to save themselves. They lower the boat “under colour,” a cover story about anchors, ready to slip away and leave 270 people behind.

Paul tells the centurion, “Except these abide in the ship, ye cannot be saved.” They'd need those sailors to steer her in at daylight.

The soldiers cut the ropes, and the boat falls away.

Every man knows the lifeboat: skip the meeting, ignore the guys' calls, “I've got this on my own.” Stay in the ship.

Bread in the storm

souls on the ship, one dot each

Fourteen days of fear, and nobody has eaten properly. Then Paul, a prisoner among 275 strangers, takes bread and “gave thanks to God in presence of them all.” No sermon. Just thanks, out loud, and a man eating.

This isn't the Lord's Supper. It's a plain meal for men about to swim for their lives. But it's what a man who walks with Jesus does: thanks first.

“For this is for your health.” Hungry, angry, lonely, tired: the guys call it HALT, and each one makes a hard day harder. Eat something. Sleep when you can. Looking after your body is part of looking after the rest.

Aground

At daylight they spot a beach, cut the anchors loose, put up a sail and run for it. But “a place where two seas met,” a sandbar with water on both sides, catches her.

The bow digs in and won't budge. The stern comes apart in the surf. By tradition this was St. Paul's Bay on Malta.

God said no loss of life, “but of the ship.” He kept every word, including the hard one.

All safe to land

reached land, out of 276

The soldiers wanted to kill the prisoners so none could escape (a guard paid with his own life for a lost prisoner, as the jailor in Acts 16 knew). Julius, who'd let Paul visit friends at Sidon, stopped them. A small kindness, and it came back.

Look who's on that beach: the men who ignored the warning, the sailors who tried to run, the soldiers who wanted blood. None earned it. “God hath given thee all them that sail with thee.” That's grace: not because they listened, but because God had said so.

Paul never got the ship back. He got every man. Maybe you're standing on a beach you never expected.

Points to remember

  1. Good counsel“the centurion believed the master and the owner of the ship, more than those things which were spoken by Paul” 27:11A soft wind makes a bad decision feel right. Listen to the one who's been through it.
  2. Despair“all hope that we should be saved was then taken away” 27:20There are nights with no sun and no stars. They're not the end of the story.
  3. God's promise“there shall be no loss of any man’s life among you” 27:22God didn't promise a smooth trip. He promised to bring them through, and He did.
  4. Staying together“Except these abide in the ship, ye cannot be saved” 27:31Don't slip away in the lifeboat. Stay with the people God put around you.
  5. Taking care“I pray you to take some meat: for this is for your health” 27:34Eat, sleep, and give thanks. Looking after the body is part of looking after your soul.
  6. Rescue“they escaped all safe to land” 27:44The ship was lost, and every life was saved. By grace, none of them was left out.

Question 1 of 6

Good counsel

Why did the ship sail on when Paul warned them not to?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

The centurion believed the pilot and the owner, not the prisoner. The harbor was poor for winter, most wanted to go, and a soft south wind made it look right. The warning came from the one man with no authority, and it was true.

Question 2 of 6

Despair

What did the men lose besides the cargo?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Hope. First the cargo went overboard, then the ship's gear, and after many days with no sun or stars to steer by, “all hope that we should be saved was then taken away.” The Bible doesn't hide the hopeless part. But it doesn't stop there.

Question 3 of 6

God's promise

What did God promise Paul in the night?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

“Fear not, Paul.” He would stand before Caesar, and God had given him every person on the ship. There would be no loss of life, only the ship. It wasn't a promise of a smooth trip, but it was a promise of coming through. Paul believed God, and it happened just as he was told.

Question 4 of 6

Staying together

Who had to stay in the ship, and why?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

The sailors. They were about to slip away in the lifeboat and leave everyone else. Paul said they had to stay aboard for the rest to be saved, because God's promise would come through the people He had put on that ship. Nobody gets through the night alone.

Question 5 of 6

Taking care

Why did Paul tell hungry, frightened men to eat?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

They'd gone fourteen days without a real meal and were about to swim for their lives. “For this is for your health.” Paul gave thanks to God in front of everyone, broke the bread, and ate, and they all took heart. God cares for the body as well as the soul.

Question 6 of 6

Rescue

Who made it safe to land?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

All 276 of them: the men who ignored the warning, the sailors who tried to run, and the soldiers who wanted to kill the prisoners. God had given Paul “all them that sail with thee.” The ship was lost, but not one life. They didn't earn it. God had said so.
