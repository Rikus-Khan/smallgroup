Mark 15

The King on a cross

Pilate gives up an innocent man to please a crowd. Barabbas walks free. Jesus is crucified, darkness covers the land, the temple veil tears in two, and He is laid in a rock tomb.

Part one

Before Pilate

Verses 1–15

The council hands Jesus to Rome. A murderer goes free, and the innocent One is sent to die.

One long Friday

The council had tried Jesus through the night (14:53–65). At daybreak they take Him to the Roman governor, Pontius Pilate, who ruled Judaea for Rome from about A.D. 26 to 36. Under Rome, the council had no power to put a man to death (John 18:31). Mark marks the hours of the day:

Morningto Pilate (v. 1)
3rd hourabout 9 a.m., crucified (v. 25)
6th hournoon, darkness (v. 33)
9th hourabout 3 p.m., He dies (v. 37)
Evenburied before the sabbath (v. 42)

Pilate asks one question: “Art thou the King of the Jews?” A rival king meant rebellion against Caesar. Jesus answers, “Thou sayest it”: yes, it is as you say. Everything that follows today happens to the King.

The silence of the Lamb

The chief priests“…accused him of many things”

PilateAnswerest thou nothing? behold how many things they witness against thee.

Jesus…
answered nothing

Pilate had seen plenty of prisoners shout, beg, and argue. He had never seen this, and he “marvelled.”

As a lamb

Isaiah saw this silence about seven hundred years before it happened:

“He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth: he is brought as a lamb to the slaughter, and as a sheep before her shearers is dumb, so he openeth not his mouth.”

Isaiah 53:7

He wasn't beaten, and He wasn't trapped. He had come to die for these very men, and He wasn't going to talk His way out of it.

Most of us learned to fight for ourselves with our mouths: arguing, excusing, blaming. Jesus could have won any argument in that hall. He chose to lose it, for us.

“For envy”

Pilate saw straight through it. The chief priests didn't hand Jesus over for blasphemy or for Rome's sake. They did it because the people were following Him and not them.

Envy is hating somebody for having what you want. It doesn't stay small. Here it ends with men who served at God's altar “moving” a crowd to ask for a murderer instead of the Son of God.

“A sound heart is the life of the flesh: but envy the rottenness of the bones.”

Proverbs 14:30

Most of us know that rot: the brother who's doing well, the man who got the job, the friend who stayed clean when we didn't. Name it before God. He can heal what envy eats.

To content the people

Pilate knew the truth. He said it out loud: “what evil hath he done?” But the crowd only shouted louder, and keeping them happy weighed more with him than doing right.

“For do I now persuade men, or God? or do I seek to please men? for if I yet pleased men, I should not be the servant of Christ.”

Galatians 1:10

Going along to get along has put a lot of us back where we swore we'd never go. The crowd will always be loud. You don't have to obey it.

The guilty man walks out

Barabbas was a rebel against Rome and a killer, locked up and waiting for a cross of his own. That morning he heard the crowd outside shouting his name.

To the crosscondemned

Out the doorreleased

Barabbasinsurrection and murder (v. 7)
Jesus“what evil hath he done?” (v. 14)

Then they trade places. The cross that was made for Barabbas gets Jesus instead.

“For he hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him.”

2 Corinthians 5:21

That's the gospel in one morning. We're Barabbas: guilty, and set free because Jesus took our place.

Part two

The place of a skull

Verses 16–32

Mocked as a king, crucified between two thieves, and dared to come down.

A joke that was true

The Praetorium was the governor's headquarters. “The whole band” was a Roman cohort, several hundred soldiers, called together for the fun. Every piece of their joke said more than they knew:

What they did to mock Him

A play king

A purple cloak, a crown of thorns, a reed for a sceptre, “Hail, King of the Jews!”, and knees bent in scorn.

What is true

The King

“That at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, of things in heaven, and things in earth, and things under the earth; And that every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father.” (Philippians 2:10–11)

He let them do it. Every man who knelt in that hall to mock Him will one day kneel for real. The only question for any of us is whether we bow to Him now, by choice, or later.

A stranger made to carry it

Cyrene was a city on the coast of North Africa, in Libya, with a large Jewish community. Simon may have come all that way for the Passover. He was just coming in from the country when the soldiers grabbed him.

The cross beam was laid on a stranger, and Simon walked the rest of the way to Golgotha behind Jesus.

Behind Jesus, carrying a cross

Mark names Simon's sons, Alexander and Rufus, as if his readers knew them. Writing to the church at Rome, Paul says:

“Salute Rufus chosen in the Lord, and his mother and mine.”

Romans 16:13

It may be the same family. A man forced to carry the cross for an hour may have raised a family that followed Jesus for life. Simon didn't choose that day. But what he carried, and Who he walked behind, changed everything after it.

“Whosoever will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow me.”

Mark 8:34

He received it not

Wine mixed with myrrh was a drug, offered to dull the pain of a man about to be nailed up. Jesus tasted it and turned it down (Matthew 27:34).

He meant to bear the whole of it, awake and clear-headed, for us. He didn't numb Himself to get through the worst day any man has ever had.

Many of us have reached for something to kill the pain. Jesus knows what that pain is from the inside. He isn't standing over you in judgment. He went through it with nothing in His hand, and He can carry you through yours.

“For in that he himself hath suffered being tempted, he is able to succour them that are tempted.”

Hebrews 2:18

On His right hand and on His left

Mark doesn't dwell on the pain. He says it in four words: “they crucified him.” Crucifixion was Rome's most shameful death, kept for slaves, rebels, and the worst of criminals. Over His head hung the charge: THE KING OF THE JEWS.

Mark 10:37, James and John, on the road

“Grant unto us that we may sit, one on thy right hand, and the other on thy left hand, in thy glory.”

Mark 15:27, at the cross

“And with him they crucify two thieves; the one on his right hand, and the other on his left.”

Jesus told them then, “Ye know not what ye ask” (10:38). This is the throne He was headed for. And Isaiah had said He would be “numbered with the transgressors” (Isaiah 53:12): counted in with the criminals, because He came for criminals.

Why He didn't come down

Everybody mocks: the people passing by, the chief priests, even the thieves. And their dare is the same: “come down from the cross.”

If He had saved HimselfHe comes down
→
thenno one is saved

The priests meant it as an insult, but they told the truth: He couldn't save Himself and save others. He could have come down. The nails weren't what held Him there. Love was.

“For even the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many.”

Mark 10:45

Part three

Darkness at noon

Verses 33–41

Three hours of darkness, a cry, a torn curtain, and a Roman soldier who sees who Jesus is.

The sun goes out at midday

From noon until three, darkness lay over “the whole land.” It couldn't have been an eclipse: Passover always fell at the full moon, when the moon is on the far side of the earth from the sun.

In the Bible, darkness at noon is a sign of God's judgment (Amos 8:9). For three hours the judgment our sin deserved was falling, and it fell on Jesus.

Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani? ay-LOH-ee, ay-LOH-ee, LAH-mah sah-BAHK-thah-nee “My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?”

Mark keeps the very words, in Aramaic, the everyday language Jesus spoke. Some bystanders heard “Eloi” and thought He was calling for Elias, Elijah. He was praying the first line of Psalm 22:

“My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? why art thou so far from helping me, and from the words of my roaring?”

Psalm 22:1

Forsaken in our place

We can't fully understand what passed between the Father and the Son in that darkness. But Scripture tells us why: He was carrying our sin, and God is “of purer eyes than to behold evil” (Habakkuk 1:13). The Son was forsaken in our place. Even then, He still said “My God.”

“Let your conversation be without covetousness; and be content with such things as ye have: for he hath said, I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee.”

Hebrews 13:5

He was forsaken so that no one who trusts Him ever will be.

Psalm 22 at the foot of the cross

David wrote Psalm 22 about a thousand years earlier, long before anyone was crucified. Watch it come true in Mark 15:

  1. ✓They part my garments among them, and cast lots upon my vesture.Psalm 22:1815:24
  2. ✓All they that see me laugh me to scorn: they shoot out the lip, they shake the head, saying,Psalm 22:715:29
  3. ✓My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?Psalm 22:115:34
  4. ✓in my thirst they gave me vinegar to drinkPsalm 69:2115:36

Gamblers, mockers, a sponge on a reed: none of them were trying to fulfil anything. The cross wasn't a plan that went wrong. It was God's plan, written down ahead of time.

Torn from the top

A great curtain, the veil, hung in the temple to “divide unto you between the holy place and the most holy” (Exodus 26:33). Only the high priest went behind it, once a year, with blood.

The moment Jesus died, it tore in two, “from the top to the bottom.” No man tore it. God opened it from above.

“Having therefore, brethren, boldness to enter into the holiest by the blood of Jesus, By a new and living way, which he hath consecrated for us, through the veil, that is to say, his flesh;”

Hebrews 10:19–20

The first line and the last word

A centurion commanded about a hundred Roman soldiers. This one had run the execution. He'd seen men die before, but never like this.

Mark 1:1, the first line of the book

“The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God;”

Mark 15:39, at the cross

“Truly this man was the Son of God.”

Mark opened his book by telling us who Jesus is. The first man in the book to say it out loud, as Jesus died, wasn't a priest or a disciple. It was a Gentile soldier with blood on his hands. Nobody is too far outside to see it.

The ones who stayed

The night before, Mark had written of the twelve: “And they all forsook him, and fled” (14:50). But some never left:

Mary Magdaleneset free from seven devils (Luke 8:2)
Marythe mother of James the less and of Joses
Salomeprobably the mother of James and John (Matthew 27:56)

They had followed Him from Galilee and “ministered unto him”: they served Him and helped pay His way. They couldn't stop what was happening. They stayed anyway. Sometimes being faithful just means showing up and not leaving.

Part four

Laid in a tomb

Verses 42–47

A council member steps out of hiding, and a stone is rolled across the door.

Really dead, really buried

The sabbath began at sundown. Joseph of Arimathaea, an “honourable counsellor,” sat on the very council that condemned Jesus. Asking for the body tied him to Jesus in front of everyone. He went anyway, “boldly.”

  1. ✓he asked him whether he had been any while deadPilate checks with the soldier who ran the execution15:44
  2. ✓he gave the body to JosephRome signs off: the man is dead15:45
  3. ✓wrapped him in the linen, and laid him in a sepulchreburied by a member of the council15:46
  4. ✓beheld where he was laidthe women saw the very tomb15:47

He really died, and He was really buried. That matters, because of what happens next.

A stone at the door

The tomb was a room cut into the rock. A heavy round stone ran in a groove across the door. Joseph rolled it shut, and that was that, as far as anyone could see.

Two women watched where He was laid. They would be back, and they would know they had the right tomb.

Mark 15 ends in the dark, with a dead man, a stone across the door, and a lot of grieving people. If that's where your life is today, hold on. Friday is not the end of this story.

Question 1 of 6

Silent

Why did Jesus answer nothing to His accusers?

What verses tell us about this?

“He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth: he is brought as a lamb to the slaughter, and as a sheep before her shearers is dumb, so he openeth not his mouth.”

Isaiah 53:7

“Who, when he was reviled, reviled not again; when he suffered, he threatened not; but committed himself to him that judgeth righteously:”

1 Peter 2:23

Answer

Because He was the Lamb going willingly to the slaughter, just as Isaiah said. He wasn't trapped or beaten. He had come to die for sinners, so He didn't defend Himself or fight back. He left His case with the Father, who judges rightly.

Question 2 of 6

Exchanged

Who walked free because Jesus was condemned?

What verses tell us about this?

“For he hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him.”

2 Corinthians 5:21

Answer

Barabbas, a rebel and a murderer. He walked out, and Jesus took the cross meant for him. And so does every sinner who trusts Christ: Jesus took our sin and our sentence, and we go free.

Question 3 of 6

The crowd

What made Pilate give up a man he knew was innocent?

What verses tell us about this?

“For do I now persuade men, or God? or do I seek to please men? for if I yet pleased men, I should not be the servant of Christ.”

Galatians 1:10

Answer

Wanting to please the crowd. Pilate knew the priests acted out of envy, and he said himself that Jesus had done no evil. But he was “willing to content the people,” and he traded the truth for their approval. A man who lives to please people can't serve Christ.

Question 4 of 6

He stayed

Why didn't Jesus come down from the cross?

What verses tell us about this?

“For even the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many.”

Mark 10:45

“Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness: by whose stripes ye were healed.”

1 Peter 2:24

Answer

Because He came to give His life as a ransom. He could have come down, but then He couldn't have saved anyone. He stayed and carried our sins in His own body on the tree. The mockers were right without meaning to be: to save others, He would not save Himself.

Question 5 of 6

Forsaken

Why did Jesus cry, “My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?”

What verses tell us about this?

“For he hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him.”

2 Corinthians 5:21

“Let your conversation be without covetousness; and be content with such things as ye have: for he hath said, I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee.”

Hebrews 13:5

Answer

Because on the cross He was carrying our sin, and He took the full weight of being cut off from God that our sin deserved. He was praying Psalm 22, still calling Him “My God.” He was forsaken in our place, so God can promise every believer, “I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee.”

Question 6 of 6

The way in

What did the torn veil of the temple mean?

What verses tell us about this?

“Having therefore, brethren, boldness to enter into the holiest by the blood of Jesus, By a new and living way, which he hath consecrated for us, through the veil, that is to say, his flesh;”

Hebrews 10:19–20

“For whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord shall be saved.”

Romans 10:13

Answer

The way into God's presence is open. For centuries the veil said “keep out”: only one man, once a year. When Jesus died, God tore it from the top down. Now anyone who comes by the blood of Jesus can come to God boldly, any day, whoever he is and whatever he has done.
