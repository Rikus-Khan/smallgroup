Genesis 24

“I being in the way, the LORD led me”

Abraham sends his oldest servant a long way to find a wife for Isaac. The servant prays, and before he finishes praying, the answer walks up to the well with a water jar on her shoulder.

Part one

The oath

Verses 1–9

An old man, with one thing left to do, makes his oldest servant promise.

Three years after Sarah

  1. Genesis 21Isaac is bornAbraham is 100
  2. 3 years beforeSarah diesGenesis 23; Isaac is 37
  3. nowThis chapterIsaac is 40 (25:20); Abraham is about 140
  4. 20 years laterThe twins are bornIsaac is 60 (25:26)
  5. 35 years laterAbraham dieshe was 175 (25:7)

Sarah is buried, and Isaac is forty and still single. Abraham is old, “well stricken in age”, which means far along in years. He has one job left, and it is the biggest one: where will the promise go next? The promise needs a family, and a family needs a wife.

Notice how the chapter opens: “the LORD had blessed Abraham in all things.” Hold that next to chapter 12, where he left Haran with nothing but a word. God kept every promise he made on that road.

“Put thy hand under my thigh”

The oldest servant of the house was the man who ran everything Abraham owned. The chapter never gives his name. (An earlier servant of Abraham's, Eliezer, is named in 15:2, but Genesis does not say it is the same man, so we leave him unnamed too.)

The oath was a very solemn custom of the time. The thigh is where a man's children were thought to come from, which is why Genesis 46:26 calls a man's descendants those who “came out of his loins”. An oath taken there was sworn on the family line itself. Jacob asks the same of Joseph at the end of his life:

“…and he called his son Joseph, and said unto him, If now I have found grace in thy sight, put, I pray thee, thy hand under my thigh, and deal kindly and truly with me; bury me not, I pray thee, in Egypt:”

Genesis 47:29

Today we don't swear that way, but the point stands: this was a promise a man could not take back, made to a dying man about the thing he cared about most.

Why not a Canaanite wife?

It isn't about skin or race. The Canaanites around Abraham worshipped other gods, and Genesis has already shown what that land was like (Sodom, chapters 18 and 19). The question was who would stand beside Isaac and who would raise his sons.

Abraham starts his oath with who God is: “the LORD, the God of heaven, and the God of the earth”. A man who knows that God does not hand his son's whole future to whoever happens to live next door.

“Be ye not unequally yoked together with unbelievers: for what fellowship hath righteousness with unrighteousness? and what communion hath light with darkness?”

2 Corinthians 6:14

For a man in recovery, this one is close to home. Who you marry, who you work beside, and who you drink coffee with all pull on you. Abraham didn't leave it to chance. Neither should you.

“Bring not my son thither again”

The servant asks a sensible question. Peradventure means perhaps: what if the woman won't come so far? Should Isaac go back to Haran and live there instead?

Abraham's answer is quick and plain: No. “Beware.” Isaac does not go back. Abraham left that country at God's word, and he will not have his son undo it.

“And truly, if they had been mindful of that country from whence they came out, they might have had opportunity to have returned. But now they desire a better country, that is, an heavenly: wherefore God is not ashamed to be called their God: for he hath prepared for them a city.”

Hebrews 11:15-16

If you have left an old life, you know the pull of the old country: the old places, the old crowd, the old habits that were so familiar. Abraham's answer is the one to borrow: don't take your son back, and don't go back yourself.

Abraham says what God had promised him: this land for his seed. He is sure God will lead the servant, and he says so out loud. Yet he leaves the woman free. The servant cannot drag a bride home; her “yes” has to be her own.

He is also kind to his servant. A man who cannot keep an oath because of someone else's “no” is not to be crushed by guilt over it. Remember that when you face something that is not wholly in your own hands. Do your part. Leave the rest with God.

Part two

At the well

Verses 10–27

A long road, a short prayer, and an answer that doesn't wait.

A very long way

The servant took ten camels and went to “Mesopotamia”, which means between the rivers: the land of the Euphrates. The “city of Nahor” is where Abraham's brother Nahor had settled, near Haran, the place Abraham left at seventy-five (12:4).

From Abraham's camp in the south of Canaan, that is well over four hundred miles in a straight line, and much more by the roads. On camels it was a journey of weeks. He did not know whether it would work.

Why the well?

Wells were outside the town, and the women of a town came out at evening, when it was cool, to draw water. In a world with no taps, the well was where a stranger went first to learn what a town was like.

The servant parked his camels by the well and waited at “the time that women go out to draw water”. He had chosen the one place in town where every young woman would eventually come.

You will see this again. Wells and water are where Genesis brings its people together: Jacob will meet Rachel at one (29:2), and Isaac at the end of this chapter lives beside the well where an outcast woman once met God.

“Shew kindness”

חֶסֶד said chesed “faithful, loyal love”: kindness that keeps its word

The servant prays as a servant. He says my master's God, and he asks for faithful kindness, for his master's sake and not for his own. He is not asking God for a good day. He is asking God to keep his promise to Abraham.

Notice what he asks. “Good speed” is an old phrase for success; “shew” is the old word for show. He asks that the right girl be shown to him by what she does, not by how she looks.

Is this how we ask for guidance?

Here is a careful answer. The servant's request was no coin toss. It was a request that a certain kind of person would turn up: a woman who gives water to a stranger and offers it to ten camels. It asked for character.

He was also already doing the right thing, going where Abraham had told him to go (verse 4), and asking inside what God had already said. God had promised Abraham a seed. The servant asked God to finish what he had begun.

So don't treat this as a trick that makes God answer on command. We have God's written Word, which the servant did not. Start there. Then ask, as he did, for help in doing what you already know God wants, and be willing to wait and watch.

Before he had done speaking

Before he had done speaking, behold, Rebekah came out.

“And it shall come to pass, that before they call, I will answer; and while they are yet speaking, I will hear.”

Isaiah 65:24

God did not make him wait while he thought it over. The answer was already on the way. It was already walking to the well while he was praying.

This isn't a promise that every prayer is answered in a minute. Often the answer waits for years, as Isaac and Rebekah will find in chapter 25: twenty years. It is the plain picture of a God who was never far from his servant, and who had been arranging things before the servant ever opened his mouth.

Rebekah is Abraham's great-niece, the granddaughter of his brother Nahor. Her brother is Laban, whose name you will hear a great deal in the chapters ahead.

She did more than anyone asked

The strangerLet me drink a little water from thy pitcher.

RebekahDrink, my lord. And I will draw water for thy camels also, until they have done drinking.
the offer nobody asked for

He asked for a sip. She offered water for ten camels. After weeks of walking, a thirsty camel can drink twenty gallons or more, and she carried it up from the well in a jar that held a few gallons at a time. That is many trips down the steps and back, and “drew for all his camels”.

“Ran again unto the well”: she did it at a run, for a stranger she had never met. This is what the servant had asked for: not beauty (it is mentioned, once, in verse 16), but a kind, strong, willing heart.

The gold, and the answer

about ⅕ ozthe earring, half a shekel
about 4 ozthe two bracelets, ten shekels
about 4⅕ ozall together

A shekel was about two-fifths of an ounce, so the weights here are only a few ounces: a real gift, but a small one next to ten camels. In verse 47 the servant says he put it “upon her face”, so many teachers think this “earring” was a ring for the nose.

He asks the question that matters: “Whose daughter art thou?” She answers, and it is Bethuel, Nahor's son: exactly the family. There is also room to lodge, and “provender”, which is feed for the animals.

“I being in the way”

I being in the way, the LORD led me.

Before he asks another question, the servant bows his head and gives God the credit. He didn't say I was lucky, or I'm good at this. He said the LORD led him, “in the way”: on the road he had been told to take.

“Trust in the LORD with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding. In all thy ways acknowledge him, and he shall direct thy paths.”

Proverbs 3:5-6

He was in the way, walking, and God led. It is hard to steer a parked car. He had gone where he was sent, and God arranged the meeting. When you do not know what comes next, do the next right thing in front of you, and keep your eyes open for the Lord.

His mercy and his truth

“Blessed be the LORD God of my master Abraham, who hath not left destitute my master of his mercy and his truth.” Destitute means left without. The servant uses two words of God: mercy (the faithful kindness of verse 12, chesed) and truth (He keeps his word).

Those two together describe how God is. He is kind, and he keeps his promises. Abraham had made a mess of things before (Hagar, chapter 16) the lie in Egypt (chapter 12) and the lie at Gerar (chapter 20), and God had not left him destitute. The servant knew that the LORD of his master was a God who stays.

And notice that “the LORD” is named 19 times in this chapter. For a story about a wedding, it is really a story about God.

In verse 21 the servant “held his peace” and watched: he was quietly checking, “to wit”, whether the LORD had made his journey go well. He did not hurry in with ideas of his own.

Part three

Laban's house

Verses 28–49

A hungry traveller who will not eat until he has said what he came to say.

Meet Laban

This is Laban's first appearance, and the Bible gives us two things about him at once. He is a warm host: “Come in, thou blessed of the LORD”. And Genesis points out that he ran out “when he saw the earring and bracelets”.

Keep an eye on this man. He will be Jacob's uncle and, in the end, his father-in-law, and the gold on his sister's arms is a hint of the man you will meet in chapters 29 to 31. The text does not call him greedy here. It just lets us notice what caught his eye.

Gold catches every man's eye. The question for the rest of the story is what a man does about it.

“I will not eat until…”

I will not eat, until I have told mine errand.

The meat is on the table (meat is the old word for food), the water is ready for his feet, and he is hungry after weeks on the road. Yet he says: first the business. Then dinner.

Hospitality was a real part of life then: water to wash the dust off the feet, straw and feed for the camels. “Ungirded” means he took the loads and straps off the camels. Laban's house has done everything right. And he still puts his errand first.

There is a lesson for men whose lives were ruled by appetite. This man could say no to a good meal, hungry as he was, because something bigger had hold of him. Wanting something more than your cravings is half of the battle.

“I am Abraham's servant”

The longest speech in the chapter starts with one honest line: who he is. A servant, and proud to be. He doesn't ask for the family's trust in himself. He asks for it for the sake of his master.

  1. blessed“the LORD hath blessed my master greatly”
  2. greatflocks, herds, silver, gold, servants, camels, donkeys
  3. an heir“Sarah my master's wife bare a son… when she was old”
  4. everything to him“unto him hath he given all that he hath”

Everything Abraham has already belongs to Isaac. The woman who marries Isaac marries into all of it. This is a picture for every believer: a son who is heir to everything, and a family joined to him.

He tells it again

Genesis 24 is the longest chapter in Genesis, 67 verses. It is longer than the whole of Genesis 1, which has 31. Much of that is because the story is told twice, once as it happens and once by the servant.

Scripture slows down for what matters. A bride for Isaac is the next link in the line that leads to the Saviour (Matthew 1:2), and the Bible spends its words on it.

Notice, too, what the servant does in the retelling. He admits his own doubt (“Peradventure the woman will not follow me”, verse 39) and he gives the LORD all the credit, again. Telling the story straight, with God in the middle, is called a testimony. You have one. Learn to tell it plainly.

“Tell me: yes or no”

If ye will deal kindly and truly with my master, tell me: and if not, tell me.

This is where he stops telling and asks. And he asks for a straight answer, either way, so that he can “turn to the right hand, or to the left”, that is, go on or go elsewhere.

He does not argue. He does not use the gold to press them. He puts the question to the family, and he is willing to hear no. A good errand never needs pressure.

Notice the phrase “deal kindly and truly”: chesed and truth again (verse 27). He asks the family to do for Isaac what God has done for Abraham.

Part four

“I will go”

Verses 50–67

She is asked, she answers, and she rides out into a life she has not seen.

Three times he bowed

  1. verse 26At the well“bowed down his head, and worshipped the LORD”
  2. verse 48In the house“I bowed down my head, and worshipped the LORD”
  3. verse 52When they say yes“bowing himself to the earth”

Every time something goes right, he stops and worships. He never skips from the answer to the next job. Each bow goes lower than the last: head, head, and then to the earth.

Laban and Bethuel say, “The thing proceedeth from the LORD”, and “we cannot speak unto thee bad or good”: it is not for them to argue with God. Even in a family that had its own gods, the hand of the LORD could be seen.

“Hinder me not”

Her brother and motherLet the damsel abide with us a few days, at the least ten.

The servantHinder me not, seeing the LORD hath prospered my way.
the door is open: go through it

They wanted ten days to say goodbye. It was natural and loving, and an ordinary delay. The servant would not accept it. God had opened the door, and a day lost might be a door half shut.

Wanting time is not always wrong. Yet when the Lord has plainly opened a door, “a few days” can turn into never. Delay is a quiet enemy of obedience, and men in recovery know how many “not yet”s it takes to lose a year.

“Wilt thou go?” “I will go.”

I will go.

The family could have settled it themselves. They did not. They called Rebekah and asked her, and the choice was hers. “I will go” is one of the shortest and bravest sentences in Genesis.

She was leaving her home, her family, and everyone she knew for a man she had never seen, on the word of a servant and the evidence of one day. Abraham had done the same in chapter 12, when he heard God and went. Rebekah does what Abraham did: she goes.

She does not go alone: “her nurse” goes too, and her damsels. We meet that nurse by name later, when she dies (35:8).

Her family's blessing

be thou the mother of thousands of millions,

and let thy seed possess the gate of those which hate them.

“Thousands of millions” is the KJV's way of saying ten thousand times a thousand: more than anyone could count. A gate was where a city's leaders sat, so “possess the gate” means to hold the place of power over your enemies.

Listen to those words. They are almost word for word what God told Abraham on the mountain in chapter 22: “thy seed shall possess the gate of his enemies” (22:17). Her family may not even have known they were repeating a promise. Yet they said it over her.

Isaac at Hagar's well

Isaac lived beside the well Lahai-roi, the well where Hagar met God in the wilderness and said, “Thou God seest me” (16:13–14). The well of the Living One who sees.

It is a quiet kind of place: a well where a woman running away from a hard life was seen, and heard, and sent home. Isaac chose to live there.

He “went out to meditate in the field at the eventide”: that is, in the evening. He is out in the open, quiet, turning things over before God, perhaps still grieving his mother.

“She lighted off the camel” means she got down quickly from her camel. Her heart was in her throat, and who can blame her?

  3. hinderhold back, delay

The vail was a bride's custom. She covers her face before the man she is to marry, and he will see her when she is his.

“Isaac was comforted”

The last verse of the chapter says two things: “he loved her”, and “Isaac was comforted after his mother's death”. He had been mourning for about three years. He had not “gotten over it”. He was comforted, and God used a person to do it.

“Blessed be God, even the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of mercies, and the God of all comfort; Who comforteth us in all our tribulation, that we may be able to comfort them which are in any trouble, by the comfort wherewith we ourselves are comforted of God.”

2 Corinthians 1:3-4

If you are grieving someone, a parent, a child, a marriage, or years you lost, hear this. Grief takes time, and comfort is real. God usually comforts through people. Call or text your brothers in Christ, and your pastor, and let them sit with you.

A picture of something bigger?

the Fathersends a servantto find a bridefor his sonthe son
the servantbrings gifts, tells the story, asks “Wilt thou go?”the bride“I will go”

This is a picture of the Father sending his Spirit to call a bride for his Son: the servant goes with gifts, tells her all about the son, and brings her home to him.

What we can say plainly is true of Rebekah: she was asked, and she answered, and she went. The Lord calls, and a man must say, “I will go.” And what we find on the other side is a Son who has everything, waiting in the field, who loves us.

Points to remember

  1. Prayer“before he had done speaking, behold, Rebekah came out” 24:15God is never far from the man who asks. The answer may already be on its way.
  2. Being led“I being in the way, the LORD led me” 24:27Keep walking in obedience. It is the moving man God steers.
  3. No going back“Beware thou that thou bring not my son thither again.” 24:6Don't take your life back to the old country.
  4. Character“I will draw water for thy camels also” 24:19Real character shows in the small kindnesses nobody asked for.
  5. Her choice“Wilt thou go with this man? And she said, I will go.” 24:58God invites; it is for each of us to say “I will go.”
  6. Comfort“Isaac was comforted after his mother's death” 24:67Grief is real, and God comforts, often through people.

Question 2 of 6

Being led

What does “I being in the way, the LORD led me” mean for us?

What verses tell us about this?

“In all thy ways acknowledge him, and he shall direct thy paths.”

Proverbs 3:6

Answer

The servant was on the road he had been sent down, doing what he was told, and there God met him. God steers a man who is moving in obedience. Do the next right thing, trust him with the rest, and give him the credit.

Question 3 of 6

No going back

Why would Abraham not let Isaac go back to the old country?

What verses tell us about this?

“And truly, if they had been mindful of that country from whence they came out, they might have had opportunity to have returned.”

Hebrews 11:15

Answer

God had taken Abraham out of that country and given him this land. To send Isaac back would be to undo the call. If you have left an old life, don't go back for a visit, and don't take anyone you love back there.

Question 4 of 6

Character

What did Rebekah do at the well that showed who she was?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

She gave a stranger a drink, and then, unasked, ran back and forth to water ten thirsty camels. Character is what a person does for a stranger when nobody asked. Be that kind of man today, in one small thing.

Question 5 of 6

Her choice

Who decided whether Rebekah would go?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Rebekah did. The family called her and asked, “Wilt thou go?” and she answered for herself: “I will go.” God leads, but he does not drag. Each of us has to say “I will go” for himself.
