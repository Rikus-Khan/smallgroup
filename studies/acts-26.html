Acts 26

Almost persuaded

Paul tells a king the story of the worst thing he ever did and the best thing that ever happened to him, and the king stops one step short.

Part one

A prisoner speaks

Verses 1–11

Paul stretches out his chained hand and begins, with the worst part first.

Who is in the room

  • Festusthe Roman governor, who has no idea what to write about Paul
  • King Agripparaised among Jews, “expert in all customs and questions”
  • Bernicehis sister, beside him
  • The chief captains and principal menthe important people of Caesarea

This is Paul's fourth hearing in two years: the Jewish council, then Felix, then Festus, and now a king. Each one has asked him to speak. None has set him free.

Notice how he opens: “I think myself happy.” Paul is a chained man talking to a king, and he is glad, because here is someone who actually knows the Scriptures. He is courteous before he is bold. Respect and truth go together.

On trial for a hope

“I stand and am judged for the hope of the promise.”

Paul is not on trial for a crime. He is on trial for a hope: that God keeps His promises, and that He raises the dead. Every Jew in that room had been taught the same hope.

Then he asks the king straight out: “Why should it be thought a thing incredible… that God should raise the dead?” If God made everything, a grave is not too big for Him.

For a man who feels finished, that is the good news. A God who can raise the dead can raise a ruined life, a wrecked marriage, a man who thinks it's too late. Today is not the end of your story.

Paul tells a king the worst of himself and doesn't flinch. “Exceedingly mad” here means raging, furious. The man who could say that out loud had stopped hiding, because Jesus had already forgiven all of it.

Part two

The light on the road

Verses 12–18

The story the Book tells three times. This is the one told to a king.

Noon on the Damascus road

Damascus lay about 130 miles north of Jerusalem. Paul was going with letters from the chief priests to arrest believers there.

At midday the light that stopped him was “above the brightness of the sun.” Nothing in his own strength could have turned him around. Jesus had to stop him.

Notice the question: “why persecutest thou me?” Whatever is done to His people is done to Him. Jesus came to find Paul on the very road he took to do harm.

Kicking against the pricks

A farmer drove his ox with a goad, a stick with a sharp point. If the ox kicked back, it only drove the point deeper.

Jesus says it on the road itself (9:5, 26:14): you've been fighting Me, and it has been hurting you.

If you've been kicking against God, you know the restless nights and the guilt. That isn't God hunting you down. It's a Shepherd who wants you to stop running.

Three tellings, one story. The facts that matter never change: the light, the name, Jesus. What shifts is what suits the listener. To a Jewish crowd, Paul stresses Ananias, a devout man of the law. To a Gentile king, he skips straight to Jesus' own commission.

That's how a real witness talks. He doesn't memorize a speech. He tells what happened, to the person in front of him.

From darkness to light

Where he wasIn the dark

“The power of Satan.” The old life had a boss, and it wasn't him.

What Jesus doesOpens their eyes

He turns them “from darkness to light.” A man can't see his own way out, so He opens the eyes first.

Where he ends upForgiven, and kept

“Forgiveness of sins, and inheritance.” Set apart “by faith that is in me.”

“Who hath delivered us from the power of darkness, and hath translated us into the kingdom of his dear Son:”

Colossians 1:13

Notice what saves: “faith that is in me.” Not clean-up first, and not trying harder. And the inheritance comes with the forgiveness. If you are in Christ, the God who started this is not going to take it back.

Part three

Almost

Verses 19–32

What Paul did next, and how a king answered him.

Damascus first, then the world

Paul gives the order: Damascus, Jerusalem, all Judaea, “and then to the Gentiles.” It's the same order Jesus gave in Acts 1:8, now worked out in one man's life.

Three journeys across Asia Minor and Greece, then the voyage to Rome. Thousands of miles on roads and ships, from a man who once took the road to arrest believers.

He says “I was not disobedient unto the heavenly vision.” He didn't wait until he felt ready. He did the next thing Jesus showed him, and then the next.

Works that fit a changed heart

Works to earn it

The root? No.

Doing good to get God to accept you. Paul says clearly it's not this: “not of works, lest any man should boast” (Ephesians 2:9).

Works that show it

The fruit. Yes.

“Works meet for repentance”: meet means fitting. A changed mind shows up in a changed life.

Repent, turn, then do. The order matters. Change your mind about sin and Jesus, turn toward God, and the life follows. For a man in recovery that means the daily things: showing up, paying back, staying clean, telling the truth. They don't save you. They show you've been saved.

Nothing new, only what was promised

  1. ✓That Christ should sufferthe suffering servant, Isaiah 5326:23
  2. ✓that he should be the first that should rise from the dead“thou wilt not leave my soul in hell,” Psalm 16:1026:23
  3. ✓and should shew light unto the people, and to the Gentiles“a light of the Gentiles,” Isaiah 49:626:23

Paul has nothing new to sell. Moses and the prophets said all of it. “The first that should rise” means the first of many: because He lives, those who trust Him will rise too.

And see how he explains his survival: “having obtained help of God, I continue unto this day.” Not his own strength. Not a clean record. Help from God, one day at a time.

Mad, or in his right mind?

Festus, shoutingPaul, thou art beside thyself; much learning doth make thee mad.

“Not done in a corner” means it all happened in the open. Anyone could check. Jesus' death and resurrection was not a secret rumour. The king knew about it, and Paul says so to his face.

Back in verse 11 Paul really was “exceedingly mad”, raging. Now he's with Jesus and he's the sane one, and the man in robes calls him a lunatic. When you start following Jesus, some people will think you've lost your mind. That's all right.

One step short

Agrippa was moved. He listened, he knew the prophets, he felt the pull. Whether he meant it seriously or brushed Paul off with a smile, the king stopped right there.

“Almost” saved is not saved. A man can stand on the last stone and still be in the river. The last step isn't more effort. It's trust: “whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord shall be saved” (Romans 10:13).

And if you already trust Jesus, “almost” is not you. You crossed. A fall since then doesn't undo it.

“Except these bonds”

Agrippa→free, crowned, “almost”Everything a man could want, and one thing missing.
Paul→in chains, and “altogether”Everything taken from him, and the one thing that matters in his hands.

Look at Paul's answer. The man in the chain feels sorry for the man in the crown. He wishes the king, and everyone listening, could have what he has, “except these bonds.” He doesn't want anyone chained.

And the danger of “almost” is the delay. Felix said, “when I have a convenient season, I will call for thee” (24:25), and never did. “Behold, now is the accepted time; behold, now is the day of salvation” (2 Corinthians 6:2). Today.

Free on paper, headed for Rome

Innocent, and still in chains.

The king, the governor and the captains all agree: “nothing worthy of death or of bonds.” And Agrippa adds that Paul could have walked free, if he hadn't appealed to Caesar.

It would be easy to call that a mistake. But the appeal was the road God had promised: “so must thou bear witness also at Rome” (23:11). It cost Paul his freedom for two more years, and it carried the gospel to the capital of the world.

When doing the right thing costs you something, it isn't wasted. God uses the long way round.

Tell it the way Paul did

Verses 9–11What I was

Paul didn't dress it up. He said what he'd done, plainly.

Verses 12–18How I met Jesus

One day, one road, one Person who stopped him.

Verses 19–23What I do now

He repented, turned, and got on with the work.

That's a story anyone can tell, in about a minute. Today, try it once, to one person who needs to hear it: a friend, a brother, a man at work. Not a sermon, just what you were, how you met Jesus, and what's different now.

You don't need anyone's permission, or a perfect past. Paul's past was the worst in the room, and God used it.

Points to remember

  1. A changed man“Saul, Saul, why persecutest thou me?” 26:14Jesus came after Paul on the very road he took to do harm. He came after you too.
  2. Salvation“from darkness to light, and from the power of Satan unto God” 26:18Jesus opens blind eyes, forgives, and brings you in. By faith, not by clean-up.
  3. Repentance“repent and turn to God, and do works meet for repentance” 26:20Change your mind, turn to God, and let the life follow. The works show it. They don't earn it.
  4. Hope“that God should raise the dead” 26:8A God who raises the dead can raise a ruined life. Today is not your end.
  5. Almost“Almost thou persuadest me to be a Christian” 26:28Almost saved is not saved. Don't wait for a convenient season. Today.
  6. Telling others“I would to God… all that hear me this day” 26:29Tell what you were, how you met Jesus, and what's different. Kindly, plainly, and wanting the best for them.

Question 1 of 6

A changed man

How did Paul go from hunting Christians to serving Jesus?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Jesus stopped him. Paul was on his way to arrest believers when a light from heaven knocked him to the ground and a voice said his name. He didn't talk himself into it. Jesus came to him, and he said yes. Then he did not disobey the vision for the rest of his life.

Question 2 of 6

Salvation

What does Jesus rescue people from, and what does He bring them into?

What verses tell us about this?

“Who hath delivered us from the power of darkness, and hath translated us into the kingdom of his dear Son:”

Colossians 1:13

Answer

He rescues us from darkness and from the power of Satan, and brings us to light, to God, to forgiveness of sins, and to an inheritance. It all comes by faith in Jesus, not by earning it.

Question 3 of 6

Repentance

What does a changed life look like?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

It starts with turning from sin to God. Then it shows: obeying what God shows you, and doing things that fit a changed heart. It isn't perfect. It carries on, with God's help, one day at a time.

Question 4 of 6

Hope

Why does the resurrection matter?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

It is the hope everything else rests on. Christ was the first to rise, and those who trust Him will follow. If God can raise the dead, no life is too far gone, and no day is too late to start over.

Question 5 of 6

Almost

What is the danger of being “almost” a Christian?

What verses tell us about this?

“(For he saith, I have heard thee in a time accepted, and in the day of salvation have I succoured thee: behold, now is the accepted time; behold, now is the day of salvation.)”

2 Corinthians 6:2

Answer

Almost saved is not saved. Agrippa was moved and still stopped short. The danger is putting it off: a convenient season that never comes. Paul's answer is “altogether,” and God's answer is “now.”

Question 6 of 6

Telling others

How did Paul speak to a king about Jesus?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Politely, honestly, and personally. He was glad to speak, he told the truth even about his worst years, and he finished by wishing the king well. Respect and boldness go together.
