Leviticus 15

Unclean until the even

The body, and what it does without asking. Uncleanness that is not sin, a washing, an evening, and an eighth day at the door of the tabernacle. And one woman in the gospels who touched the hem of his garment.

Part one

“A running issue out of his flesh”

Verses 1–15

What it did to a man and to everything around him, and the way back.

Old words for a plain subject

  • an issuea flowing out: the KJV's word for a discharge from the body
  • uncleannessunfit to come near the tabernacle for a time, until he is washed (it is not the same as sin)
  • the eventhe evening, the end of the day
  • bathewash the whole body in water
  • rinsedwashed out with water
  • a vessel of eartha clay pot
  • a saddlethe seat laid on a beast to ride it

This chapter is about the body. Moses writes it plainly and without shame, and we should read it the same way. God made the body, and he is not embarrassed by it. And note that it begins like chapter 13: the LORD “spake unto Moses and to Aaron,” in the one month at Sinai between the tabernacle going up (Exodus 40:17) and the numbering of the people (Numbers 1:1).

Until the even

Whoever touched his bed or sat where he sat, or touched him, had one thing to do: “wash his clothes, and bathe himself in water, and be unclean until the even.”

“Until the even” comes 15 times in this chapter. That is the whole sentence of most uncleanness here: a washing, and a day that ends. Evening comes every day without being asked. For this kind of uncleanness, God put a wall of time between the man and the shame. When the sun went down, it was over.

The pot and the bowl

A vessel of earth

broken

A clay pot he had touched was broken (verse 12).

A vessel of wood

rinsed in water

A wooden bowl was rinsed in water (verse 12).

The text does not say why the two are treated differently. We only need what it says: the uncleanness was taken seriously, but a thing that could be washed was washed, and washing was the road back. Verse 11 even says what a rinse of the hands was worth: if the man with the issue touched someone and “hath not rinsed his hands in water,” that person had to wash and wait until the even.

The weight of this chapter

“unclean”

27

times, in 33 verses

“clean”

3

times, in verses 8, 13 and 28

It reads heavy, and it was meant to. But look at the three “clean”s. One is a clean man who must not be spat upon (verse 8). The other two are the ends of the roads: a man healed of his issue washes “in running water, and shall be clean” (verse 13), and a woman cleansed of her issue counts seven days, “and after that she shall be clean” (verse 28). Every road in this chapter ends there.

The way back

  1. the issue stopshe is cleansed of itverse 13
  2. seven dayshe counts them himselfverse 13
  3. running waterwash clothes, bathe fleshverse 13
  4. the eighth daytwo doves at the doorverses 14–15

He “shall number to himself seven days”: the counting is his own. Then he comes “before the LORD unto the door of the tabernacle,” the same door the healed leper came to in chapter 14. One dove is for a sin offering, and one for a burnt offering, and “the priest shall make an atonement for him before the LORD for his issue.”

Two things stand out. First, the chapter never says the man with an issue has sinned, and yet an atonement is made for him. Our bodies are not what they were made to be, and we all need a way back to God, whether the trouble is our doing or not. Second, the offering is two small birds, the poor man's offering again (14:22). Nobody is too poor for this door.

Part two

“Seed of copulation”

Verses 16–18

A very short passage about a very ordinary thing, and the same answer: wash, and wait for the evening.

Uncleanness is not the same as sin

“Seed of copulation” is the KJV's plain old phrase for a man's seed. Verse 18 speaks of a man and a woman lying together, and says that “they shall both bathe themselves in water, and be unclean until the even.” That is not a charge of sin. The law treats it as the same kind of uncleanness as everything else in this chapter: wash, and wait until the evening.

The New Testament says plainly that marriage is not sin:

“Marriage is honourable in all, and the bed undefiled: but whoremongers and adulterers God will judge.”

Hebrews 13:4

So what is uncleanness, if it is not sin? It was a sign. It kept the people always aware of three things: that God is holy, that he dwells in the midst of them, and that a man cannot walk up to him as he is, but must be washed first. Sin is the real uncleanness, and it needs more than water. God marked the difference, and he gave the cure for each.

Part three

“If a woman have an issue”

Verses 19–30

The same law for a woman, and a woman in the gospels who broke it, and was made whole.

More old words

  • her flowersthe KJV's old word for a woman's monthly time
  • her separationthe days she is set apart
  • put apartset aside from others, seven days
  • a running issuea flowing that does not stop (verses 25–27)
  • turtlesturtledoves (verse 29), small wild doves

Seven days of being put apart: the number comes four times in this chapter (verses 13, 19, 24 and 28). The words are plain and the law is short. Nothing here is a judgment on a woman's character. It is the same wash-and-wait as for a man, and it is for the same reason: the tabernacle “is among them.”

A woman who could not get clean

Verse 25 describes a woman whose issue goes on “many days” past its time. Until it stopped, there was no “if she be cleansed” for her (verse 28): she stayed unclean, and whoever touched what she sat on was unclean until the even (verse 27). Hold that, and read this:

“And a certain woman, which had an issue of blood twelve years, And had suffered many things of many physicians, and had spent all that she had, and was nothing bettered, but rather grew worse, When she had heard of Jesus, came in the press behind, and touched his garment.”

Mark 5:25–27

Twelve years. She had “suffered many things of many physicians, and had spent all that she had, and was nothing bettered, but rather grew worse.” She had run out of money, and she was out of options. And by this law, anyone who touched her was unclean until the even (verse 19), so she had no business in a crowd. She “came in the press behind” him, in secret.

She touched him, and he stopped

“For she said, If I may touch but his clothes, I shall be whole. And straightway the fountain of her blood was dried up; and she felt in her body that she was healed of that plague.”

Mark 5:28–29

“But the woman fearing and trembling, knowing what was done in her, came and fell down before him, and told him all the truth. And he said unto her, Daughter, thy faith hath made thee whole; go in peace, and be whole of thy plague.”

Mark 5:33–34

Under this law, contact with a woman in her condition made a man unclean until the even. It did not here. The cleanness went the other way: she was made whole. “Fearing and trembling” she told him “all the truth,” and he called her “Daughter.” This is what the chapter was waiting for: a hand that can touch the unclean and not be made unclean.

Matthew tells it too: “thy faith hath made thee whole… And the woman was made whole from that hour” (Matthew 9:22). “Go in peace.”

Part four

“That they die not in their uncleanness”

Verses 31–33

Why all of this?

“My tabernacle that is among them”

Here is the reason, in one verse: “that they die not in their uncleanness, when they defile my tabernacle that is among them.” God's house stood in the middle of the camp. The law was not there to make people feel bad about their bodies. It was there because a holy God lived among them, and a man who came near him unwashed was in real danger.

God in the middle of the camp

The same reason is given where the lepers, those with an issue, and those defiled by the dead are all put out of the camp:

“Command the children of Israel, that they put out of the camp every leper, and every one that hath an issue, and whosoever is defiled by the dead: Both male and female shall ye put out, without the camp shall ye put them; that they defile not their camps, in the midst whereof I dwell.”

Numbers 5:2–3

“In the midst whereof I dwell.” It is a holy neighbourhood. Every wash in this chapter, every evening and every dove at the door, said the same thing: the LORD is here, and he is holy, and there is a way to come near him.

The blood that does what water cannot

Water cleaned the body. The doves at the door dealt with the man before God. And the New Testament says what finally cleans us:

“For if the blood of bulls and of goats, and the ashes of an heifer sprinkling the unclean, sanctifieth to the purifying of the flesh: How much more shall the blood of Christ, who through the eternal Spirit offered himself without spot to God, purge your conscience from dead works to serve the living God?”

Hebrews 9:13–14

“Purge your conscience.” The part of a man that water cannot reach: the part that remembers, and carries shame into the night.

“And such were some of you: but ye are washed, but ye are sanctified, but ye are justified in the name of the Lord Jesus, and by the Spirit of our God.”

1 Corinthians 6:11

Paul is writing to men with a past, and he does not hide it: “such were some of you.” Then three buts: washed, sanctified, justified. Past tense, and finished. Not by water and not by willpower, but “in the name of the Lord Jesus, and by the Spirit of our God.”

Real world: the shame in the body

Most men carry something about their body, or what they have done with it, that they would not say out loud. This chapter is God speaking plainly about the body, and not looking away.

  • SinSome of what the body does is not sin at all (verse 18; Hebrews 13:4). Some is. Do not mix the two up, and do not let anyone else mix them up for you.
  • RegretIf the shame is for something you did, bring it to the one who washes. “Such were some of you: but ye are washed.”
  • GraceThe longest sentence for most of this chapter is “until the even.” God made evening, and he made a door.
  • HopeA woman twelve years unclean touched his clothes and heard “Go in peace.”
  • TodayIf there is something you have been carrying alone, call or text your brothers in Christ, or your pastor, and say it out loud. What is said in the light loses the power it has in the dark.

Points to remember

  1. The touch“whosoever toucheth his bed shall wash his clothes, and bathe himself in water, and be unclean until the even” 15:5The answer was always the same: wash, and wait for the evening.
  2. The things“the vessel of earth… shall be broken: and every vessel of wood shall be rinsed in water” 15:12The uncleanness was taken seriously, and what could be washed was washed.
  3. The evening“be unclean until the even” 15:5Fifteen times: most uncleanness here lasted a day. The sun goes down on it.
  4. The eighth day“two turtledoves, or two young pigeons, and come before the LORD unto the door of the tabernacle” 15:14Even here, a way back to the door, with the poor man's offering.
  5. The woman“she shall number to herself seven days, and after that she shall be clean” 15:28Her road ends in “clean” too. And one woman touched Jesus and heard “Go in peace.”
  6. The reason“that they die not in their uncleanness, when they defile my tabernacle that is among them” 15:31God lives in the middle of his people. He is holy, and he made the way to come near.

Question 3 of 6

The evening

How long did that kind of uncleanness last?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Until the evening: “until the even” comes 15 times in this chapter. A washing, and a day that ends.

Question 4 of 6

The eighth day

What did a man bring on the eighth day, and where?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Two turtledoves or two young pigeons, to the door of the tabernacle, after he had counted seven days and washed in running water. The priest offered one for a sin offering, one for a burnt offering, and made an atonement for him.

Question 5 of 6

The woman

What was done when a woman had an issue of blood?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

She was put apart seven days. If her issue ran on many days beyond that, then when she was cleansed she counted seven days, and on the eighth brought two turtledoves or two young pigeons to the priest. A woman in Mark 5 who had been unclean twelve years touched Jesus' clothes and was made whole.
