Genesis 49

Twelve sons, twelve blessings

Jacob calls his sons around his bed and tells each the truth: Reuben's fall, Simeon and Levi's anger, a King from Judah, and one cry in the middle: “I have waited for thy salvation, O LORD.”

Part one

“Gather yourselves together”

Verses 1–7

A dying father tells each son the truth, and the first three hear hard words.

A father's last words

Jacob is about to say the last things he will ever say to his twelve sons. “The last days” here means the days to come: what will happen to each of them and to the tribes that come from them.

These are not twelve greeting cards. Some are praise, some are warning, and some sound like a curse. All of them are true, and he calls the whole speech a blessing (verse 28).

Most of us never had a father who sat us down and told us the truth about ourselves. Some of the men you meet in the church will do that for you. Listen to them when they do. A truth from a man who loves you is a gift, even when it hurts.

Reuben: from “my might” to “unstable as water”

Reuben, thou art my firstborn, my might, the excellency of dignity, and the excellency of power:

Unstable as water, thou shalt not excel;

Reuben had everything: the firstborn's place, the double share, the leadership. Then he went up to his father's bed. Genesis 35:22 says it in one line: he lay with Bilhah, his father's concubine, “and Israel heard it.” That is all the Bible says, and it is all we need to say.

Unstable as water: water poured out has no shape of its own. It goes wherever the ground tips. One act of sin was the sign of a man who could not hold his shape.

Reuben lived, with a scar

The birthright went to Joseph's sons, and the rule went to Judah (1 Chronicles 5:1–2). As far as the Bible records, no judge, king or prophet came from Reuben's tribe. His sin cost him and his sons, and it lasted.

“Let Reuben live, and not die; and let not his men be few.”

Deuteronomy 33:6

But Moses, generations later, still prays for Reuben's tribe: let him live. The consequence stood. So did the mercy. If your own record has scars you can't undo, notice that God's answer to a failed firstborn was not to throw him away.

Sin has a price, and grace is real. Both are true at once.

Old words in verses 1–7

  • befallhappen to
  • excelhold the first place, stand above the rest
  • defiledstdefiled: made unclean, dishonoured
  • habitationshomes, the places where they live
  • selfwillstubborn will, doing whatever he pleased

Anger with a sword

Dinah, Jacob's daughter, was wronged by a man of Shechem (chapter 34). Her brothers Simeon and Levi answered with a lie and a massacre: they killed Hamor, his son, and every male in the town. Jacob was horrified even then:

“Ye have troubled me to make me to stink among the inhabitants of the land, among the Canaanites and the Perizzites: and I being few in number, they shall gather themselves together against me, and slay me; and I shall be destroyed, I and my house.”

Genesis 34:30

Close to fifty years later, he has not forgotten. Notice the care in verse 7: he curses their anger, not the men. Their sin was real, and it was fierce, but they were still his sons.

Anger, and what God did with it

Levi, at Shechem

Sword in his own cause

“In their anger they slew a man.” The tribe is to be scattered, with no land of its own.

Levi, at Sinai

On the LORD's side

“All the sons of Levi gathered themselves together unto him” (Exodus 32:26). The scattering became a calling: priests among all the tribes.

“For the wrath of man worketh not the righteousness of God.”

James 1:20

Anger is the old enemy of many of you. Jacob's word was cursed be their anger. Yet a tribe known for violence became the tribe that served God. Hold on to that. Your worst habit does not have the last word.

Part two

Judah

Verses 8–12

The fourth son gets the longest promise, and the line of the King runs through him.

Old words in verses 8–12

  • whelpa young lion, a cub
  • couchedcrouched down, lay down like a cat
  • sceptrethe staff a king holds, the sign of his rule
  • lawgivera ruler who gives the orders (the word may also mean his staff)
  • foala young donkey
  • choice vinethe best kind of vine

The picture of verse 9: a young lion has brought down his prey, and now he lies down, and no one dares to wake him. The strength is real, and it is at rest.

Judah, the man who changed

Genesis 38

“She hath been more righteous than I”

Judah failed Tamar, his daughter-in-law, and had to say so out loud. It is one of the lowest points of the book.

Genesis 44

“Let thy servant abide instead of the lad”

Years later he offered to become a slave so that Benjamin could go home. This is the man whom his brothers will praise.

Judah sold his brother (37:26–27). Judah failed Tamar. And God picked him, not Reuben or Joseph, to carry the royal line. A man can fail badly, own it, and be changed. Look at what he does when Benjamin is in danger (44:33): he steps in front.

“Until Shiloh come”

The sceptre shall not depart from Judah,

nor a lawgiver from between his feet,

until Shiloh come; and unto him shall the gathering of the people be.

Judah's tribe will hold the ruler's staff. It did not start until David, more than eight hundred years after Jacob said it, and it ran through the kings of Judah. Then it will pass to Shiloh, and the peoples will gather to him.

Who is Shiloh? The Messiah: the King to whom the sceptre belongs, as Ezekiel 21:27 says, “until he come whose right it is”. Jewish readers of old took this verse the same way. It is not the town of Shiloh: this one is a man, and the gathering is to him.

The Lion of the tribe of Judah

“For it is evident that our Lord sprang out of Juda; of which tribe Moses spake nothing concerning priesthood.”

Hebrews 7:14

“And one of the elders saith unto me, Weep not: behold, the Lion of the tribe of Juda, the Root of David, hath prevailed to open the book, and to loose the seven seals thereof.”

Revelation 5:5

The New Testament says Jesus came from Judah, and it uses the lion of verse 9 for him. The King Jacob saw coming is the one the gospel is about. The lion who overcame is the same Jesus who laid down his life like the lamb. A man at the end of himself can bring everything to him.

Plenty, and a word about wine

Verses 11–12 are a picture of plenty: so many vines that a man ties his donkey to one and washes his clothes in the juice, so much milk that the teeth are white. It means a land overflowing, a king's land.

Wine is a word that lands differently for some of you. The Bible uses it both ways, as plenty here and as a warning in Proverbs 20:1. Nothing in this verse is an invitation to drink. It says the King's land will have more than enough.

If a drink has ever owned you, the thing you want most is the one the King gives: not a bottle, but a land with more than enough, and a Lord who is enough.

Part three

Six sons in a line

Verses 13–21

Short words for six men, and one cry from the old man's heart.

Zebulun and Issachar

  • havena harbour, a safe place for ships
  • assa donkey, a strong working animal
  • tributea tax, a payment to a ruler

Zebulun: harbours and trade, up toward Zidon on the coast. Issachar: a strong donkey, lying down between two loads. He saw that rest was good and the land pleasant, so he bent his shoulder and became a servant under tribute.

That is an honest warning. A man can get so comfortable that he will carry anything, rather than fight for freedom. Quiet and rest are good. Settling for a life that is easy and owned by someone else is not.

Dan, and the snake in the path

Dan means judge, and the first line plays on it: Dan shall judge. Samson, who judged Israel, came from Dan (Judges 13–16). The second line is about an adder by the road that bites the horse's heel, so the rider falls backward: small, hidden, and deadly.

Later the tribe of Dan fell into idol worship (Judges 18; 1 Kings 12:28–30). Jacob's picture of a hidden snake fits sin too: it lies low, bites at the heel, and a strong man goes down.

That is why it is the next verse that matters most.

“I have waited for thy salvation, O LORD”

יְשׁוּעָה said yeshuah “salvation, rescue”: the same root as the name Jesus

In the middle of the list of sons the old man stops and prays, as though a snake had crossed the path. He cannot save his sons. They cannot save themselves. Only the LORD can, and he says it plain: I have waited.

“It is good that a man should both hope and quietly wait for the salvation of the LORD.”

Lamentations 3:26

The Hebrew word for salvation here is yeshuah, and the name Jesus (Yeshua) comes from it. You will not beat the serpent in your own strength. You wait on him, and he comes.

Gad, Asher and Naphtali

  • Gadraiders will attack him in a troop, but he will turn and overcome them at the last
  • Asherfat bread, rich food: “royal dainties” (dainties are delicacies)
  • Naphtalia hind (a female deer) let loose: free, swift, and he “giveth goodly words”

Gad is knocked down and rises. Asher is fed. Naphtali is free. Not one of these men is made to be the same as the others. God gave each son something of his own, even the quiet ones, the ones who are not leaders.

Where they ended up

Joshua divided the land among the tribes generations later (Joshua 13–19). The colours show it: Asher and Naphtali in the north, Zebulun and Issachar in the middle, Judah in the south, and Joseph's sons, Ephraim and Manasseh, in the heart of the land.

What Jacob said in his bed was still true when each tribe got its land, and a word spoken by an old man in Egypt was still working centuries later.

Part four

Joseph and Benjamin

Verses 22–28

The longest blessing goes to the son who was hurt the most, and the last goes to the youngest.

Joseph: “a fruitful bough”

The archers have sorely grieved him, and shot at him, and hated him:

But his bow abode in strength, and the arms of his hands were made strong

“A fruitful bough by a well”: a bough is a branch, a vine by a spring, growing so well it climbs over the wall. Ephraim's name means fruitful (41:52). The archers are the people who shot at him: his brothers, the slave market, Potiphar's wife, the prison.

Look at what is missing: there is no word of blame for Joseph in the whole chapter. His bow “abode in strength”: he did not shoot back. His strength came “by the hands of the mighty God of Jacob.”

“The shepherd, the stone of Israel”

In the middle of Joseph's blessing, Jacob names God again: “the mighty God of Jacob (from thence is the shepherd, the stone of Israel).” It is the same word he used in 48:15, “the God which fed me”: shepherded me.

“I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep.”

John 10:11

Jacob began as a shepherd for Laban, and he ends by calling God his own Shepherd and Rock. The One who shepherded him is the One who gave his life for the sheep.

Blessings on the head of the one set apart

  • progenitorsancestors, the ones who came before (Abraham and Isaac)
  • the deep that lieth undersprings and water under the ground
  • the breasts, and the wombchildren and families
  • separate from his brethrenset apart, the prince among them (the word can mean crowned)

Heaven above, water below, children, many sons. Jacob piles it up for the son who had been thrown into a pit. The brothers took him out of the family, and God set him apart as a prince.

If you were thrown out, or you threw out someone you love, this is where grace is working. God can raise up the one who was cast away.

Benjamin the wolf

Ravin means to tear and devour prey, like a wolf. Benjamin was the smallest of the tribes, and the fiercest. Saul, Israel's first king, came from Benjamin, and he called it “the smallest of the tribes” (1 Samuel 9:21). Paul, the apostle, was a Benjamite too.

“I say then, Hath God cast away his people? God forbid. For I also am an Israelite, of the seed of Abraham, of the tribe of Benjamin.”

Romans 11:1

In the morning he devours, at night he divides the spoil. Many have seen Paul in that picture: he began by tearing at the church, and ended by giving himself to it. The wolf became the one who fed the sheep. Nobody is too fierce for God to use.

“Every one according to his blessing”

The twelve sons did not hear the same thing. Reuben heard that he would not excel. Simeon and Levi heard that their anger was cursed. Judah heard of a King. Yet Jacob says he blessed them, every one.

A true word, said by a father who loves you, is a blessing, even when it names your sin. The reverse is also true: flattery is not a blessing. If someone you trust tells you what you need to hear, do not reach for the door. Hear him out.

And if you are the one who has to say the hard word to another man, say it the way Jacob did: name the sin, and curse the sin, not the man.

Part five

“Bury me with my fathers”

Verses 29–33

The old man's last wish is a grave in the land of the promise.

A grave in the promised land

Jacob has lived in Egypt for seventeen years, in the best land of Egypt. But he does not want to be buried there. He wants the cave of Machpelah, near Hebron, which Abraham bought (chapter 23), because it is the only piece of the promised land his family owned.

A grave is a small thing to own. But it was a deed to the promise: this land is ours, and we are staying.

Who lies in the cave

  • Abraham and Sarahthe first two, and the first to be buried there (23, 25)
  • Isaac and Rebekahthe next generation
  • Leah“there I buried Leah”
  • Jacobthe last one to be added

Not Rachel. Rachel, whom he loved, was buried on the road to Bethlehem. Jacob chooses to rest beside Leah, the wife he had not chosen and the one who was loved less (29:31). Many men have a marriage that began in a mess. Here is a man whose last choice was to lie with the wife God gave him.

“Gathered unto his people”

The old words in verse 33: he gathered up his feet into the bed means he lay back down, quietly. Yielded up the ghost means he breathed out his spirit, and died. Buryingplace, in verse 30, is a burial ground.

Then “was gathered unto his people.” But Jacob's body was still in Egypt. He was buried in Machpelah later (50:13). So this phrase cannot be only about the grave. It speaks of going to where his people already were.

“Now that the dead are raised, even Moses shewed at the bush, when he calleth the Lord the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob. For he is not a God of the dead, but of the living: for all live unto him.”

Luke 20:37-38

Jesus read the phrase the same way: the God of Jacob is not the God of the dead. Jacob is alive to him.

Question 1 of 6

Honesty

Did Jacob say only nice things to his sons?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

No. He told Reuben he would not excel, and told Simeon and Levi their anger was cursed. Yet he called every word a blessing. A truth from someone who loves you is a gift. Find the men who will tell you the truth.

Question 2 of 6

Sin and its cost

What did Reuben's sin cost him?

What verses tell us about this?

“Let Reuben live, and not die; and let not his men be few.”

Deuteronomy 33:6

Answer

His place as firstborn and the double share, which went to Joseph and Judah. Sin has a cost that can last. But Reuben's tribe was allowed to live, and Moses prayed for it. If you carry scars from what you have done, God's answer is still mercy.

Question 3 of 6

Anger

What did Jacob say about Simeon and Levi's anger?

What verses tell us about this?

“For the wrath of man worketh not the righteousness of God.”

James 1:20

Answer

He cursed their anger, not the men, and said they would be scattered. Levi's tribe was scattered, but it later stood on the LORD's side and became his priests. Anger does not have to be the last word about you. Take it to your brothers in Christ before it goes to your hands.

Question 4 of 6

Christ

What did Jacob promise about Judah, and who is Shiloh?

What verses tell us about this?

“For it is evident that our Lord sprang out of Juda; of which tribe Moses spake nothing concerning priesthood.”

Hebrews 7:14

Answer

That the ruler's staff would stay with Judah, until the One to whom it belongs comes, and the peoples gather to him. Shiloh is a person here, not the town. The New Testament says our Lord sprang out of Juda.

Question 5 of 6

Hope

What did Jacob stop and say in the middle of the blessings?

What verses tell us about this?

“It is good that a man should both hope and quietly wait for the salvation of the LORD.”

Lamentations 3:26

Answer

A prayer: “I have waited for thy salvation, O LORD.” The Hebrew for salvation is the root of the name Jesus. Jacob could not save his sons and they could not save themselves. Neither can we. Wait on the LORD, and call on your brothers in Christ and your pastor to wait with you.

Question 6 of 6

Faith

Where did Jacob ask to be buried, and why does it matter?

What verses tell us about this?

“For he is not a God of the dead, but of the living: for all live unto him.”

Luke 20:38

Answer

In the cave of Machpelah, in Canaan, with Abraham, Isaac, and Leah. After seventeen years in Egypt, he still believed God's promise of the land. He wanted his body to rest in it. And the God of Jacob is not the God of the dead: Jacob is alive to him.
