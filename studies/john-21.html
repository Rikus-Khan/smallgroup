Part one

Nothing all night

Verses 1–8

Back on the boat, back in the dark, and the net comes up empty.

Back home in Galilee

The Sea of Tiberias is the Sea of Galilee. It took that name from the city Herod built on its shore in honor of the Roman emperor Tiberius.

The disciples went north because they'd been told to. The angel's message on Easter morning even named Peter:

“But go your way, tell his disciples and Peter that he goeth before you into Galilee: there shall ye see him, as he said unto you.”

Mark 16:7

Seven men are here: Peter, Thomas, Nathanael, James and John, and two more. Most of them had grown up on this water.

Fishermen on this lake worked at night. Fishing wasn't a sin, and they had to eat. But notice who goes back first: Peter, the man who had failed the worst. When a man is carrying shame, he drifts back to the old life he knows.

They did everything they knew how to do, all night long, and caught nothing.

“I am the vine, ye are the branches: He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing.”

John 15:5

“Have ye any meat?”

“Children” here is friendly, like calling out “Lads!” And “meat” just means food. He's asking a question He already knows the answer to.

He wanted them to say it out loud: “No.” Empty hands, honestly admitted. That's where help starts.

Then one command, the same lake and the same net, a few feet over: “Cast the net on the right side.” And the net is suddenly too full to pull in.

This had happened before

Luke 5:5, the day Jesus called Peter

And Simon answering said unto him, Master, we have toiled all the night, and have taken nothing: nevertheless at thy word I will let down the net.

John 21:3, 6, three years later

that night they caught nothing … Cast the net on the right side of the ship, and ye shall find.

At the start, a night of nothing, then a word from Jesus, then more fish than the nets could hold. That was the day He said, “Follow me.” Now it happens again, on purpose.

Jesus is taking Peter back to the beginning. It's as if He's saying, “Remember when I first called you? I still mean it.”

John sees, Peter jumps

John is the first to recognize Him. Peter is the first to get to Him. He'd been stripped down for work, so he threw on his outer coat and went over the side.

This is the same man who had denied Him three times. He didn't hang back and wait to be sure he was welcome. Guilt says, stay away from Him. Peter swam straight toward Him. That's the right instinct.

Part two

Come and dine

Verses 9–14

The risen Lord cooks breakfast for the men who ran.

The second fire of coals

The Greek word for “a fire of coals,” anthrakia, shows up only twice in the whole New Testament:

John 18:18, the night of the denials

And the servants and officers stood there, who had made a fire of coals; for it was cold: and they warmed themselves: and Peter stood with them, and warmed himself.

John 21:9, this morning

As soon then as they were come to land, they saw a fire of coals there, and fish laid thereon, and bread.

The last time Peter stood by a charcoal fire, he swore he didn't know Jesus. Now Jesus has built one Himself. He brings Peter back to the place of his worst failure, not to shame him, but to heal him there.

great fishes

Somebody counted. That's what fishermen do, and it's what an eyewitness remembers. People have guessed at hidden meanings in 153 for centuries. John doesn't give one, so we won't either.

What John does point out: the net didn't break. The first time, “their net brake” (Luke 5:6). This time it held every one. What Jesus gathers, He keeps.

Breakfast is ready

Before they ever landed, He already had fish on the fire and bread ready. He didn't need their catch. He asked them to bring some anyway, and then He served them with His own hands.

This is the risen Son of God, cooking for men who had run out on Him. No speech about where they were that night. Just “Come and dine.”

Part three

Lovest thou me?

Verses 15–19

Three denials by a fire. Three questions by a fire.

One question for every denial

by a fire, John 18“I am not.” 18:17“I am not.” 18:25“Peter then denied again” 18:27
answeredansweredanswered
by a fire, John 21“Lovest thou me?” 21:15“Lovest thou me?” 21:16“Lovest thou me?” 21:17

Jesus didn't pretend the denials never happened. He didn't make Peter grovel either. He gave him three chances to say out loud what he'd once denied out loud. Peter was hurt by the third question, because he knew exactly what it matched.

“And Peter remembered the word of Jesus, which said unto him, Before the cock crow, thou shalt deny me thrice. And he went out, and wept bitterly.”

Matthew 26:75

Two words for love

English has one word here. The Greek has two, agapaō and phileō, and John uses both:

1st · v.152nd · v.163rd · v.17 Jesus asksagapaōagapaōphileō Peter saysphileōphileōphileō Jesus saysFeed my lambsFeed my sheepFeed my sheep

Many teachers see something here: Jesus asks for the highest love, and the humbled Peter only claims to love Him as a dear friend, until Jesus comes down to Peter's word. Others point out that John uses the two words interchangeably elsewhere (both are used of the Father loving the Son, 3:35 and 5:20). Be careful not to build too much on it.

What's certain is in the text: three questions, three answers, three charges. And “more than these” recalls Peter's old boast: “Though all men shall be offended because of thee, yet will I never be offended” (Matthew 26:33). No more boasting now. Just, “Lord, thou knowest all things.”

Peter's road down and back up

Jesus didn't just forgive Peter and leave him on the bench. He handed him a job: take care of My people. Years later Peter passed the same charge on:

“Feed the flock of God which is among you, taking the oversight thereof, not by constraint, but willingly; not for filthy lucre, but of a ready mind;”

1 Peter 5:2

Your worst failure doesn't have to be the end of your story. A man who's been restored is often the one best fit to help the next man up.

“Follow me,” again

“Stretch forth thy hands … another shall gird thee” points to Peter's own death. Early church writers say he was crucified in Rome, about thirty years later. Peter knew it was coming:

“Knowing that shortly I must put off this my tabernacle, even as our Lord Jesus Christ hath shewed me.”

2 Peter 1:14

Matthew 4:19, by this same sea, at the start

And he saith unto them, Follow me, and I will make you fishers of men.

John 21:19, by this same sea, at the end

This spake he, signifying by what death he should glorify God. And when he had spoken this, he saith unto him, Follow me.

The first “Follow me” was an invitation. This one comes after Peter knows his own weakness and knows where the road leads. Same two words. A man who has failed and been forgiven hears them differently.

Part four

What is that to thee?

Verses 20–25

Keep your eyes on your own road.

“And what shall this man do?”

Peter has just heard hard news about his own future, and the first thing he does is look over at John. What about him?

Peter

a hard road, ending at a cross

By early church tradition, he died for Christ in Rome, about A.D. 64–68.

John

a long road, into old age

By tradition, he outlived the other apostles and died an old man, around A.D. 100.

Two different roads, and both men were faithful. Jesus' answer is short: “What is that to thee? follow thou me.”

“But let every man prove his own work, and then shall he have rejoicing in himself alone, and not in another.”

Galatians 6:4

In recovery it's easy to look around the room: his life came together faster, his family took him back, he got the job. Jesus didn't call you to his road. He called you to follow Him on yours.

Read what He actually said

A rumor spread among the believers that John would never die. John sets the record straight:

  • “that disciple should not die”what people said He said
  • “If I will that he tarry till I come, what is that to thee?”what He actually said

One small word, “if,” changed everything. A lot of confusion about God comes from repeating what somebody said the Bible says. Go read it for yourself.

An eyewitness wrote this

The man who leaned on Jesus' chest at the Last Supper, who stood at the cross, who ran to the tomb and saw the grave clothes, wrote this book. He wasn't passing on rumors. He was there. He says so again in one of his letters:

“That which was from the beginning, which we have heard, which we have seen with our eyes, which we have looked upon, and our hands have handled, of the Word of life;”

1 John 1:1
John

And this is only a small part. If everything Jesus did were written down, John says, the world couldn't hold the books. We have what we need. There's always more of Him to know.

The end of John's Gospel

Twenty-one chapters. It opened with “In the beginning was the Word.” It ends with a fisherman on a beach being told, “Follow thou me.” In between: seven signs, seven “I am” sayings, a cross, and an empty tomb.

“But these are written, that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing ye might have life through his name.”

John 20:31

If you've believed, you have life in His name, and nothing can take it from you. All that's left is what He told Peter. Follow thou me. Today, and tomorrow, and the day after that.

Question 1 of 6

Going back

What happened when the disciples went back to their old work?

What verses tell us about this?

“I am the vine, ye are the branches: He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing.”

John 15:5

Answer

They worked all night and caught nothing. When Jesus asked, they had to admit it: “No.” Then one word from Him filled the net to bursting. The old life always comes up empty in the end. What we can't do on our own, He can do in a moment.

Question 2 of 6

Grace

How did Jesus treat the men who had let Him down?

What verses tell us about this?

“It is of the LORD’S mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness.”

Lamentations 3:22–23

Answer

He fed them. He had a fire going and breakfast cooking before they reached shore, and He served them with His own hands. No lecture about the night they ran. His mercies are new every morning, and He still says to failures, “Come and dine.”

Question 3 of 6

Restoration

How did Jesus restore Peter after his three denials?

What verses tell us about this?

“If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness.”

1 John 1:9

Answer

By a fire, just like the one where Peter denied Him, Jesus asked him three times, “Lovest thou me?” One question for each denial. It hurt, but it healed. Then He gave Peter work to do. Jesus doesn't sweep failure under the rug. He brings it into the open and forgives it completely.

Question 4 of 6

Love

What does Jesus ask of a man who loves Him?

What verses tell us about this?

“Feed the flock of God which is among you, taking the oversight thereof, not by constraint, but willingly; not for filthy lucre, but of a ready mind;”

1 Peter 5:2

Answer

Every time Peter said, “I love thee,” Jesus answered with a job: feed My lambs, feed My sheep. Love for Jesus isn't just a feeling. It shows up as taking care of His people. Peter did it for the rest of his life.

Question 5 of 6

Comparison

What should we do when we start comparing ourselves to others?

What verses tell us about this?

“But let every man prove his own work, and then shall he have rejoicing in himself alone, and not in another.”

Galatians 6:4

Answer

Stop looking sideways and look at Jesus. Peter wanted to know about John's future, and Jesus told him it wasn't his business: “Follow thou me.” God writes a different story for every man. Your job is to follow Him on the road He's given you.

Question 6 of 6

The Word

Why can we trust what John wrote?

What verses tell us about this?

“That which was from the beginning, which we have heard, which we have seen with our eyes, which we have looked upon, and our hands have handled, of the Word of life;”

1 John 1:1

“But these are written, that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing ye might have life through his name.”

John 20:31

Answer

Because the man who wrote it was there. John saw the signs, stood at the cross, ran to the empty tomb, and ate breakfast with the risen Lord on this beach. He wrote down what he saw so that we would believe and have life.
