Genesis 37

A coat, a pit, and a lie

A father's favourite, a dream his brothers hate, and a pit at Dothan. Eleven men who sell their own brother and then lie to their father for twenty years. It is the darkest day of Joseph's life, and God is already at work in it.

Part one

The coat and the dreams

Verses 1–11

A father's favourite, and two dreams his brothers cannot forgive.

Jacob (also called Israel) had twelve sons by four women. Joseph is seventeen, and he works with “the sons of Bilhah, and the sons of Zilpah”, his half-brothers. Rachel, Joseph's mother, had died years before, in childbirth (35:19). Joseph is the oldest son of the wife Jacob loved best.

“Their evil report” does not say what the brothers did, or whether Joseph was right to tell. It only tells us that he carried tales to his father, and that this family was already split into camps.

A coat that said it out loud

What the brothers wore

Work clothes

Short tunics, made for the flock and the field. Dust, sweat, and sheep.

What Joseph wore

A coat of many colours

A coat of many colours: a robe of honour, not for work. Every day they saw it, they saw who their father loved.

Jacob should have known better. He had grown up in a house split down the middle: “Isaac loved Esau... but Rebekah loved Jacob” (25:28). That favouritism cost him twenty years away from home, and a brother who wanted to kill him. Now he does the same thing to his own sons.

Fathers hand down their wounds. If you grew up as the one who was passed over, or the one who was favoured, you know it. And if you are a father, this is a warning: your sons can tell.

“Could not speak peaceably”

שָׁלוֹם said shalom “peaceably”: the common Hebrew word for peace, and for “hello”

Read it again: they “could not speak shalom unto him”. They could not even say good morning. Hatred in a family often starts exactly like that: not a fight, just a silence at the table, and words that stop coming.

There is no fight yet in verse 4. That is the warning. This is what it looks like early.

Eleven sheaves bow to one

Joseph tells the dream plainly: grain was being tied in bundles, “sheaves”, and his bundle stood up while theirs “made obeisance” (bowed low) to it. His brothers understood it at once: Shalt thou reign over us?

By chapter 42 the dream has plainly come true (42:9). But at seventeen Joseph told it with no care for how it would land, and they hated him “for his dreams, and for his words.” A man can be handed something real by God and still need years of humbling before he can carry it well. Joseph is about to get them.

Two ways to hear the same dream

The brothers

They envied him

They heard only what it took from them. They looked at Joseph's good fortune and counted it against themselves.

His father

He observed the saying

Jacob scolded the boy, but he kept the dream in his mind (“observed” means kept, watched). He knew God sends dreams. He waited to see.

“A sound heart is the life of the flesh: but envy the rottenness of the bones.”

Proverbs 14:30

“For where envying and strife is, there is confusion and every evil work.”

James 3:16

Envy is hatred's seed. It looks at a brother's good and calls it a theft. If there is a man you cannot stand to see do well, name him to God today, and pray for him by name. Envy dies in the light.

Part two

Dothan, and the pit

Verses 12–24

A father sends his son to check on his brothers, and he never comes home.

“Here am I”

הִנֵּנִי said hinneni “Here am I”: I'm ready, I'm available, send me

Joseph knew how his brothers felt. Even so, when his father said go and see if it is well with your brothers, he did not argue. He said what Abraham said to God (22:1): Here am I. The trip from Hebron to Shechem was a long way on foot, around fifty miles.

Jacob had no idea. A father sent a son to check on his brothers' welfare, and the son went. It is a picture of obedience at the worst possible time, and it is also where a hard road starts.

Hebron, Shechem, Dothan, Egypt

Joseph walks from Hebron up to Shechem and finds nobody. Then comes the line the whole story turns on: “a certain man found him... wandering in the field.”

A stranger, with no name and no other part in the Bible. He asks What seekest thou? and tells him which way they went. Had he been elsewhere, Joseph would have walked home. The same road that leads to the pit leads to the palace, and God uses a passing stranger to put him on it. The red line shows the rest: the caravan to Egypt.

How hatred grows

  1. a father's favouritethe coat (v. 3)
  2. they hated himand “yet the more” (v. 4, 5, 8)
  3. could not speak peaceablysilence at the table (v. 4)
  4. conspired to slay him“afar off”, before he even arrived (v. 18)
  5. a cover story ready“Some evil beast hath devoured him” (v. 20)

“Whosoever hateth his brother is a murderer: and ye know that no murderer hath eternal life abiding in him.”

1 John 3:15

Nobody wakes up and plans a murder. It begins with a feeling, nursed for years. The way out is on the first step, not the fourth: say it to God, and to a brother or your pastor, before it grows teeth.

And look at verse 20: “we shall see what will become of his dreams.” They think they can end God's plan with a pit. Keep that line in mind to the end of the book.

Reuben: better than the rest, and still not enough

The brothersLet's kill him. Say a wild animal got him.

ReubenDon't shed blood. Throw him in this pit. a secret plan

Reuben is the oldest, and he does something good: he stops the murder. Credit that. But he does it by cleverness, not courage. He never says “No, he is our brother, and we will not touch him.” He keeps the peace in the group and plans to slip back later and get Joseph out (v. 22).

Then he leaves, and while he is gone, the others sell Joseph (v. 29). A secret good intention is not the same as standing up. When your friends are doing wrong, the man who says no, not me, out loud, in front of them, is the one who can change the room.

Stripped

  1. v. 3The coat is givena father's love

  3. v. 31It is dipped in a goat's bloodto make a lie
  4. v. 32It is carried home“know now whether it be thy son's”

The pit was “empty, there was no water in it.” A dry cistern, a deep hole cut in the rock for storing rain, with a narrow neck at the top and no way out without help. Joseph is thrown into it with nothing: no coat, no water, and brothers sitting above him.

We are not told yet what Joseph said from the bottom. We will be, twenty years later, from the brothers' own mouths.

Part three

Twenty pieces of silver

Verses 25–36

They sat down to eat, and a caravan came by.

“And they sat down to eat bread”

Read that line slowly. Their brother is in a hole a few feet away, and they sit down to lunch. That is what a hardened conscience looks like: not a monster, just a man who has learned to eat while it happens.

“And they said one to another, We are verily guilty concerning our brother, in that we saw the anguish of his soul, when he besought us, and we would not hear; therefore is this distress come upon us.”

Genesis 42:21

There it is, more than twenty years later. They had heard him begging, “the anguish of his soul”, and they had not listened, and then they ate. The conscience went quiet; it did not die. When trouble came, it woke up and told them exactly what they had done.

If there is something you did and have buried, it is still down there. Dug up in the light, with a brother or your pastor, it can be forgiven. Left buried, it comes up on its own, usually at the worst moment.

The caravan

  1. Ishmeelitessons of Ishmael, Abraham's first son (Genesis 16), travelling traders
  2. Midianitessons of Midian, Abraham's son by Keturah (25:2). The text uses both names for the same company.
  3. spiceryspices; gum from desert shrubs, burned as incense
  4. balma healing resin, famous from Gilead
  5. myrrha sweet-smelling resin, used for perfume and burial
  6. merchantmentraders

Egypt wanted these goods for medicine, perfume and embalming, so this was a regular trade route. Abraham's sons by other mothers, Ishmael's and Midian's families, are the ones who buy Joseph. His own cousins carry him down to Egypt.

One more thing. “Twenty pieces of silver” was not coins. Silver was weighed out. When the law was written later, Leviticus 27:5 set the value of a boy between five and twenty at twenty shekels of silver. Joseph was sold for about the price of a boy.

“What profit is it?”

JudahWhat profit is it if we slay our brother, and conceal his blood?

The same Judah, one verse laterCome, let us sell him. He is our brother and our flesh. and they were content

Judah talks his brothers out of murder. And in the same breath he turns the sin into business: what is there in it for us? Mercy and profit in one sentence. He has found a way to sell his own brother and still call himself kinder than the others.

Hold on to this man. In Genesis 38 he falls hard, and later in the book he is the one who steps forward and offers to be a slave in place of another brother (44:33). Judah's story is the story of what grace does to a man.

His name in Greek is Judas. Another man of that name sold a Man for silver. The difference is where the story goes next.

A kid, a coat, and a blind old man

Jacob's own youth

“And she put the skins of the kids of the goats upon his hands, and upon the smooth of his neck:”

Genesis 27:16

His sons, years later

“And they took Joseph’s coat, and killed a kid of the goats, and dipped the coat in the blood;”

Genesis 37:31

The Bible does not draw the line for us, but it is hard to miss. Jacob once used a goat to deceive his old father about his son. Now a goat is used to deceive Jacob about his son. “Be not deceived; God is not mocked: for whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap” (Galatians 6:7).

Notice how the lie is told. “This have we found”: with a straight face and a piece of true evidence. And they say thy son's coat, not our brother's. They have already cut him off in their own mouths.

Jacob, the old deceiver, is deceived. Even so, “he refused to be comforted”, and he mourned a loss that was not real. A lie can make a family grieve for something that is not even true.

What a lie leaves behind

  1. a fatherin sackcloth, mourning “many days”
  2. ten sonswho “rose up to comfort him”, the very men who knew
  3. a consciencethat woke up twenty years later (42:21)
  4. a brotherwho was not dead at all

Look at verse 35: all his sons stand around their father's bed and try to comfort him. They watched him weep for years and never said a word. One lie, and every day after that was spent keeping it. That is the real price of covering your sin: you have to keep paying.

“He that covereth his sins shall not prosper: but whoso confesseth and forsaketh them shall have mercy.”

Proverbs 28:13

“He refused to be comforted”

“I will go down into the grave unto my son mourning.” Jacob's grief is real, and the Bible does not scold him for it. It gives it to us as it was. A man who has lost a child, a marriage, a job, or the man he used to be may say the same thing: I will carry this to the grave.

“The LORD is nigh unto them that are of a broken heart; and saveth such as be of a contrite spirit.”

Psalm 34:18

Grief that will not be comforted is the time not to sit alone. Jacob had sons and daughters standing around him, and he turned them away. Do not do that. When the heaviness comes, call or text your brothers in Christ, and your pastor, the same day. You are part of a company of men fighting the same enemy, and none of us was meant to carry this by ourselves.

Jacob also had it wrong. Joseph was not dead. The hardest day of his life was built on something that was not true, and the LORD was already moving.

Did God lose track of Joseph?

Look at how chapter 37 ends: “the Midianites sold him into Egypt unto Potiphar.” The caravan came by at the very moment the brothers needed one, and it was going the one direction that would put Joseph where God needed him. Joseph's brothers meant it to end his dreams. The Bible's later verdict is very different.

“He sent a man before them, even Joseph, who was sold for a servant: Whose feet they hurt with fetters: he was laid in iron: Until the time that his word came: the word of the LORD tried him.”

Psalm 105:17-19

“But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive.”

Genesis 50:20

“He sent a man before them.” Every step Joseph took against his will, God had already planned. The pit was not the end of the dreams. It was the first stage of the road to them.

Thirteen years from the pit to the palace, and about twenty-two from the pit to the day his brothers finally bowed to him. God does not tell you the schedule. He only promises that he is the One keeping it.

If you are in a long stretch right now (a job you did not want, a debt, a court date, a family that will not speak to you), the LORD can do in it what he did for Joseph: get you ready.

Question 1 of 6

Family wounds

What did Jacob's favouritism do to his sons?

What verses tell us about this?

“And Isaac loved Esau, because he did eat of his venison: but Rebekah loved Jacob.”

Genesis 25:28

Answer

It turned ten sons into men who hated their brother, so much that they “could not speak peaceably unto him”. Jacob knew how favouritism feels, because he grew up in it. He did it anyway. Love your children evenly, and where a father hurt you, let it stop with you.

Question 2 of 6

Sin

Where does hatred between brothers lead?

What verses tell us about this?

“Whosoever hateth his brother is a murderer: and ye know that no murderer hath eternal life abiding in him.”

1 John 3:15

Answer

Toward murder, one small step at a time. Envy, hatred, silence, a plot. They never killed Joseph with their hands, but they sold him, and God counts the hatred behind it as serious. The time to stop is on the first step. Name envy to God and a brother before it grows.

Question 3 of 6

Half-measures

Who spoke up for Joseph, and was it enough?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Reuben and Judah both did something. Reuben stopped the murder, but with a secret plan, and Joseph was sold while he was gone. Judah talked them out of killing him, then sold him for silver. Better than the worst is not right. Say no out loud, to the group.

Question 4 of 6

Regret

What does a lie leave a family carrying?

What verses tell us about this?

“And they said one to another, We are verily guilty concerning our brother, in that we saw the anguish of his soul, when he besought us, and we would not hear; therefore is this distress come upon us.”

Genesis 42:21

Answer

A father in mourning for years, brothers who watched him and said nothing, and a conscience that woke up twenty years later. A lie has to be kept up every day, and it still comes out. Confessed, it can be forgiven (Proverbs 28:13). Covered, it keeps costing.

Question 5 of 6

Grief

What should a man do when he cannot be comforted?

What verses tell us about this?

“The LORD is nigh unto them that are of a broken heart; and saveth such as be of a contrite spirit.”

Psalm 34:18

Answer

Not what Jacob did. He turned away the family around him and said he would carry it to the grave. The LORD is near the broken-hearted, and he often comes through other men. When heaviness settles on you, call or text your brothers in Christ, and your pastor, the same day.

Question 6 of 6

Hope

Did God lose track of Joseph in the pit?

What verses tell us about this?

“He sent a man before them, even Joseph, who was sold for a servant: Whose feet they hurt with fetters: he was laid in iron: Until the time that his word came: the word of the LORD tried him.”

Psalm 105:17-19

“But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive.”

Genesis 50:20

Answer

No. The caravan came at the right moment, going the right way, and “he sent a man before them.” The brothers meant the pit to end his dreams, and God used it as the first step toward them. If you are in a pit today, it does not mean you were forgotten.
