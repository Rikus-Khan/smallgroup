1 Corinthians 10

A warning, a way out, and a table

Israel was saved out of Egypt and still fell in the wilderness. Paul says their story is a mirror for us, and promises that no temptation ever comes without a door out.

Paul wrote this to the church in Corinth, from Ephesus, about AD 55. Chapters 8 to 10 answer one question: may a believer eat meat that was offered to an idol? Chapter 9 ended with Paul running his race and saying he could be disqualified. Chapter 10 picks that up.

It opens with a warning from Israel's history, gives the best promise about temptation in the Bible, and finishes at two tables: the Lord's, and the idol's.

Part one

Our fathers in the wilderness

Verses 1–13

They were rescued, fed and guided, and most of them still fell.

All, all, all, all

“Our fathers” are the Israelites Moses led out of Egypt, about 1,400 years before Paul (about 1450 BC). Count how many times Paul says all.

  1. Allunder the cloudGod's presence leading them
  2. Allthrough the seathe Red Sea, on dry ground
  3. Allbaptized unto Mosesjoined to their leader
  4. Allate the same foodmanna, from heaven
  5. Alldrank the same drinkwater from the rock

“Baptized unto Moses” means they were joined to him as their leader, going down into the sea and up the other side. It pictures how a believer's baptism joins him to Christ. But the water didn't save them, and being a part of the crowd didn't keep them: God “was not well pleased” with most of them. Going through the motions of religion has never been the same as walking with God.

The Rock that followed them

In the desert there was no water, and Moses struck a rock at Horeb and water poured out (Exodus 17). Paul says the supply never left them: it went where they went. And that Rock, Paul says, was Christ.

So Christ was there in the wilderness, giving the thirsty a drink they'd never earn. The rock was struck once; Christ was struck once for sin. Now He stands and says “If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink” (John 7:37).

One camp. All of them blessed.

Overthrown in the wilderness

God had freed them from slavery. Yet they kept longing for Egypt's food and refused to trust Him at the edge of the promised land. So that whole generation died in the desert, short of the land, all but two.

“Doubtless ye shall not come into the land… save Caleb the son of Jephunneh, and Joshua the son of Nun.” (Numbers 14:30)

“Lust” means a strong craving. They were free, and the old life still looked better from the desert.

What this does and doesn't mean

It doesn't mean

a saved man is un-saved

They were already out of Egypt, delivered by blood on the doorposts. What they lost in the wilderness was the land, the rest, and years of their lives. Jesus says of His own, “they shall never perish” (John 10:28).

It does mean

sin costs a believer plenty

The Father disciplines the sons He loves. It can cost health, years, family, a clear conscience, and usefulness. Not His love, and not your place in His family.

“But when we are judged, we are chastened of the Lord, that we should not be condemned with the world.”

1 Corinthians 11:32

If you've fallen, take this as the opposite of a death sentence: a Father who corrects you is a Father who still owns you.

Four ways they fell

Verse 7 · the golden calf

Idolatry

They made an idol, feasted before it, and “rose up to play” (Exodus 32:6).

Today: whatever you run to for comfort in place of God.

Verse 8 · Baal-peor

Fornication

Israel's men were drawn into sex and idol feasts with Moab's women. 23,000 fell in a day.

Today: sex outside marriage, and everything that leads to it.

Verse 9 · the serpents

Tempting Christ

They tested God's patience and complained He hadn't done enough. Snakes came.

Today: seeing how close to the edge you can stand.

Verse 10 · Korah's rebellion

Murmuring

Constant grumbling against God and the men He put over them. “The destroyer” came.

Today: bitter talk that poisons the room.

Paul's “23,000” counts those who fell in one day; Numbers 25:9 gives 24,000 for the whole plague. Careful readers have long known they describe the same event in two ways.

Take heed

Verse 11: these things are written “for our admonition”, as warnings. They're not there to shame anybody. They show us where other people went wrong, so we don't have to learn it the hard way.

Then the sharp one: the man most in danger is the man who is sure he can't fall. “Take heed” means watch out: step back from the edge while you still can. Knowing you could fall is the first defense you have.

Four promises in one verse

  1. It's common to man
    You aren't the only one, and you aren't the first. Other men have faced exactly this and come through.
  2. God is faithful
    He stays on your side, even on the day you were not on His.
  3. Never above what you're able
    He sets the limit on the pressure.
  4. A way to escape, with it
    The way out comes with the temptation, at the same time, not afterward.

A door in the wall

God doesn't promise there'll be no walls. He promises there's a door in them. The Greek word is ekbasis: a way out, like a pass through a mountain.

The temptation is real. At that moment, it feels like the walls are shutting in and there's only one thing left to do.

It isn't true. God has already put a door in that wall. “Faithful is he.” He doesn't just say it exists; the Spirit lives in you now and will show it to you.

Your part is to walk through it. Pick up the phone. Leave the room. Walk out of the place. Tell somebody. Do one thing today: write down your door ahead of time, the name you'll call and where you'll go, while the walls are still far off.

He knows what it is to be tempted

“For in that he himself hath suffered being tempted, he is able to succour them that are tempted.”

Hebrews 2:18

“Succour” means run to help. Jesus was tempted in every way, and didn't give in. He isn't looking down from a distance. He knows exactly how heavy it is.

And if you've already gone through the wall the wrong way, again, the door hasn't been bricked up. It's open this morning, and so is He. Confess it, and take the next step through it.

Part two

Flee, and the table

Verses 14–22

Some things you stand and fight. This one you run from.

Flee from idolatry

An idol is whatever you serve in the place of God, the thing you go to for comfort, courage or escape. In Corinth it was a stone god at a feast. For us it might be a bottle, a woman, money or a screen.

Notice the word. Not resist, not reason with. Flee. When Potiphar's wife grabbed Joseph, “he left his garment in her hand, and fled” (Genesis 39:12). He lost the coat and kept his life. And to Timothy: “Flee also youthful lusts” (2 Timothy 2:22).

Running isn't weakness. Everyone who gets free has to learn it: some things you don't fight, you leave.

The cup and the bread

In verse 16 Paul calls the Lord's Supper a communion. The Greek is koinōnia: sharing together, fellowship. It's a reminder, not a magic act: the bread and cup stay bread and cup, and don't save anyone.

“And when he had given thanks, he brake it, and said, Take, eat: this is my body, which is broken for you: this do in remembrance of me.”

1 Corinthians 11:24

So it does two things: it remembers what Christ did for you, and it joins you to everyone else at the table. One loaf, many men: “we being many are one bread.” A man with nobody around him needs this table.

Two tables

Eating the sacrifice made Israel partners with the altar (verse 18). It's the same here. An idol is nothing (8:4), but Paul says the powers behind it are real: “they sacrifice to devils”, meaning demons. A believer who joined the feast was sitting at their table.

“Ye cannot drink the cup of the Lord, and the cup of devils.” You can't be at both. If you've spent years at the other table, with the old crowd and the old routine, hear it plainly: you can't keep a seat there and be fed here.

Verse 22 asks, “are we stronger than he?” If we make Him jealous, we'll lose. But He holds you, and He'll set you a place at the better table.

Part three

To the glory of God

Verses 23–33

What about the meat itself, and the dinner invitations? Paul sorts it out, and lands on a verse for every day.

Three questions before I do it

  1. “All things are lawful for me”
  2. “but all things are not expedient”
  3. “all things edify not”

Lawful means I'm allowed. Expedient means it actually helps. Edify means it builds someone up. Paul said much the same in 6:12.

This isn't a license. A man in recovery can say “nobody can stop me” about a hundred things and still be walking back under their power. Ask the second and third questions too: does it help me, and does it build up the people around me?

“Let no man seek his own, but every man another's wealth”: “wealth” here means good. Look out for the next man as well as yourself.

“The earth is the LORD’S, and the fulness thereof; the world, and they that dwell therein.”

Psalm 24:1

Meat from the market

The shambles was the meat market. A lot of its meat had come from temple sacrifices and was sold on. Paul says: buy it, eat it, and don't quiz the butcher. The food itself is the Lord's, as Psalm 24 says, and it can't be spoiled by an idol that's nothing.

The line is between eating a steak and sitting at an idol's feast (verses 14–22). A feast is worship. A shop is only food.

The one who spoke up has a tender conscience, and your plate isn't worth tripping him over. Verses 29 and 30 raise the obvious complaint: why should another man's conscience limit my liberty? Paul's answer is in the next verses. He isn't trying to win the argument. He's aiming at God's glory and other people's good.

Paul is talking about idol meat, but the principle carries over. A man in recovery can ask the same about any invitation: will this table pull me back or push someone else back? You don't owe anyone an apology for saying no.

An ordinary day

  1. Wake upanother day given
  2. Eat“whether ye eat”
  3. Work“whatsoever ye do”
  4. Drink“or drink”: water, coffee, thanks
  5. Eveninghome, family, the phone call
  6. Bedhonest before God

Glory isn't only for church. A man does his job honestly, shows up on time, tells the truth, and doesn't take the drink he'd have taken last year. That's all done “to the glory of God.”

Verses 32 and 33 add the aim. “Give none offence”, neither to the Jews, nor to the Gentiles, nor to the church. The same pattern as chapter 9: a life lived so that others “may be saved.”

Points to remember

  1. Examples“These things were our examples” 10:6Their failures are warnings, not shame. Learn from them so you don't have to learn the hard way.
  2. The Rock“That Rock was Christ” 10:4Christ was with them in the wilderness, giving water they'd never earn. He gives it to you too.
  3. Falling“Let him that thinketh he standeth take heed lest he fall” 10:12The man who is sure he can't fall is the man in danger. Step back from the edge.
  4. Temptation“God is faithful… will with the temptation also make a way to escape” 10:13It's common, it's limited, and there's always a door. Write down yours today.
  5. Two tables“Ye cannot drink the cup of the Lord, and the cup of devils” 10:21Flee the old table. The Lord's table is a memorial, and a fellowship with Him and His people.
  6. Glory“Whether therefore ye eat, or drink, or whatsoever ye do, do all to the glory of God” 10:31Every ordinary hour of today can be worship.

Question 1 of 6

Examples

Why did God put Israel's failures in the Bible?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

As examples and warnings for us. Israel was rescued, fed and guided, and still fell into craving, idols, sex, testing God and complaining. Their story shows where those roads lead, so we can turn off them early.

Question 2 of 6

The Rock

Who was the Rock in the wilderness?

What verses tell us about this?

“Behold, I will stand before thee there upon the rock in Horeb; and thou shalt smite the rock, and there shall come water out of it, that the people may drink.”

Exodus 17:6

“In the last day, that great day of the feast, Jesus stood and cried, saying, If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink.”

John 7:37

Answer

Christ. Paul says the spiritual Rock that gave Israel water in the desert was Christ Himself. He was there, supplying them, and He still says to the thirsty, “Let him come unto me, and drink.”

Question 3 of 6

Falling

What does “let him that thinketh he standeth take heed” mean?

What verses tell us about this?

“For whom the Lord loveth he chasteneth, and scourgeth every son whom he receiveth.”

Hebrews 12:6

“But when we are judged, we are chastened of the Lord, that we should not be condemned with the world.”

1 Corinthians 11:32

Answer

The man most in danger is the one who is sure he is safe. “Take heed” means watch out and step back from the edge. A believer who falls isn't thrown out of the family; he's disciplined by a Father who loves him, but the fall is still costly.

Question 4 of 6

Temptation

What does God promise when I'm tempted?

What verses tell us about this?

“For in that he himself hath suffered being tempted, he is able to succour them that are tempted.”

Hebrews 2:18

“And she caught him by his garment, saying, Lie with me: and he left his garment in her hand, and fled, and got him out.”

Genesis 39:12

Answer

Four things: it's common to man, God is faithful, He won't let it go beyond what you can bear, and with the temptation He makes a way to escape. Jesus was tempted too, and He runs to help. Your part is to take the way out, like Joseph, who ran.

Question 5 of 6

Two tables

Why can't we sit at the Lord's table and the table of devils?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Because each table is a fellowship, and the powers behind idols are real. The Lord's Supper remembers Christ and joins His people together; it can't be mixed with a seat at the old table. So flee idolatry and the places and company that go with it.

Question 6 of 6

Glory

How do I live “to the glory of God” on an ordinary day?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Whether you eat, or drink, or whatever you do: in honest work, plain truth, and in doing right by the people around you. Every ordinary hour can be worship, aimed at helping others be saved.
