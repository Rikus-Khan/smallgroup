Part one

A new governor, an old plot

Verses 1–12

Festus has barely unpacked before Paul's enemies are at his door.

The same hatred, two years later

Paul has been held at Caesarea for two years. Now there is a new governor, Festus, and the high priest and the leaders go straight to him.

The request sounds polite: send Paul up to us. The plan is the old one, “laying wait in the way to kill him.” Jerusalem is about sixty miles away.

Two years didn't cool it. A grudge feeds on itself, and a man who won't forgive is chained too.

Two years in a Roman cell

  1. about AD 57Paul arrested in the temple at Jerusalem (Acts 21)
  2. about AD 57–59Two years held at Caesarea under Felix, who “left Paul bound” (24:27)
  3. about AD 59Festus replaces Felix. Paul's case starts over
  4. soon afterPaul appeals to Caesar
  5. about AD 59–60The voyage, the shipwreck, and Rome (Acts 27–28)

Two years is a long time to sit in a cell with nothing proved against you. Paul had every reason to be bitter, and every reason to think God had forgotten him.

But God had spoken to him in the middle of the trouble: “Be of good cheer, Paul: for as thou hast testified of me in Jerusalem, so must thou bear witness also at Rome” (23:11). The waiting was not wasted. It was how he got there.

Three charges, no proof

  • “against the law of the Jews”he lived as a Jew and kept to the faith of Moses
  • “against the temple”he was in the temple to worship when they seized him
  • “against Caesar”he had stirred up no revolt against Rome
  • “many and grievous complaints… which they could not prove”loud, serious, and empty

A man shouting a charge isn't the same as a man proving it. Paul answered calmly, with the truth. That's what a clear conscience does: you don't have to shout when you have nothing to hide.

A favour, and its cost

FestusWilt thou go up to Jerusalem, and there be judged of these things before me?

PaulI refuse not to die: but if there be none of these things whereof these accuse me, no man may deliver me unto them. I appeal unto Caesar.
not on that road

Festus wasn't wicked, only weak. Felix had done the same (24:27). “The fear of man bringeth a snare” (Proverbs 29:25).

Paul owned what he'd face if truly guilty: “I refuse not to die.” And he refused to be handed over for what he hadn't done. Face what you really owe. Don't take on what you don't.

A Roman's right

Paul was born a Roman citizen (22:28). A citizen could appeal to the emperor himself, Nero, and the governor had to send him to Rome.

Festus took advice, then answered: “Hast thou appealed unto Caesar? unto Caesar shalt thou go.” Paul used the law that protected him, and God used it to put him on the road to Rome.

Part two

A king comes to town

Verses 13–27

Festus doesn't know what to write about Paul, so he asks a king.

The Herods: four generations meet the gospel

“Herod” was a family name, a bit like “Caesar.” This family ruled bits of the Jewish homeland for more than a hundred years, and every generation met the gospel and had to decide. Today it's the last one's turn.

The whole case in one sentence

“One Jesus, which was dead, whom Paul affirmed to be alive.”

Festus is a Roman lawyer. He sat through all that shouting and boiled it down to a single question. It was not about the temple or the law or Caesar. It was whether a man who died on a cross is alive.

He calls it “superstition,” which in a Roman's mouth just means religion. He has no idea he has just stated the heart of the gospel.

That's still the question for every man, whatever he has done: is Jesus alive? If He is, a man's past is not the last word on him.

Great pomp, and one man in chains

Agrippa and Bernice→robes, officers, “great pomp”The most important people in the room, by every ordinary measure.
Paul→a prisoner with one message“he is a chosen vessel unto me, to bear my name before the Gentiles, and kings” (9:15).

Jesus said that on the Damascus road, about twenty-five years before. Now it comes true: Paul stands before a king, in a chain, and the king has to listen.

Festus calls the emperor “my lord.” Paul has another Lord. Every man in that room will answer to Him, whoever wore the robes.

Points to remember

  1. Hatred“laying wait in the way to kill him” 25:3Some hatred never cools. Don't answer it with your own. Leave it with God.
  2. The truth“whom Paul affirmed to be alive” 25:19When the noise is stripped away, the question is whether Jesus is alive. He is.
  3. A clear conscience“I refuse not to die” 25:11Own what you really did. Don't take the blame for what you didn't. Then stand firm.
  4. Waiting“so must thou bear witness also at Rome” 23:11God was working through the waiting. He keeps His word, even through a locked cell.
  5. Open doors“I would also hear the man myself” 25:22God put the gospel in front of a king through a prisoner. He can use your hard place too.

Question 1 of 5

Hatred

What did the men who hated Paul want?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

They wanted him dead. Two years on, they were still asking for Paul to be sent to Jerusalem so they could kill him on the way. Hatred like that doesn't fade with time. Paul didn't hit back. He trusted God and used the law.

Question 2 of 5

The truth

What did Festus find when he looked at the charges?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Nothing he could use. The complaints were loud and could not be proved. Underneath them was one question: a man named Jesus, who died, and whom Paul said is alive. Festus said it himself: Paul had done nothing worthy of death.

Question 3 of 5

A clear conscience

How could Paul stand firm before his accusers?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

He had nothing to hide on these charges, and he was honest about the rest: if he were truly guilty, he would take it. A man who tells the truth, owns what is his, and refuses what isn't, can stand firm. For what Paul did before he met Jesus, he stood forgiven, as we will see in the next chapter.

Question 4 of 5

Waiting

What was God doing in Paul's long wait?

What verses tell us about this?

“And the night following the Lord stood by him, and said, Be of good cheer, Paul: for as thou hast testified of me in Jerusalem, so must thou bear witness also at Rome.”

Acts 23:11

Answer

Keeping His word. God had promised Paul he would reach Rome. Two years in a cell looked like a delay, but each step, a new governor, an appeal, a Roman escort, was part of the road. When God's plan feels stuck, it may be on its way.

Question 5 of 5

Open doors

Who ended up hearing about Jesus because Paul was in chains?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

A governor, a king, a queen, the chief captains and the principal men of the city. No church would ever have gotten Paul into that room. His chains did. God can use the very thing that feels like your defeat to open a door you couldn't open yourself.
