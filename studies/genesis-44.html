Genesis 44

The silver cup, and the man who took his brother's place

A silver cup is found in Benjamin's sack, and the other ten are free to go home. Instead Judah, the brother who once sold Joseph, steps forward and offers himself as a slave in Benjamin's place. It is the clearest picture of substitution in Genesis, and the clearest picture of a changed man.

Part one

The silver cup

Verses 1–13

The brothers set out for home, and a steward rides after them.

The old set-up, with a new ending in view

Then (chapter 37)

The pit

Rachel's favoured son, alone, in their power. A way to be rid of him. They took it, and went home with a lie.

Now (chapter 44)

The cup

Rachel's other son, the new favourite, alone, and in danger. And a way to be rid of him: walk away, and go home free. What will they do this time?

Joseph never tells us why he plants the cup. But look at the shape: the same sort of brother, the same sort of power, and the same easy exit. The only thing that can be different is the men.

This is how God proves a change in a man. Not by what he says on his knees, but by what he does when the old chance comes round again, and nobody would blame him for taking it. If you are trying to get clean, you know this test. The old situation will come back. The question is who walks into it.

“Whereby indeed he divineth”

“Divine” means to foretell, to read signs. Some ancient peoples looked for omens in a cup of water or oil. Joseph says in verse 15: “wot ye not that such a man as I can certainly divine?” (“Wot” means know.)

Is Joseph claiming to practise divination? He is speaking in character, as the Egyptian lord they believe him to be. The Bible never says he used the cup that way, and when Pharaoh asked him for a dream's meaning he said, “It is not in me: God shall give Pharaoh an answer of peace” (41:16).

Read the charge too: “Wherefore have ye rewarded evil for good?” They had been fed and given back their silver. And it lands on their conscience: it is just what they did to a brother who came to them with food in the field.

A confident oath, and a lighter sentence

What they swore (verse 9)

“Let him die”

“With whomsoever of thy servants it be found, both let him die, and we also will be my lord's bondmen.” They are so sure of their innocence they stake a man's life on it.

What Joseph answered (verse 10)

“My servant”

“He with whom it is found shall be my servant; and ye shall be blameless.” No death. One servant. And the other ten go free.

Notice Joseph's reply. Even in the performance he reduces the penalty from death to service, and he frees the rest. The offer in verse 10 is the same open door he will hold open in verse 17.

Their own father once swore such an oath, without checking what he swore about: “With whomsoever thou findest thy gods, let him not live” (31:32). He did not know his own wife had taken them. A rash promise, made without the facts, can fall on someone you love. Look before you swear.

From the eldest to the youngest

Read the last point twice. The steward had said that only the man with the cup would be kept (verse 10). They could have gone home. Instead they tore their clothes (“rent” means tore, a sign of grief), loaded their donkeys (“laded” means loaded) and went back, all eleven of them. Not one man left Benjamin.

Twenty years ago, they left Joseph and went home. Today they turn around.

Part two

Found out

Verses 14–17

Judah does not argue. He admits.

“God hath found out the iniquity”

Judah leads now (“Judah and his brethren”). They did not steal the cup, yet he says: “God hath found out the iniquity of thy servants.” Iniquity means sin, the crooked thing.

He is not talking about a cup. He means Joseph, the pit, the silver and the lie, twenty years of it. When the hard thing arrives, he knows exactly what it is for.

“But if ye will not do so, behold, ye have sinned against the LORD: and be sure your sin will find you out.”

Numbers 32:23

That sounds like a threat. For Judah it was a mercy: a sin found out can finally be dealt with, and one that stays hidden never gives a man peace.

The open door

What Joseph offered (verse 17)

“Get you up in peace”

“God forbid that I should do so: but the man in whose hand the cup is found, he shall be my servant; and as for you, get you up in peace unto your father.” Ten men may walk out. The old, easy way is wide open.

What Judah does (verse 18)

“Then Judah came near”

He does not take the door. He steps toward the man who holds all the power, to plead for a brother who is not even his own full brother.

They were offered the very thing they took in chapter 37: leave him, and go home. When the old chance comes round again and nobody would blame you for taking it, that is where a changed man shows up. Tell your brothers in Christ what your old doors are, before you reach them.

Part three

Judah's plea

Verses 18–34

The longest speech in Genesis, and the shortest request.

How Judah pleads

  1. Come nearhumbly, “let not thine anger burn” (18)
  2. Tell the storya father, an old man, a young son (19–23)
  3. The father's love“his life is bound up in the lad's life” (24–31)
  4. The pledge“thy servant became surety” (32)
  5. Take me instead“let thy servant abide” (33–34)

“Thou art even as Pharaoh” means: you hold a king's power, and I know it. He does not threaten or bargain. He argues not for his own freedom, but for his father's heart. Everything he says is about someone else.

Said to the face of the man he sold

Judah repeats his father's words: “Surely he is torn in pieces; and I saw him not since.” He knows that is a lie, and who helped make it. He says it in front of the very brother it is about.

In chapter 37 Jacob's sons stood around his bed to comfort him (37:35), and said nothing. For twenty years Judah watched his father grieve a death that never happened. Now he cannot bear to do it again: “How shall I go up to my father, and the lad be not with me?”

What changed him was not a rule, but seeing what his sin did to a father. Regret that only feels bad does nothing. Regret that stands in the gap for the one you hurt is the kind God uses. Who did you hurt? What can you do for him today?

Instead of the lad

Judah has nothing to gain and everything to lose. A slave in Egypt, forever, so that a boy he does not even have to protect can go home to his father.

Then they trade places. The brother who was caught goes home to his father, and the man who volunteers takes the chains.

“Instead of” is the heart of substitution. Not with him, not for the sake of him, but in his place. And it is the same man who once said “What profit is it?”

One who does it in full

The Bible never calls Judah a “type” of Christ, and Judah was a guilty man. But the pattern is plain, and the New Testament uses Judah's word:

“By so much was Jesus made a surety of a better testament.”

Hebrews 7:22

Judah

A guilty man, for a brother

He offered to take Benjamin's place as a slave.

Jesus

The innocent One, for the guilty

He did not only offer. He did it, for men who had done the wrong.

Someone has paid

“For Christ also hath once suffered for sins, the just for the unjust, that he might bring us to God, being put to death in the flesh, but quickened by the Spirit:”

1 Peter 3:18

“All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned every one to his own way; and the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all.”

Isaiah 53:6

If you have ever thought, someone has to pay for what I did, you are right. And Someone has. He stood where you stood, took what you had coming, and rose again. That is why you may come in.

Judah's road, down and up

Judah sold his brother, fell into sin, was caught, and owned it: “She hath been more righteous than I” (38:26). God did not drop him. If you fell last night or this morning, the same God is not done with you. Own it, tell your brothers in Christ and your pastor, and take the next step up.

The tribe of the sceptre

“The sceptre shall not depart from Judah, nor a lawgiver from between his feet, until Shiloh come; and unto him shall the gathering of the people be.”

Genesis 49:10

When Jacob blesses his sons, the sceptre (the king's rod) goes to Judah, the son who sold his brother, fell, repented, and offered himself. David came from Judah, and so did Jesus:

“And one of the elders saith unto me, Weep not: behold, the Lion of the tribe of Juda, the Root of David, hath prevailed to open the book, and to loose the seven seals thereof.”

Revelation 5:5

“Juda” is the New Testament spelling of Judah. God brought His Son through the line of a man with Judah's record. Grace works with the family you have.

Question 1 of 6

Testing

What chance did Joseph set up for the brothers to repeat their old sin?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

The cup in Benjamin's sack gave them what they had in chapter 37: a favoured brother in trouble, and the chance to leave him and go home free. The text does not say why Joseph did it, but it shows what he was watching for. A changed man is shown by what he does when the old chance comes round again.

Question 2 of 6

Regret

What did Judah mean by “God hath found out the iniquity of thy servants”?

What verses tell us about this?

“But if ye will not do so, behold, ye have sinned against the LORD: and be sure your sin will find you out.”

Numbers 32:23

Answer

They had not stolen the cup. Judah means the old sin: Joseph, the pit, the silver, the lie. Twenty years later it had found them. A sin found out can finally be dealt with. Do not wait to be found: bring it out to God, and to a brother in Christ or your pastor, today.

Question 3 of 6

Loyalty

What way out did Joseph offer, and did the brothers take it?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

“Get you up in peace unto your father”: ten men could walk home free. They did not. All of them had already turned back to the city with Benjamin, and Judah stepped forward. Twenty years ago they left a brother. Today nobody does.

Question 4 of 6

Change

How was Judah different from the man who said “What profit is it?”

What verses tell us about this?

“And Judah said unto his brethren, What profit is it if we slay our brother, and conceal his blood?”

Genesis 37:26

“And Judah acknowledged them, and said, She hath been more righteous than I; because that I gave her not to Shelah my son. And he knew her again no more.”

Genesis 38:26

Answer

Then he thought of profit. Then he was caught in his own sin, and owned it. Now he thinks of his father's heart and offers himself. A man can be remade, and God uses the broken.

Question 5 of 6

Substitution

What did Judah offer in Benjamin's place, and who does that point to?

What verses tell us about this?

“By so much was Jesus made a surety of a better testament.”

Hebrews 7:22

Answer

Himself, as a slave, in the boy's place. It is a picture of what Jesus did in full: “the just for the unjust” (1 Peter 3:18). Judah was a guilty man offering; Jesus actually paid. Whatever you owe, Someone has already stood in your place.

Question 6 of 6

Hope

Where does the story of Judah and his family end up?

What verses tell us about this?

“The sceptre shall not depart from Judah, nor a lawgiver from between his feet, until Shiloh come; and unto him shall the gathering of the people be.”

Genesis 49:10

“And one of the elders saith unto me, Weep not: behold, the Lion of the tribe of Juda, the Root of David, hath prevailed to open the book, and to loose the seven seals thereof.”

Revelation 5:5

Answer

The sceptre goes to Judah. King David comes from him, and so does Jesus, “the Lion of the tribe of Juda.” The man who sold his brother became the father of kings. If you fell yesterday, your worst chapter is not your last. Own it, and take the next step with your brothers in Christ beside you.
