Genesis 32

Afraid, on his knees, and wrestling at the river

Jacob is going home, and the brother he cheated is coming with four hundred men. He is terrified. He sends a gift, prays the best prayer of his life, and spends the night alone at the Jabbok, wrestling with a man who is more than a man. He comes out with a new name and a limp.

Part one

Two camps

Verses 1–8

God sends an army to meet a man who is about to get bad news.

Mahanaim

מַחֲנָיִם said Machanayim “two camps”: God's host, and Jacob's

Twenty years ago Jacob left this land and saw angels on a ladder at Bethel (28:12). Now he comes back, and angels meet him again. Angels at the going out, angels at the coming in.

“And he answered, Fear not: for they that be with us are more than they that be with them. And Elisha prayed, and said, LORD, I pray thee, open his eyes, that he may see. And the LORD opened the eyes of the young man; and he saw: and, behold, the mountain was full of horses and chariots of fire round about Elisha.”

2 Kings 6:16-17

There are two camps at Mahanaim, and only one is visible. Within a few verses Jacob will split his own company into two bands (verses 7–8), counting on his own two camps and forgetting the first. God's host is there whether you can see it or not.

Esau, in Seir

Seir is the hill country south-east of the Dead Sea, “the country of Edom.” Jacob sends word ahead. Look at his words: “my lord Esau”, “thy servant Jacob”, “that I may find grace in thy sight.” The man who was told be lord over thy brethren now calls his brother lord.

The line shows the way Esau's men would have come, along the old road east of the Dead Sea. The text does not give the route.

Old words: sojourned lived as a stranger; asses are donkeys; menservants and womenservants are male and female servants.

What the messengers did not say

Esau's men

400

A small army, riding toward him.

Since they met

20

Years since Jacob stole the blessing and ran (27:41–43).

The messengers do not say whether Esau is coming for a reunion or a revenge. Jacob fills in the silence the only way a guilty man can: Esau is coming to finish it. The text says he was “greatly afraid and distressed”, both words.

Being afraid is not the sin. Look at what Jacob does with it. He makes a plan (verses 7–8, 13–21) and he prays (verses 9–12). He planned first and prayed second. Next time, flip it. But he did pray, and that is what matters.

“I sought the LORD, and he heard me, and delivered me from all my fears.”

Psalm 34:4

Part two

A prayer and a present

Verses 9–21

Fear sends Jacob to his knees, and then to his flocks.

“With my staff”

Twenty years ago

1 staff

All he had when he crossed the Jordan going out.

Now

2 bands

Two companies: wives, children, servants, flocks and herds.

“I am not worthy of the least of all the mercies.” Jacob does not argue his case. He says what he has: every good thing in his hands came from God, and he did nothing to earn it.

Old word: shewed is showed. Mercies and truth are God's kindness and his faithfulness to what he promised.

The shape of a prayer

  1. 1O God of my father Abraham, and God of my father IsaacStart with who he is.9
  2. 2which saidst unto me, Return unto thy countryRemind him of what he told you, and that you are doing what he said.9
  3. 3I am not worthy of the least of all the merciesCome with no case to make for yourself.10
  4. 4Deliver me, I pray thee, from the hand of my brotherAsk for exactly what you need, in plain words. He fears most for “the mother with the children.”11
  5. 5Thou saidst, I will surely do thee goodEnd by holding on to his promise.12

Jacob's prayer is not pretty. It is honest. He says “I fear him” (verse 11) out loud to God. If your own prayers feel clumsy, this one is a pattern: fear, promise, and a plain request.

“Be careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God. And the peace of God, which passeth all understanding, shall keep your hearts and minds through Christ Jesus.”

Philippians 4:6-7

What the present was worth

  1. 440goats and sheep (220 goats, 220 sheep)
  2. 30milch camels (mothers with milk), with their colts
  3. 50cattle: forty kine (cows) and ten bulls
  4. 30donkeys: twenty she asses and ten foals
  5. 550+animals in all, not counting the camel colts

This was a large part of everything Jacob had worked twenty years for, handed over in one gift. Old words: milch means giving milk, and kine are cows.

He is trying to buy peace, and he is willing to pay a great deal. When a man has really wronged someone, nothing he gives feels like enough.

Wave after wave

Esau, to the first servantWhose art thou? and whither goest thou? and whose are these before thee?

The servantThey be thy servant Jacob's; it is a present sent unto my lord Esau. a drove

The second, the thirdBehold, thy servant Jacob is behind us. and another

Jacob spaced the droves out (“put a space betwixt drove and drove”) so that Esau would meet one gift after another, and hear the same words each time. Servant. Present. My lord. By the time Esau reached Jacob himself, he would have been softened by five or six rounds of kindness.

Jacob is a planner. That is his strength and his flaw. He plans for everything but the one thing he cannot plan: how Esau's heart will go.

“I will appease him”

אֲכַפְּרָה said akapperah “I will cover”: the word behind atonement

The King James says appease. The Hebrew is the word for cover: the same word used of covering sin in the law, which is why it is often translated “atonement.” Jacob wants to cover what he did with a present.

It is the oldest trick: pay the person off and hope. Giving is good. But no gift can cover what a man did. For that, God gave his own Son.

“And he is the propitiation for our sins: and not for ours only, but also for the sins of the whole world.”

1 John 2:2

Watch what happens in chapter 33. Esau never needed the present. The covering Jacob wanted comes free.

Part three

Alone at the Jabbok

Verses 22–32

The night before the meeting, Jacob meets the One he has been wrestling all his life.

And Jacob was left alone.

He sent his wives, his children, his servants and everything he owned across the river. Nothing left but Jacob and the dark: no flock to count, no plan to make, no one to hide behind.

God often does his hardest work with a man when he is down to himself. But being alone with God is not the same as hiding from people. In the morning Jacob walked out to his family. If you are in a dark stretch of your own, you can call a brother in Christ or your pastor. You do not have to wrestle in the dark by yourself.

Old words: a ford is a shallow place for crossing a river. The breaking of the day is dawn.

The sound of the story

וַיֵּאָבֵק said vaye'aveq “and he wrestled”: grappled, hung on

Hebrew readers heard a play on words. The river is the Yabbok. The man is Ya'akov (Jacob). The wrestling is ye'abek. Yabbok, Ya'akov, ye'abek: the river, the man and the fight all sound alike.

Who was the man?

  1. Verse 24“a man”: that is what the text calls him, at first
  2. Hosea 12:4“he had power over the angel, and prevailed”
  3. Verse 30Jacob: “I have seen God face to face”

“He took his brother by the heel in the womb, and by his strength he had power with God: Yea, he had power over the angel, and prevailed: he wept, and made supplication unto him: he found him in Bethel, and there he spake with us;”

Hosea 12:3-4

This is the LORD himself, the Son of God, taking the form of a man long before he was born at Bethlehem. The text says a man; Hosea says the angel; Jacob says God. Jacob left knowing he had met the Almighty.

Notice who “prevailed not.” God did not need to win by strength. One touch of the hip was enough. He let Jacob struggle, and then he ended it with a touch.

From fighting to clinging

Verse 24In the dark

Alone, Jacob wrestles with all his strength. He has been fighting all his life.

Verses 25–26Touched

One touch, and the hip is out of joint. A strong man cannot stand. So he clings: “I will not let thee go, except thou bless me.”

Verse 31The sun rose

He walks away blessed, and limping.

The hollow of the thigh is the hip socket. At the moment Jacob stopped being strong, he started asking. The night turned when he quit wrestling and started holding on.

“What is thy name?”

The Man knows. The question is not for information. It makes Jacob say it out loud. And Jacob's name means the one who grabs the heel, the cheater. Twenty years earlier Esau had said:

“And he said, Is not he rightly named Jacob? for he hath supplanted me these two times: he took away my birthright; and, behold, now he hath taken away my blessing. And he said, Hast thou not reserved a blessing for me?”

Genesis 27:36

So before the blessing, Jacob says his own name, the one that means what he did. “Jacob.” That is a confession.

“If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness.”

1 John 1:9

A man who says it plainly to God (this is who I am, this is what I did) is in the right place for a blessing.

Jacob → Israel From “he grabs the heel, he cheats” to a name Bible teachers give as “he struggles with God” or “God strives.” The verse explains it itself: “as a prince hast thou power with God and with men, and hast prevailed.”

God did not just rename him; he named him by what he had become that night: a man who held on to God and would not let go.

The new name does not wipe out the old one on the same day. In the chapters ahead the Bible still calls him Jacob, sometimes in the same paragraph. When he walks by faith, he is Israel; when he schemes or fears, he is Jacob again. That is honest for us too: a new man, with old habits that die slowly.

Peniel

פְּנִיאֵל said Peni-el “the face of God”

The same place is spelled Penuel in verse 31. It is one place, with two spellings.

Jacob had prayed in verse 11, “Deliver me.” Now in verse 30, “my life is preserved.” In Hebrew these are the same word. He asked to be rescued from Esau, and before he even met Esau, God had rescued him from something deeper: from facing God and not living through it.

The sun rose upon him, and he halted upon his thigh.

To halt here is to limp. God did not heal the hip. He blessed the man and left the limp. Jacob went to meet his brother not as the strong planner but as a man who leaned.

“And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness. Most gladly therefore will I rather glory in my infirmities, that the power of Christ may rest upon me.”

2 Corinthians 12:9

Many of us carry a limp from our own past: lost years, a record, broken family. It does not disqualify you. It may be the very place you learn to lean.

A people who remembered

  1. hollow of the thighthe hip socket
  2. sinewthe strong cord of muscle or tendon
  3. shrankwas strained or shrivelled
  4. unto this daywhen Moses wrote this, about 1400 BC

Verse 32 gives a custom: Israel did not eat that sinew of an animal. A whole nation built a habit to remember a single night at a river. If God has done something for you, build a small habit that remembers it: a verse on the fridge, a call to a brother on the same day each year.

Question 1 of 6

Not alone

Who did Jacob meet on the road, and what did he call that place?

What verses tell us about this?

And the LORD opened the eyes of the young man; and he saw: and, behold, the mountain was full of horses and chariots of fire round about Elisha.”

Answer

The angels of God met him. He said, “This is God's host”, and he named the place Mahanaim, “two camps.” God's army was camped beside a frightened man who could not see it. If the road ahead looks bad, remember that the unseen camp is there.

Question 2 of 6

Fear

What did Jacob do when he heard Esau was coming with four hundred men?

What verses tell us about this?

“I sought the LORD, and he heard me, and delivered me from all my fears.”

Psalm 34:4

Answer

He was afraid, and he did two things with it. He made a plan: two bands, and a huge present. And he prayed. Fear itself is not a sin. What matters is where it takes you. Take yours to God first, then to a brother in Christ.

Question 3 of 6

Prayer

What can a frightened man learn from Jacob's prayer?

What verses tell us about this?

“Be careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God. And the peace of God, which passeth all understanding, shall keep your hearts and minds through Christ Jesus.”

Philippians 4:6-7

Answer

Start with who God is. Remind him what he has said. Admit you have no claim. Ask plainly for what you need. And hold on to his promise. Jacob was honest enough to say, “I fear him.” Tell God what you are afraid of, and the peace of God will keep your heart.

Question 4 of 6

The wrestling

Who wrestled with Jacob until daybreak, and why would he not let go?

What verses tell us about this?

“He took his brother by the heel in the womb, and by his strength he had power with God: Yea, he had power over the angel, and prevailed: he wept, and made supplication unto him: he found him in Bethel, and there he spake with us;”

Hosea 12:3-4

Answer

A man, who Hosea calls the angel and Jacob calls God. Many teachers see the LORD Himself. Jacob would not let go because he knew he needed a blessing more than he needed to win. The night turned when he stopped fighting and started clinging.

Question 5 of 6

A new name

Why did God change Jacob's name to Israel?

What verses tell us about this?

“And he said, Is not he rightly named Jacob? for he hath supplanted me these two times: he took away my birthright; and, behold, now he hath taken away my blessing. And he said, Hast thou not reserved a blessing for me?”

Genesis 27:36

“If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness.”

1 John 1:9

Answer

First God had him say his old name, the one that meant cheat: that was a confession. Then God gave him a new name for what the night had made of him: a man who struggled with God and held on. Say honestly who you are to God, and he will give you a name by what he will make of you.

Question 6 of 6

The limp

What did Jacob carry out of that night, and what did it mean?

What verses tell us about this?

“And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness. Most gladly therefore will I rather glory in my infirmities, that the power of Christ may rest upon me.”

2 Corinthians 12:9

Answer

A limp. God touched his hip and left it. Jacob went to meet his brother leaning, not strutting. His weakness was the place he learned to depend on God. The scars of your past do not disqualify you. Let them be where you lean.
