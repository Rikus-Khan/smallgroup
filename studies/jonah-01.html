Jonah 1

Running from God

God tells Jonah to go east to Nineveh. Jonah buys a ticket west, and finds out there's nowhere a man can run where God can't reach him.

The book of Jonah

Who, when, and to whom

Jonah son of Amittai was a prophet from Gath-hepher, a town in Galilee, in Israel, the northern kingdom. He served under King Jeroboam II, about 790–750 BC, and he had already brought Israel good news:

“He restored the coast of Israel from the entering of Hamath unto the sea of the plain, according to the word of the LORD God of Israel, which he spake by the hand of his servant Jonah, the son of Amittai, the prophet, which was of Gath-hepher.”

2 Kings 14:25
  1. about 930 BCThe kingdom splits: Israel north, Judah south
  2. about 785 BCJonah, under Jeroboam II
  3. about 760 BCAmos and Hosea warn Israel
  4. 722 BCAssyria takes Israel
  5. 612 BCNineveh falls

Jonah's book is different from the other prophets. It's mostly a story, and the prophet is the one who needs changing. God sends him not to Israel but to Nineveh, a great city of Assyria, Israel's worst enemy.

Part one

Running

Verses 1–3

God says go east. Jonah heads west.

That great city

Nineveh was one of the great cities of Assyria, the superpower of the day, and later its capital. Assyria was known for cruelty. Their own palace carvings show captives impaled and skinned, and their kings bragged about it.

“Woe to the bloody city! it is all full of lies and robbery; the prey departeth not;”

Nahum 3:1

God saw all of it: “their wickedness is come up before me.” And He sent a warning anyway. That tells you something about God before Jonah ever leaves home.

The opposite direction

Nineveh was about 500 miles east, over land. Tarshish was far to the west, probably in Spain, about as far as ships went in those days.

Twice the verse says why: “from the presence of the LORD.” And notice, “he paid the fare.” Running from God always costs something, and the bill usually runs higher than the ticket.

Part two

The storm

Verses 4–10

God throws a wind at the sea, and a ship full of pagans ends up praying.

Down, down, down

Watch the direction Jonah keeps going:

  1. down to Joppa(1:3)
  2. down into the ship(1:3)
  3. down into its sidesfast asleep (1:5)
  4. into the sea(1:15)
  5. to the bottoms of the mountains(2:6)

Running from God is never a climb. Every step away is a step lower. And Jonah slept through a storm that had sailors screaming. A man running from God can get numb enough to sleep through anything.

“If I take the wings of the morning, and dwell in the uttermost parts of the sea; Even there shall thy hand lead me, and thy right hand shall hold me.”

Psalm 139:9–10

“O sleeper”

The shipmasterWhat meanest thou, O sleeper? arise, call upon thy God, if so be that God will think upon us, that we perish not.

Jonah…
no prayer recorded

A pagan sea captain has to tell God's prophet to pray. And listen to his first word: “arise.” It's the same word God used in verse 2. God's call caught up with Jonah through a stranger's mouth.

Sometimes God's word comes back to us from somebody we'd never expect: a cop, a judge, a nurse, a cellmate. It's still God calling.

What he said, what he did

What Jonah said

“I fear the LORD”

The God of heaven, who made the sea and the dry land.

What Jonah did

Ran from Him

Onto the very sea that God made, hoping to get away from Him.

The sailors saw it before Jonah did: “Why hast thou done this?” A man's beliefs and his life can drift far apart, and other people notice. And the lots weren't luck:

“The lot is cast into the lap; but the whole disposing thereof is of the LORD.”

Proverbs 16:33

Part three

Overboard

Verses 11–17

Jonah goes into the sea, and God has a fish waiting.

“For my sake”

Finally, the truth. Jonah doesn't blame the weather, the crew, or God. “I know that for my sake this great tempest is upon you.” That's owning it.

He owns the storm, but notice what he still doesn't say: “Turn the ship around, I'll go to Nineveh.” Owning it is where change starts. It isn't the whole of it. That comes later.

“He that covereth his sins shall not prosper: but whoso confesseth and forsaketh them shall have mercy.”

Proverbs 28:13

“The men rowed hard … but they could not.”

These were decent men. They didn't want to throw anybody overboard, so they tried everything else first. They rowed for all they were worth, and the sea only got worse.

A lot of us know that kind of rowing: white-knuckling, promising to try harder, one more plan. You can't out-row a storm that God sent to turn you around. At some point the only thing left is to stop rowing and deal with what God is dealing with.

And look who's praying now: “O LORD.” The pagan sailors are calling on Israel's God by name.

The sea ceased

Jonah 1:5Every man to his god

Each sailor cries to his own idol.

Jonah 1:14“O LORD”

They call on the true God by name.

Jonah 1:16Feared the LORD

A sacrifice and vows, to Him.

Jonah ran to keep God’s mercy from outsiders (4:2). God used his running to bring a boatload of outsiders to Himself anyway.

The sign of the prophet Jonas

Jonah 1:17

Now the LORD had prepared a great fish to swallow up Jonah. And Jonah was in the belly of the fish three days and three nights.

Matthew 12:40

For as Jonas was three days and three nights in the whale’s belly; so shall the Son of man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth.

“Prepared” means God had it ready. The fish wasn't the punishment. It was the rescue. A drowning man got swallowed by mercy.

Jesus treated Jonah as a real man and his three days as a sign of His own death, burial, and resurrection. Jonah went down because of his own sin. Jesus went down for ours, and came up again. “Behold, a greater than Jonas is here” (Matthew 12:41).

Points to remember

  1. Running“To flee unto Tarshish from the presence of the LORD” 1:3There's no place God isn't. A man can run, but he can't get away.
  2. The way down“Jonah was gone down into the sides of the ship” 1:5Every step away from God is a step lower, and it can leave a man numb.
  3. Storms“The LORD sent out a great wind into the sea” 1:4Some storms are God's love coming after a runaway, to turn him around.
  4. Owning it“For my sake this great tempest is upon you” 1:12No blaming. Confession is where change starts.
  5. God's reach“Then the men feared the LORD exceedingly” 1:16God's mercy reached the sailors even through a runaway prophet.
  6. The sign of Jonas“Three days and three nights” 1:17Jesus pointed to Jonah as a sign of His own death and resurrection.

Question 1 of 6

Running

Can a man outrun God?

What verses tell us about this?

“Whither shall I go from thy spirit? or whither shall I flee from thy presence?”

Psalm 139:7

“If I take the wings of the morning, and dwell in the uttermost parts of the sea; Even there shall thy hand lead me, and thy right hand shall hold me.”

Psalm 139:9–10

Answer

No. Jonah bought a ticket to the end of the known world, and God met him in the middle of the sea. There's no place God isn't. That's a warning to the man who's running, and it's comfort too: the hand that finds you is the hand that holds you.

Question 2 of 6

The way down

Where does running from God take a man?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Down. Down to Joppa, down into the ship, down into its belly, down into the sea. It costs him his fare, his peace, and nearly his life, and it puts other people in danger too. And on the way down, he got numb enough to sleep through a storm. Running always goes lower than a man plans.

Question 3 of 6

Storms

Why does God send storms?

What verses tell us about this?

“For whom the Lord loveth he chasteneth, and scourgeth every son whom he receiveth.”

Hebrews 12:6

“Then they cry unto the LORD in their trouble, and he bringeth them out of their distresses. He maketh the storm a calm, so that the waves thereof are still.”

Psalm 107:28–29

Answer

Not every storm is a punishment. Jesus' disciples hit storms while doing exactly what He said. But this one came because a man was running, and God loved him too much to let him go. A storm like that isn't God giving up on you. It's God coming after you, to turn you around and bring you home.

Question 4 of 6

Owning it

What does it look like to own what you've done?

What verses tell us about this?

“He that covereth his sins shall not prosper: but whoso confesseth and forsaketh them shall have mercy.”

Proverbs 28:13

“If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness.”

1 John 1:9

Answer

It sounds like Jonah: “for my sake.” No blaming the weather, the crew, or God. Owning it means telling the truth about what I did and what it's costing the people around me. That's where change starts, and God meets confession with mercy. Then comes the next part: turning around and doing what He says.

Question 5 of 6

God's reach

How did God use even Jonah's running?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

The sailors started the day crying to their own gods and ended it fearing the LORD, offering Him a sacrifice and making vows. Jonah ran to keep God's mercy away from outsiders, and God brought a whole crew of outsiders to Himself anyway. Running is never right, but God's mercy reaches further than our failures.

Question 6 of 6

The sign of Jonas

What did Jesus say about Jonah's three days?

What verses tell us about this?

“For as Jonas was three days and three nights in the whale’s belly; so shall the Son of man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth.”

Matthew 12:40

“The men of Nineveh shall rise in judgment with this generation, and shall condemn it: because they repented at the preaching of Jonas; and, behold, a greater than Jonas is here.”

Matthew 12:41

Answer

Jesus called it “the sign of the prophet Jonas.” As Jonah was three days and nights in the fish, Jesus would be three days and nights in the grave, and then come out alive. Jonah went down for his own sin. Jesus went down for ours. The empty tomb is the sign that God's rescue is real.
