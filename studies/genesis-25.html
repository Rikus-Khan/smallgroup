Genesis 25

A good old age, and a bowl of red stew

Abraham dies, and his two oldest sons bury him side by side. Then the story turns to the twins: one brother who is hungry now, and one who wants what is coming.

Part one

Abraham's last chapter

Verses 1–11

A big family, a tidy will, and a good ending.

Abraham's sons

  • Abraham
    • Isaacby Sarah: the promise
    • Ishmaelby Hagar
    • Zimran
    • JokshanSheba, Dedan
    • Medan
    • Midian
    • Ishbak
    • Shuah

Keturah's six sons add up to a big new family. Genesis says plainly that she is a wife (verse 1), and verse 6 calls such women “concubines”, which means wives of a lower standing in the household. When she came into Abraham's life, the text does not say.

Her sons are the fathers of desert peoples. The one you will meet again is Midian: Midianite traders buy Joseph (37:28), and Moses will later marry a Midianite woman. God had promised Abraham “a father of many nations” (17:5), and here some of them are.

A will, made while he lived

Isaac

“All that he had”

The land, the flocks, the promise, and the place at the head of the family: everything that came with God's covenant.

The other sons

Gifts, and a home elsewhere

Gifts while he was still alive, and a place to live, “eastward, unto the east country”, away from Isaac.

There was no grabbing over the will, because Abraham settled it himself, while he lived. That is how you keep brothers from fighting over a grave. Put your house in order while you can: say what you mean, and make the peace you can.

Isaac was the son of the promise. He got the covenant because God chose him (21:12), not because he was first or best. The others were not thrown out empty-handed.

“A good old age”

Abraham was 175, “full of years”. Add up the ages and he lived to see his twin grandsons grow to fifteen: three generations of the promise at once.

“Gathered to his people”

He “gave up the ghost”: the old way of saying his spirit left him and he died. He died “full of years”: satisfied, with nothing left undone.

“Gathered to his people” is said before the burial in verse 9, so it is not about the grave: his family lay far away in Mesopotamia. Teachers take it as a sign that death is not the end of the person.

He is not a God of the dead

Abraham had no great monument, no kingdom, and he owned one cave in a land that was not his. He died in a good old age. Is that a good ending?

“For he is not a God of the dead, but of the living: for all live unto him.”

Luke 20:38

“Precious in the sight of the LORD is the death of his saints.”

Psalm 116:15

Jesus quotes God saying “I am the God of Abraham” (Luke 20:37), long after Abraham died, and says it proves Abraham still lives. A believer's death is “precious” to God. Not an accident, and not the end.

If you are afraid of dying, or have buried someone you loved who knew the Lord, take this seriously. Death is not where the story stops for a man who has faith.

Two brothers at one grave

Isaac + Ishmael The son of the promise and the son of the mistake, standing together to bury their father.

Last time we saw them together, Ishmael was a teenager sent away (21:14). Years of hurt lay between them. Yet when their father dies, Genesis says “his sons Isaac and Ishmael buried him”: Isaac's name first, and both there.

We are not told what they said to one another. Still, a funeral can be the place where a broken family stands in the same spot again. If you have a brother or a father you have not spoken to in years, you do not have to fix everything. Start by turning up.

The cave of Machpelah

The cave in “the field of Ephron the son of Zohar the Hittite” is the plot Abraham paid for in chapter 23, to bury Sarah. It is the only land in Canaan Abraham ever owned, and the family tomb is the first piece of the promised land that they held.

Verse 10 says Abraham “purchased” it, an old word for bought, “of the sons of Heth” (the Hittites), and that is where he lies, beside Sarah.

Verse 11 says “God blessed his son Isaac”, and Isaac dwelt by the well Lahai-roi, the same place we found him at in chapter 24. The blessing did not die with Abraham. God had made the promise, and he keeps it with the next man too.

Ishmael's twelve

A list of names that shows that God keeps his word, even to the son nobody planned.

“And as for Ishmael, I have heard thee: Behold, I have blessed him, and will make him fruitful, and will multiply him exceedingly; twelve princes shall he beget, and I will make him a great nation.”

Genesis 17:20

Twelve names, and twelve princes: exactly what God said in 17:20. Ishmael was born out of Abraham and Sarah's mistake, and God still kept every word he spoke over him.

“By their towns, and by their castles”

Verse 16 says these twelve are “princes according to their nations”, living “by their towns, and by their castles”. A “castle” here means an enclosed camp or a walled village, not a stone fortress: each prince had a settlement.

The names come up again in the Bible, as tribes of northern Arabia: Kedar, Tema, Dumah and the rest. They were real peoples with real settlements, and God remembered every name.

That is why a list like this is in the Bible. God knows every man by name, even the ones history forgot. If you ever feel like a footnote, or the child of someone else's mistake, remember Ishmael. Twelve princes were counted by name, because God had said he heard him.

From Havilah to Shur

Ishmael's family “dwelt from Havilah unto Shur, that is before Egypt, as thou goest toward Assyria”. That is the desert country between Canaan and Egypt on one side, and Assyria on the other. (Bible teachers are not sure exactly where Havilah was, so we won't pin it down.)

Ishmael was 137 when he died, and he “died in the presence of all his brethren”. The old words ring a bell. Before Ishmael was born, the angel told Hagar: “he shall dwell in the presence of all his brethren” (16:12).

What God said to a runaway slave girl at a well came true, down to the detail.

“Concubines” (verse 6) were secondary wives, not slaves or strangers. “Brethren” is the old plural of brother.

Part three

Twenty years of waiting

Verses 19–26

Isaac does what his father did not: he asks God, and he waits.

“Isaac intreated the LORD”

  1. about 2025 BCIsaac marrieshe is 40
  2. twenty yearsno child“she was barren”
  3. about 2005 BCThe twins are bornIsaac is 60

“Intreated” means pleaded with: asked earnestly, again and again. And “was intreated of him” means let himself be asked: God answered. Twenty years of asking.

Think about what his parents did. When Sarah could not have a child, she and Abraham grew tired of waiting and made their own plan (Hagar, chapter 16). Isaac has the same promise and the same wait, and he does what they did not do at first: he asks the LORD for it.

When you are tired of waiting for something God has promised, don't make a plan of your own. Ask, and keep on asking.

What the LORD told Rebekah

Two nations are in thy womb,

and two manner of people shall be separated from thy bowels;

the one people shall be stronger than the other people;

and the elder shall serve the younger.

Rebekah felt the babies struggling and asked, “If it be so, why am I thus?” (why is this happening to me?). She took her worry to the LORD.

Two nations will come from her, and the older will serve the younger, which was not how things usually went. Hold on to that for chapter 27.

A heel and a name

יַעֲקֹב said Ya'aqov “he takes by the heel”: Jacob

Esau came first, red and hairy “like an hairy garment”, which is a cloak of hair. Jacob came out with his hand gripping Esau's heel. A grip on the heel was a picture of one trying to pull another back.

The name stuck, and later it gets a second meaning. Esau says: “Is not he rightly named Jacob? for he hath supplanted me these two times” (27:36). To supplant is to trip someone up and take his place.

Isaac was “threescore years old”, which is sixty. He had waited twenty years.

Part four

The stew

Verses 27–34

One bowl, one afternoon, and a choice that can't be taken back.

Two boys, two favourites

Esau

A “cunning hunter”: skilled at it. A man of the open country. His father loved him “because he did eat of his venison”, which is game meat.

Jacob

A “plain man, dwelling in tents”: quiet, settled, at home with the flocks. His mother loved him.

The word “plain” here is the Hebrew tam, which can mean quiet, mild or complete. It does not mean Jacob was without fault. The rest of the story shows he was far from that.

The sad part is the last line of verse 28: one parent for each boy. The father liked what Esau brought him. The mother loved the quiet one. When parents divide the children between them, the children learn to compete. Chapter 27 is the harvest of this verse.

If you are a father, hear it. Love each of your sons for who he is, not for what he does for you.

Old words in verses 27–34

  1. venisonmeat from hunting (here, any wild game)
  2. sodboiled, cooked in water
  3. pottagea thick stew or soup
  4. lentileslentils, a small bean
  5. faintworn out and very hungry
  6. birthrightthe firstborn son's special rights

“Cunning” in verse 27 means skilled, with no bad meaning here.

Red

Esau → Edom “Edom” means red. “Therefore was his name called Edom.” His descendants became the nation of Edom.

His skin was red at birth (verse 25), and now he asks for “that same red pottage”. In Hebrew the words are close to “that red stuff, that red stuff”, which is how a man talks when he is too hungry to ask properly.

Esau came in from the field “faint”: worn out and starving. Hold on to that word, because it matters for what he does next.

What a birthright was

The firstborn son received a double share of everything the father owned, and became the head of the family when his father died. For Isaac's family it was more. The firstborn was heir to God's promise to Abraham: the land, and the seed that would bless all nations.

What he got

One meal

A bowl of lentil stew and a piece of bread. Gone in ten minutes, and he would be hungry again by evening.

What he gave away

A lifetime, and a promise

The double share, the headship of the family, and the place in the line God was building.

Notice what Esau says: “I am at the point to die: and what profit shall this birthright do to me?” He wasn't dying. He was hungry. A man who is tired and starving feels like he is dying, and he starts to think nothing in the future matters.

What the New Testament says about Esau

“Lest there be any fornicator, or profane person, as Esau, who for one morsel of meat sold his birthright. For ye know how that afterward, when he would have inherited the blessing, he was rejected: for he found no place of repentance, though he sought it carefully with tears.”

Hebrews 12:16-17

The writer lists Esau with the sexually immoral, as one more man who gave up what is holy for what feels good right now. “Profane” means a man who treats holy things as worth nothing. “One morsel of meat” is one meal. Esau was not forced and not tricked. Verse 34 gives the verdict in four words: Esau despised his birthright. He ate, he drank, he got up, and he left.

Hebrews says he later wanted the blessing and cried for it (the story is in chapter 27), but he could not undo the trade. That is the sting of regret: sometimes the thing cannot be taken back.

Esau's stew is every man's story

Esau was hungry and worn out. Many recovery programs warn men not to get too Hungry, Angry, Lonely or Tired, because that is when a man trades his future for the next thing.

  1. One more drinkan hour of relief for what it costs you tomorrow
  2. One looka moment's pleasure against a marriage
  3. One more betthe rent for a thrill
  4. One angry worda minute's win against a son's trust

Each one is a bowl of stew: a small choice, and a large cost. When you are hungry, tired, angry or alone, call your brothers in Christ before you decide anything.

And if you have already eaten the stew?

You have your own trades behind you: years, money, people. Some of it cannot be undone, as it could not for Esau. Hebrews uses Esau as a warning, and a man should take it seriously.

Notice who the warning is aimed at. It is aimed at the man who stays careless, who despises what God gave him and does not care. It is not aimed at the man who sits ashamed over his past and wants to come back.

“…him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out.”

John 6:37

Jesus said that. Come to him as you are, with the stew already eaten. You cannot un-trade the past, but he receives the man who comes, and he does not throw him out. Then tell your brothers in Christ, and your pastor, and start again today.

Jacob was not innocent

It would be easy to say Esau was foolish and Jacob was wise. Read it again. Jacob saw a brother who was faint with hunger, and he named his price: sell me this day thy birthright. Then, to be safe, “swear to me this day”.

God had already told Rebekah that the elder would serve the younger (verse 23). Jacob did not have to bargain with a starving man to get what God had already promised him. He grabbed it, as his name said he would.

God keeps his word with men like Jacob in spite of them, and Jacob will pay for his schemes. God's promise never needed his trick.

Points to remember

  1. The promise“And Abraham gave all that he had unto Isaac.” 25:5The promise went to the son God chose, and the rest were not left empty-handed.
  2. A good death“Abraham gave up the ghost, and died in a good old age, an old man, and full of years” 25:8A man of faith dies full of years, and death is not the end.
  3. Brothers“his sons Isaac and Ishmael buried him” 25:9Broken families can still stand at the same grave. Start by turning up.
  4. God keeps his word“twelve princes according to their nations” 25:16God kept every word to Ishmael. The child of a mistake was still counted by name.
  5. Prayer“Isaac intreated the LORD for his wife” 25:21When you are waiting on a promise, ask and keep asking. Don't make your own plan.
  6. Appetite“thus Esau despised his birthright” 25:34When you are hungry, tired and in a hurry, don't trade your future for a bowl of stew.

Question 1 of 6

The promise

Whom did Abraham give “all that he had”, and what did he do for his other sons?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Everything went to Isaac, the son of the promise. The sons of Keturah and the other wives got gifts and a home in the east country, while Abraham still lived. He settled it himself, so there would be no fighting at his grave. Put your house in order while you can.

Question 2 of 6

A good death

How does Genesis describe Abraham's death?

What verses tell us about this?

“Precious in the sight of the LORD is the death of his saints.”

Psalm 116:15

Answer

“A good old age”, “full of years”, and “gathered to his people”. He died at 175, satisfied. A believer's death is precious to the LORD. If you are afraid of dying, or grieving someone who knew the Lord, hold on to that.

Question 3 of 6

Brothers

Who stood at Abraham's grave together?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Isaac and Ishmael: the son of the promise and the son sent away. They buried their father in the cave of Machpelah beside Sarah. Years of hurt were between them, but they were in the same place for that day. If you have a family you have not spoken to in a long time, you do not have to fix everything. Start by turning up.

Question 4 of 6

God keeps his word

Did God keep his promise about Ishmael?

What verses tell us about this?

“And as for Ishmael, I have heard thee: Behold, I have blessed him, and will make him fruitful, and will multiply him exceedingly; twelve princes shall he beget, and I will make him a great nation.”

Genesis 17:20

Answer

Yes, to the letter: twelve sons, twelve princes, a nation, all of them named. The son of a mistake was not forgotten. God does not lose track of the people the world forgets, and he keeps his word even to the ones nobody planned.

Question 5 of 6

Prayer

What did Isaac do when his wife could not have children, and what did God tell Rebekah?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Isaac asked the LORD for twenty years, and he answered. Rebekah took her worry to him too, and he told her two nations were in her womb. When you are waiting, ask the LORD instead of making your own plan.

Question 6 of 6

Appetite

What verses tell us about this?

Hebrews 12:16

Answer

His birthright: the double share, the headship of the family, and his place in God's promise. He was hungry and thought of nothing but now. When you are hungry, tired, angry or lonely, call your brothers in Christ before you decide anything.
