Genesis 21

The promised son, and the boy God heard

After twenty-five years of waiting, Isaac is born and Sarah laughs for joy. Then the household breaks over Hagar and Ishmael, a thirsty boy under a bush is heard by God, and Abraham makes peace at a well.

Things to watch for

Keep these questions in the back of your mind as we read. We'll come back to every one of them at the end.

Part one

At the set time

Verses 1–8

A hundred-year-old father, a ninety-year-old mother, and a promise kept to the day.

Twenty-five years

  1. about 2090 BCThe promiseAbram is 75, called out of Haran (12:4)
  2. about 2080 BCThe shortcutHagar, and Ishmael born the next year (ch. 16)
  3. about 2066 BC“The set time”“in the next year” (17:21)
  4. about 2065 BCIsaac is bornAbraham is “an hundred years old” (21:5)

Twenty-five years. Abraham was seventy-five when God first said it, and a hundred when it came true. The text says Sarah “conceived, and bare Abraham a son in his old age”. “Bare” is the old word for gave birth to.

Visited is an old way of saying that someone comes to look after you, and it is a kind word. “The LORD visited Sarah” means He came to her and did for her what He had said.

As he had said.

Verse 1 says it twice: “as he had said”, “as he had spoken”. Then verse 2 adds “at the set time”. God had named the month, so to speak, a year before (17:21), and He kept it.

And look at what Abraham does. He names the boy Isaac, as God had told him (17:19). He circumcises him on the eighth day, “as God had commanded him”. This time, no shortcut, no plan of his own. He simply does what God said.

If you are waiting on God for something (a repaired relationship, a clean year, a job, a child who comes home), He keeps His word on His own clock. The answer to a long wait is not another Hagar plan. It is to keep walking with Him, and keep your brothers close while you wait.

Two laughs

At the tent door (18:12)

The laugh of doubt

“Therefore Sarah laughed within herself, saying, After I am waxed old shall I have pleasure, my lord being old also?”

In the nursery (21:6)

The laugh of joy

“And Sarah said, God hath made me to laugh, so that all that hear will laugh with me.”

Sarah's first laugh was the laugh of a woman who had given up. God answered it with a son whose name, Isaac, means he laughs, chosen before he was conceived (17:19).

Hebrews remembers Sarah for something else, not the laugh and not the plan with Hagar: “she judged him faithful who had promised” (11:11). That is grace. God keeps the record of our faith, and He does not read out the rest.

Old words in verses 1–8

  1. visitedcame to her to help, as a good friend comes
  2. baregave birth to
  3. suckgave children suck: nursed babies at the breast
  4. weanedtaken off the breast and fed other food. In that world a child was often nursed for two or three years
  5. a great feasta party to mark that the boy had come through the dangerous early years

So Isaac is a small boy, about two or three, in verse 8. Remember that for what comes next: Ishmael, who was fourteen when Isaac was born, is a teenager by now.

Part two

Two boys, one house

Verses 9–21

The old shortcut comes due, and a mother and her son are sent into the wilderness.

“Mocking”

מְצַחֵק said metsacheq “laughing” (the same word-root as Isaac's name)

The word is built on the root of Isaac, “he laughs”. It can mean laughing, playing, or laughing at someone. Genesis does not say which. Paul, in Galatians 4:29, calls it persecution.

Picture the house. Ishmael is about sixteen, and the party is for the little brother who took his place. Sarah sees it and she remembers everything: the plan, the contempt, the years. Bondwoman means a woman slave, which is what Hagar was, and heir is the one who inherits.

Nobody in this house comes out of the story clean. And nobody in it is a villain either. It is a family wound that nobody healed, and now it has a deadline.

How a shortcut grows

  1. A shortcut“go in unto my maid” (16:2)
  2. A son born of itIshmael (16:15)
  3. Two rival mothers“her mistress was despised in her eyes” (16:4)
  4. A mockingat the feast (21:9)
  5. A boy sent away(21:14)

This is what a shortcut does. It takes fifteen minutes to make and fifteen years to clear up, and the bill falls on other people: a servant woman, a teenager, and an old man whose heart was “very grievous” (grievous means heavy, hard to bear).

God had already forgiven Abraham and walked with him (chapter 17). But grace does not erase consequences. It carries us through them. If your old shortcuts still have people attached to them (children, a wife, a family that stopped trusting you), don't hide from it. Name it, and walk through it with your brothers beside you.

What God told a troubled father

  1. “Let it not be grievous”God sees Abraham's pain, and He speaks to it before anything else
  2. “In Isaac shall thy seed be called”the promise stays on its road. The Saviour will come through Isaac
  3. “Also of the son of the bondwoman will I make a nation”and the other boy is not forgotten. “Because he is thy seed”

Hearken means listen and obey. God is not praising Sarah's temper. He is telling Abraham that this painful step lines up with what He had already planned, and that He will look after the boy Abraham is about to send away.

Abraham still had to do the hard thing. But he did it knowing God had promised to care for both boys. That is the difference between obeying in fear and obeying in faith.

A picture of the two ways

Born after the flesh

Ishmael: by a human plan

“He that was born after the flesh persecuted him that was born after the Spirit.”

Born by the promise

Isaac: by God's word

“We, brethren, as Isaac was, are the children of promise.”

“Now we, brethren, as Isaac was, are the children of promise. But as then he that was born after the flesh persecuted him that was born after the Spirit, even so it is now. Nevertheless what saith the scripture? Cast out the bondwoman and her son: for the son of the bondwoman shall not be heir with the son of the free woman.”

Galatians 4:28-30

Paul uses the two boys as a picture: nobody gets into God's family by his own plan and effort. It is a gift, by promise, received by faith in Jesus. He is not saying that Ishmael's people are cast off from God's care.

If you have trusted Jesus, you are on Isaac's side of that picture: a child of promise. You don't have to prove yourself every day to stay there.

The wilderness of Beer-sheba

A wilderness is empty country, not jungle: little water, little shade. This is the dry Negev, south of where Abraham lived. The “bottle” is a skin bag, “shrubs” are bushes, and a “bowshot” is as far as an arrow flies, perhaps a hundred yards.

Bread and a bag of water was a day or two of supplies, and then it was “spent”, used up. Abraham “rose up early in the morning” and did not drag it out, trusting God's promise about the boy (verse 13).

“Let me not see the death of the child.”

This is a mother at the very end. The water is gone, the boy is weak, and she cannot watch. So she sits down, a good way off, and weeps. There is no plan left.

Some of you know the place. You have watched someone you love go down, or you have been the one going down. It is the lowest point in this chapter, and God's help comes right into it.

If you are ever where Hagar was, don't sit down alone a bowshot away. Call or text your brothers in Christ, and your pastor. Tell them where you are, right then.

Ishmael: “God hears”

יִשְׁמָעֵאל said Yishma'el “God hears”

“And the angel of the LORD said unto her, Behold, thou art with child, and shalt bear a son, and shalt call his name Ishmael; because the LORD hath heard thy affliction.”

Genesis 16:11

God had named the boy long before, at the spring in chapter 16: God hears. Now, in a desert, with the water gone, that name was proved. “God heard the voice of the lad.”

Aileth is an old word: “What ails you? What is troubling you?” In verse 16, “lift up her voice” is old for “lifted”. It is a gentle question, and it comes “out of heaven”, with “fear not”. God does not scold a woman at the end of her rope. He asks what is wrong, and then He helps.

“The eyes of the LORD are upon the righteous, and his ears are open unto their cry.”

Psalm 34:15

What Hagar couldn't see

What she saw (v. 16)

A dead end

An empty bag. A boy under a bush. A road with nothing on it.

What God showed her (v. 19)

A well of water

“God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water.” It was there.

Notice that Genesis does not say God made a new well. It says He opened her eyes, and she saw it. The help may have been close the whole time. In despair, a person simply stops seeing it.

A man at the end of himself often can't see what is near: a brother who would answer the phone, a meeting down the road, a pastor who has been praying for him. Ask the men you trust what they see, and ask God to open your eyes. Then go and drink, and give the boy some too: “filled the bottle with water, and gave the lad drink”.

“God was with the lad.”

Five words, and the sent-away boy is all right. He grew up in the wilderness, became an archer, a hunter, and settled in the wilderness of Paran, the rocky desert of the Sinai side. His mother, who was Egyptian, found him a wife “out of the land of Egypt”.

God had said He would make Ishmael “a great nation” (verses 13, 18), and in chapter 25 we will see his twelve sons. He kept His word to the boy who was not the child of the promise.

Many of you have children who grew up in the wilderness of your choices. This is the verse to pray over them: God was with the lad. Pray for your children by name, and then leave them in His hands.

Part three

A well at Beer-sheba

Verses 22–34

A king asks for peace, and a well gets its name.

Who is watching you?

AbimelechGod is with thee in all that thou doest.

and thenSwear unto me here by God that thou wilt not deal falsely with me.

AbrahamI will swear.

Phichol was the commander of Abimelech's army, “the chief captain of his host” (a host is an army). Abimelech is the king of Gerar from chapter 20, the man Abraham once misled about Sarah. Now he asks Abraham for peace.

He can see that God is with this man “in all that thou doest”. Abraham was not perfect, but after twenty-five years the people around him could see where his strength came from. The men at your work and in your family are watching, too.

Old words in verses 22–34

  1. swear / swareto promise with an oath, calling God as witness
  2. sojournedlived as a guest in a land that was not his own
  3. deal falselycheat, lie, betray
  4. reprovedtold him plainly what he had done wrong
  5. violently taken awayseized by force
  6. ewe lambsfemale lambs
  7. grovea planted tree (many think a tamarisk), not a forest

Wot (verse 26) is old English for “know”: “I wot not who hath done this thing”, “I don't know who did this”.

Say it to his face

AbrahamYour servants have taken my well by force.

AbimelechI don't know who did this. You never told me, and I never heard of it until today.

thenAbraham gave him sheep and oxen, and both of them made a covenant.

In the desert a well is life, and a dug well is a man's claim to the country. This was not a small thing. Abraham did not swallow it, and he did not whisper about it. He went to the king and told him.

Tell him, plainly.

It turned out the king did not know. A great many of our resentments are like this: the other man doesn't know he did it, because nobody told him. Bottling it up helps nobody, and it will drive a man back to the old comforts.

So say it, plainly and without a fight. Or tell a brother first and ask how to say it. Abraham's way ended in a covenant, not a feud.

Beer-sheba

בְּאֵר שֶׁבַע said Be'er Sheva “well of the oath”, or “well of seven”

The Hebrew for seven and for swear are built from the same root, so the name says both: the well of the oath, and the well of the seven lambs. The text itself gives the reason: “there they sware both of them”.

Seven lambs were given as a witness, proof before everyone that Abraham had dug this well. He settled the question in public, with a gift, so nobody could argue about it later. We will see him do the same thing with a burial cave in chapter 23.

“The everlasting God”

אֵל עוֹלָם said El Olam “the everlasting God”, the God who does not change

“Hast thou not known? hast thou not heard, that the everlasting God, the LORD, the Creator of the ends of the earth, fainteth not, neither is weary? there is no searching of his understanding.”

Isaiah 40:28

This is the first time anyone in the Bible calls Him by this name. Abraham had just lived through twenty-five years of waiting, a family wound, and a dispute over water. What he reached for was the God who is the same yesterday, today, and always.

Notice what Abraham did once there was peace. He planted a tree, which is what a man does who plans to stay, and he called on the LORD. Peace with your neighbour is a good day to say thank you to God.

Question 1 of 6

A promise kept

Did God do what He said He would do, and when?

What verses tell us about this?

“For with God nothing shall be impossible.”

Luke 1:37

Answer

Yes, to the letter: “as he had said”, “as he had spoken”, “at the set time”. It was twenty-five years from the first promise, but nothing was late. If you are still waiting on God, the answer is not another shortcut. Keep trusting the One who keeps His word.

Question 2 of 6

Joy

What did Sarah's laughter turn into?

What verses tell us about this?

“Therefore Sarah laughed within herself, saying, After I am waxed old shall I have pleasure, my lord being old also?”

Genesis 18:12

Answer

At the tent door she laughed because it looked impossible. In the nursery she laughed because God had done it. He did not hold the first laugh against her: He named the boy Isaac, “he laughs”, and Hebrews remembers her for this: “she judged him faithful who had promised.”

Question 3 of 6

Consequences

Where did the trouble in Abraham's house come from?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

From the shortcut in chapter 16. Abram and Sarai took the promise into their own hands, and years later the result was a rival mother, a teenage son, and a house that could not hold them both. Grace does not erase consequences. It carries us through them. Own your old shortcuts, and don't carry them alone.

Question 4 of 6

God hears

Did God forget Hagar and her boy when they were sent away?

What verses tell us about this?

“The eyes of the LORD are upon the righteous, and his ears are open unto their cry.”

Psalm 34:15

Answer

No. A woman and a boy with an empty water bag were as far from help as anyone can be, and God heard. “Ishmael” means “God hears”. Nobody is too far out in the wilderness to be heard. If you are there, don't sit down alone. Call or text your brothers in Christ, and your pastor.

Question 5 of 6

Help at hand

What did Hagar not see until God opened her eyes?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

A well. Genesis does not say God dug a new one. It says He opened her eyes and she saw it. At the end of yourself, help may be nearer than you think: a brother who will pick up the phone, a meeting, a pastor, the Lord Himself. Ask them what they see, and ask God to open your eyes.

Question 6 of 6

A peacemaker

How did Abraham settle his quarrel with Abimelech?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

He said it to the man's face, instead of stewing in it. Abimelech had not known, so they made a covenant at a well named Beer-sheba. Then Abraham planted a tree and “called there on the name of the LORD, the everlasting God.” Say the hard thing to the person, not about him, and when it is settled, worship.
