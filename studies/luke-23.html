Luke 23

To day in paradise

Found innocent again and again, Jesus is crucified anyway. He prays for the men nailing Him there, and promises a dying thief paradise.

Part one

I find no fault in this man

Verses 1–25

Two rulers look Him over. Neither can find a crime. The crowd shouts louder.

Three charges

“Perverting the nation”?FalseHe taught men to love God and their neighbour. He healed their sick and fed their hungry.
“Forbidding to give tribute to Caesar”?FalseA few days earlier He'd said the opposite, in the temple: “Render therefore unto Caesar the things which be Caesar’s” (Luke 20:25).
“He himself is Christ a King”?TrueBut not a rebel against Rome. “Thou sayest it” means yes, it is as you say.

The night before, the council condemned Him for saying He was the Son of God (22:70–71). Rome didn't care about that, so in front of the Roman governor they turned it into politics: a tax rebel and a rival king.

Two rulers, one city

Pilate was the Roman governor of Judaea and Samaria, from about AD 26 to 36. He lived on the coast at Caesarea and came up to Jerusalem for the feasts, to keep order.

Herod Antipas, a son of Herod the Great, ruled Galilee and Perea for Rome. He was in Jerusalem for the Passover too. When Pilate heard “Galilee,” he saw a way out: send the problem to someone else.

Herod was the man who had beheaded John the Baptist (Luke 9:9).

He answered him nothing

Herod was “exceeding glad.” He'd wanted to see Jesus for a long time, the way a man wants to see a magic act. He asked question after question. Jesus didn't say one word.

“He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth: he is brought as a lamb to the slaughter, and as a sheep before her shearers is dumb, so he openeth not his mouth.”

Isaiah 53:7

Herod had already heard God's word from John, and he silenced the messenger. Jesus doesn't perform for a man who wants a show and not a Saviour. Anyone who honestly comes to Him for help gets an answer. Herod only wanted to be entertained.

Friends that same day

Before

At enmity

Pilate had once killed Galilaeans, Herod's own people, at the temple (Luke 13:1). That may be part of the bad blood.

After

Made friends

Two proud men found something to agree on: making fun of Jesus. A gorgeous robe was a bright, shining one, dressing Him up as a joke king.

To set at nought is to treat as nothing. Herod and his soldiers treated the King of kings as a nobody. Men still bond over mocking Him. It's a cheap kind of friendship.

Punish Him anyway

Listen to Pilate's reasoning: No fault. Nothing worthy of death. Therefore I'll whip Him. To chastise here meant a beating with a Roman whip, cruel enough on its own. He hoped a bloody prisoner would satisfy the crowd.

Verse 17 tells of a custom: at the Passover feast the governor let one prisoner go free. Pilate hoped they'd ask for Jesus.

Pilate knew what was right, said it out loud three times, and didn't do it. A man who knows the truth and won't stand on it ends up going wherever the loudest voices push him.

Barabbas walks free

Luke tells us twice what Barabbas had done: sedition, a violent uprising in the city, and murder (19, 25). He was exactly what they'd falsely accused Jesus of being.

The crosscondemned

The door outreleased

Barabbasguilty: sedition and murder
Jesus“no cause of death in him”

Then they trade places. The guilty man walks out the prison door. The innocent One goes to the cross. Every one of us is Barabbas.

Part two

The place called Calvary

Verses 26–38

A stranger carries the cross. Jesus prays for the men who drive the nails.

A man from Cyrene

Jesus had been beaten all night and couldn't carry the beam any farther. The soldiers grabbed a man coming in from the country. Cyrene was a city in North Africa, in today's Libya, with a large Jewish community. Simon had likely come a long way for the Passover.

He didn't choose it, but Luke chooses his words: he carried it “after Jesus,” walking behind Him. Mark names his sons, Alexander and Rufus (Mark 15:21), as if the church knew them. It seems that day changed the family.

“And he said to them all, If any man will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross daily, and follow me.”

Luke 9:23

Green wood and dry

Even on the road to His death, Jesus is thinking about others. He tells the weeping women to save their tears for the trouble coming on their city.

Green wood doesn't burn easily. Dry wood goes up at once. If Rome does this to an innocent man, what will happen when the nation that rejected Him rebels? About forty years later, in AD 70, Roman armies destroyed Jerusalem and the temple.

“…and they shall say to the mountains, Cover us; and to the hills, Fall on us.”

Hosea 10:8

Verse 30 picks up the words of Hosea: people so desperate they'd rather be buried than face what's coming. Jesus wept over this city once already (19:41). He's still grieving for it on the way to His own cross.

Among the transgressors

Calvary comes from the Latin word for a skull. It's the same place the other gospels call Golgotha, “the place of a skull”: just outside the city wall, by a road, where everyone passing could see.

Luke says only “there they crucified him.” It was Rome's slowest, most shameful death. The gospels don't dwell on the pain. They want us to see who was on the cross, and why.

A malefactor is a criminal. They put Jesus in the middle, as if He were the worst of the three, just as Isaiah wrote:

“…and he was numbered with the transgressors; and he bare the sin of many, and made intercession for the transgressors.”

Isaiah 53:12

Father, forgive them

“Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do.”

The first thing Jesus says from the cross is a prayer, and it's for the men hurting Him: the soldiers with the hammer, the leaders who planned it, the crowd that shouted. That's the intercession for the transgressors Isaiah wrote about.

“They know not” doesn't mean they were innocent. They had no idea Whom they were killing. Seven weeks later, thousands in this city repented and were forgiven (Acts 2).

Some of us carry what was done to us. Some of us carry what we did. Jesus prayed this for both kinds of men. If He could forgive the men with the nails, there's no one He can't forgive, and no one He can't help us forgive.

Save thyself

Three times they throw the same words at Him:

  • “let him save himself”the rulers, v. 35
  • “save thyself”the soldiers, v. 37
  • “save thyself and us”one of the criminals, v. 39

They were right about one thing: He saved others. And He could have saved Himself. Twelve legions of angels were a word away (Matthew 26:53). But He couldn't save Himself and save us. So He stayed.

The vinegar was the soldiers' cheap sour wine, offered here as part of the mocking.

In three languages

A superscription is the notice nailed above the condemned, naming his crime. Pilate wrote Jesus' crime this way, in every language of the crowd:

Βασιλεύς
Basileus, king
Greek: the trade and the street
Rex
king
Latin: Rome and the soldiers
מֶלֶךְ
Melek, king
Hebrew: the temple and the Jews

They meant it as a joke and a warning. It was the plain truth, announced to the whole world. The King was on His throne, and it was a cross.

Written long before

Nobody at the cross was trying to fulfil prophecy. Soldiers gambled, rulers sneered, and every word came true anyway:

  1. ✓he was numbered with the transgressorsIsaiah 53:1223:32–33
  2. ✓and made intercession for the transgressorsIsaiah 53:1223:34
  3. ✓They part my garments among them, and cast lots upon my vesture.Psalm 22:1823:34
  4. ✓All they that see me laugh me to scornPsalm 22:723:35–37
  5. ✓in my thirst they gave me vinegar to drinkPsalm 69:2123:36

The cross wasn't a plan that went wrong. It was God's plan, kept to the letter.

Part three

Lord, remember me

Verses 39–49

A dying criminal asks for one thing, and gets everything. Then the sun goes dark.

Two men, the same distance from Jesus

At first both criminals mocked Him (Matthew 27:44). One kept on railing: cursing and insulting.

The other changed his mind, there on the cross. He feared God, owned his guilt (“we indeed justly”), and saw Jesus was innocent.

Same hill, same sentence. One died cursing Him. The other went with Him to paradise.

What did he bring?

  • A clean recordhe said himself he deserved to die
  • Good workshis hands were nailed down
  • Baptism, church, a changed life firsthe had a few hours left
  • Time to make it all rightthere wasn't any
  • “Lord, remember me”faith in Jesus, and nothing else

He called a dying man Lord, and believed He'd come into a kingdom. That was all. It was enough.

“For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: Not of works, lest any man should boast.”

Ephesians 2:8–9

To day, with me, in paradise

  1. “To day”not some day, after he'd earned it. Before the sun went down.
  2. “with me”not just a place. In the company of Jesus Himself.
  3. “in paradise”the word means a garden or park. The presence of God.

“We are confident, I say, and willing rather to be absent from the body, and to be present with the Lord.”

2 Corinthians 5:8

He asked to be remembered someday. Jesus gave him more than he asked. Some of us think we've waited too long, or done too much. This man was a convicted criminal with hours to live. It isn't too late for anyone still breathing.

Darkness at noon

  1. the third hourabout 9 a.m.: crucifiedMark 15:25
  2. “Father, forgive them”the soldiers cast lots23:34
  3. the sixth hourabout noon: darkness23:44
  4. the ninth hourabout 3 p.m.: He dies23:44–46
  5. before sundownlaid in the tomb23:53–54

The Jews counted hours from sunrise, about 6 a.m. So for three hours in the middle of the day, the land went dark. It wasn't an eclipse: Passover always fell at the full moon, when the moon is on the far side of the earth and can't block the sun.

In the Bible, darkness at noon is a sign of God's judgment (Amos 8:9). On the cross, Jesus was carrying the judgment for our sin.

The veil was rent

The veil was a huge, heavy curtain in the temple. Behind it was the holiest place, where only the high priest could go, once a year, with blood (Hebrews 9:7). It said to everyone else: keep out.

When Jesus died, God tore it in two. The way to God is open, for any man who comes through Jesus.

“Having therefore, brethren, boldness to enter into the holiest by the blood of Jesus, By a new and living way, which he hath consecrated for us, through the veil, that is to say, his flesh;”

Hebrews 10:19–20

Into thy hands

Luke gives us three things Jesus said on the cross. Two begin with Father.

  1. “Father, forgive them”for His enemies (23:34)
  2. “To day … with me in paradise”for a sinner who believed (23:43)
  3. “Father, into thy hands”for Himself, trusting His Father (23:46)

“Into thine hand I commit my spirit: thou hast redeemed me…”

Psalm 31:5

He prayed a psalm, with a loud voice, not the whisper of a man who's given out. He gave up His life on purpose, when He chose (John 10:18). And He shows us how a believer can face death: in the Father's hands.

Not guilty

A centurion was a Roman officer over about a hundred men. He'd seen many men die. He'd never seen one die like this. Count how often Luke has had someone say it:

  1. Pilate“I find no fault in this man” (23:4)
  2. Pilate“have found no fault in this man” (23:14)
  3. Herod“nothing worthy of death” (23:15)
  4. Pilate“I have found no cause of death in him” (23:22)
  5. The thief“this man hath done nothing amiss” (23:41)
  6. The centurion“Certainly this was a righteous man” (23:47)

Six times. He didn't die for anything He'd done. He died for what we've done. The crowd went home beating their chests, grieving. His friends stood at a distance and watched it all.

Part four

Laid in a sepulchre

Verses 50–56

A council member steps out of the crowd, and the women stay to the end.

Joseph steps out

A counsellor was a member of the council, the same council that condemned Jesus. Joseph hadn't gone along with it. Arimathaea was his home town in Judaea; nobody knows exactly where it stood.

Going to Pilate and asking for the body of a man just executed as a rebel was a risk. Joseph put his name and his place on the line, and laid Him in his own new tomb (Matthew 27:60).

The women saw where

  1. Friday, 3 p.m.He dies23:46
  2. before sundownburied; the women watch23:53–55
  3. Friday sundownthe sabbath begins23:54
  4. Saturdaythey rest, by the commandment23:56
  5. Sunday, very earlyback with the spices24:1

The preparation was Friday, the day to get ready for the sabbath, which began at sundown. There was just time to wrap the body and lay it in the tomb.

The disciples had scattered. These women from Galilee stayed: at the cross, at the tomb, and back again on Sunday. They saw exactly which tomb, and how He was laid. Nobody went to the wrong grave on Sunday morning.

Friday isn't the end

Luke 23 ends with a stone in front of a tomb and the women resting through a sad sabbath. Every one of His friends thought it was over.

But look at what the cross did. The guilty went free. A criminal went to paradise. The curtain that kept men out came down. All because the innocent One took the place of the guilty.

And Jesus had told them more than once: the Son of man would be killed, “and the third day he shall rise again” (18:33). Sunday is coming.

Points to remember

  1. Innocent“I find no fault in this man” 23:4Six times He's called innocent. He died for our sins, not His.
  2. In our place“Release unto us Barabbas” 23:18A guilty man walked free while Jesus took his cross. So do we.
  3. Forgiveness“Father, forgive them” 23:34He prayed for the men driving the nails. No one is beyond His forgiveness.
  4. Empty hands“Lord, remember me” 23:42The thief brought nothing but faith, and was promised paradise that day.
  5. The way in“The veil of the temple was rent” 23:45The way to God is open to any man who comes through Jesus.
  6. Faithful“Beheld the sepulchre, and how his body was laid” 23:55Joseph stepped out, and the women stayed to the end.

Question 1 of 6

Innocent

How many times is Jesus called innocent in this chapter?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Six times: Pilate three times (4, 14, 22), Herod through Pilate (15), the thief (41), and the centurion (47). Jesus had no sin of His own to die for. He died for ours.

Question 2 of 6

In our place

Who walked free when Jesus was condemned?

What verses tell us about this?

“For Christ also hath once suffered for sins, the just for the unjust, that he might bring us to God, being put to death in the flesh, but quickened by the Spirit:”

1 Peter 3:18

Answer

Barabbas, a man in prison for sedition and murder. The guilty man went free and the innocent One died. That's the gospel: the just for the unjust, Jesus in our place.

Question 3 of 6

Forgiveness

Who did Jesus pray for from the cross?

What verses tell us about this?

“…and he bare the sin of many, and made intercession for the transgressors.”

Isaiah 53:12

Answer

The very men who put Him there: the soldiers, the rulers, the crowd. They didn't know Whom they were killing. If He prayed for them, there's no man He won't forgive, and He can help us forgive too.

Question 4 of 6

Empty hands

What did the dying thief bring to Jesus?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Nothing but an honest confession and faith. No good works, no time to fix his life. He owned his guilt, called Jesus Lord, and asked to be remembered. Jesus promised him paradise that same day. That's how every man is saved: by grace, through faith.

Question 5 of 6

The way in

What happened to the veil of the temple?

What verses tell us about this?

“Having therefore, brethren, boldness to enter into the holiest by the blood of Jesus, By a new and living way, which he hath consecrated for us, through the veil, that is to say, his flesh;”

Hebrews 10:19–20

Answer

It was torn in the middle when Jesus died. The curtain that kept sinners out of God's presence came down. Now any man can come to God through Jesus' blood, with boldness.

Question 6 of 6

Faithful

Who cared for Jesus' body when it was over?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Joseph of Arimathaea, a council member who hadn't agreed with their verdict, risked going to Pilate and laid Jesus in his own new tomb. And the women from Galilee watched where He was laid, then prepared spices for Him.
