Colossians 4

Pray, speak, and know your people

Paul closes with how to pray and how to talk, and then a roll call: a runaway slave, a man who quit, a doctor, and one who would walk away. Every name is a story about grace.

This is the last chapter. Paul wrote the letter from Rome, about AD 60 to 62, chained to a soldier in a rented house (Acts 28:16, 30). Chapter 3 showed what the new life looks like. Chapter 4 shows it in real people.

It starts with one more line for the boss, then prayer and speech, then ten names. Don't skip the names. They are the best part: each one is a man God did something with.

Part one

Pray and speak

Verses 1–6

What you say to God, and what you say to everybody else.

The boss has a Boss.

This finishes chapter 3's household. “Just and equal” means fair pay, fair treatment, and fair play. Paul told the slave to work as for the Lord. Now he tells the owner that he answers to the Lord too.

If any of you supervise a crew, a team, or a couple of guys on a job, this is your verse. Pay what you promised, say thanks, and don't lean on a man because you can.

Pray like a sentry

Three words. “Continue”: keep at it, don't pray once and quit. “Watch”: a sentry's word for staying awake on the wall. And “with thanksgiving.”

A watchman is looking for what's coming at him. For us that means seeing the pull before it arrives: the dark hour, the old route home, the text you know is coming. Pray about it before, not after.

And thank God out loud for what He has already done. A thankful man is hard to knock over.

Pray for the door

Look at what Paul asks for. He's in chains, and he doesn't say “pray that I get out.” He says “pray … that God would open unto us a door of utterance.” He wants a way to speak about Christ.

That's a prayer worth stealing. Don't only pray for the chains to come off. Pray for a door: one honest conversation, one man at work who's ready to hear it. A man in a bad spot can still be useful.

“And for me, that utterance may be given unto me, that I may open my mouth boldly, to make known the mystery of the gospel, For which I am an ambassador in bonds: that therein I may speak boldly, as I ought to speak.”

Ephesians 6:19–20

Salt on your words

“Them that are without” means people outside the church: the neighbor, the boss, the old friend who is watching how you've changed. Salt was in every kitchen, and Paul counts on that:

1It flavorsPlain, true talk beats bland talk.
2It keepsIt stops words from going bad.
3It stingsA little truth, on the right spot, hurts and helps.
4Not too muchSalt, not the whole shaker.

“A soft answer turneth away wrath: but grievous words stir up anger.”

Proverbs 15:1

Buying back the time

“Redeeming” is a market word: to buy up, to snap up a bargain. Time here means an opportunity. Paul sees a day full of hours and says, buy up the good ones.

Sixteen waking hours. Most go to the clock and the couch. Four, bought back on purpose, change the day.

five minutes of prayer first thinga kind word on the joba call to a brotherwalking away from the old route

Paul was in prison, and he still counted his hours. Every man here has time he used to burn. It can be bought back, one hour at a time.

Part two

The faithful band

Verses 7–14

Eight men, and every one a story about grace.

Two men and a letter

Tychicus carried the letter from Rome to Colosse, more than a thousand miles. He carried the letter we call Ephesians too, and he came to “comfort your hearts.” A mailman who is also a pastor.

He didn't travel alone. “With Onesimus,” says verse 9. We'll meet him in a moment.

“But that ye also may know my affairs, and how I do, Tychicus, a beloved brother and faithful minister in the Lord, shall make known to you all things: Whom I have sent unto you for the same purpose, that ye might know our affairs, and that he might comfort your hearts.”

Ephesians 6:21–22

A runaway, now a brother

a runaway slave → a faithful and beloved brother “who is one of you.” He had run from Colosse, and Colosse is now told he belongs to them again.

Onesimus belonged to Philemon, probably a believer in Colosse. He ran off, maybe with money, and ended up in Rome. There he met Paul, and there he met Christ. His name means useful (Philemon 11).

“For perhaps he therefore departed for a season, that thou shouldest receive him for ever; Not now as a servant, but above a servant, a brother beloved, specially to me, but how much more unto thee, both in the flesh, and in the Lord?”

Philemon 15–16

Paul steps in

A runaway could be punished hard under Roman law. Paul sends Onesimus home carrying a letter, and Paul puts his own name on the debt:

Onesimusowes

Paulpays

Onesimuslost time, maybe lost money, a broken trust
Paul“put that on mine account”

Philemon 18–19: “If he hath wronged thee, or oweth thee ought, put that on mine account … I will repay it.” The guilty man's debt lands on the one who didn't owe it.

That is the gospel in small. Christ didn't owe it either, and He wrote our names on His account. And a man who once stole from his boss gets to go home as a brother.

The man who quit

Marcus is Mark, John Mark, the cousin of Barnabas (the KJV says “sister's son”). Early on he quit in the middle of the first missionary trip. When Barnabas wanted to give him another chance, Paul said no, and the two friends split over it.

Quit once. Useful again.

Paul had every reason to keep Mark on the bench. Years later he tells the whole church at Colosse: “if he come unto you, receive him.” Near the end of his life he asked for him by name:

“Only Luke is with me. Take Mark, and bring him with thee: for he is profitable to me for the ministry.”

2 Timothy 4:11

Early Christians held that this same Mark wrote the Gospel that carries his name. If you've quit on a job, a program or a person, that is not your last chapter.

Verse 11: of the Jewish believers, only Aristarchus, Marcus and Jesus Justus (a common name) were working beside Paul, “a comfort unto me.” Three men, and they mattered more than a crowd.

Fighting on his knees

Epaphras was from Colosse (“one of you”). He started the church there and then came to Rome, and he's in prison with Paul (Philemon 23). He can't walk home, so he prays his way home.

“Labouring fervently” is the word for an athlete's struggle, the root of our word agony. He prays for three towns, by name, always, so that they “stand perfect and complete in all the will of God.”

Somebody prayed for you like that. Be that man for somebody today.

A doctor who stayed.

Luke, “the beloved physician,” a doctor, a Gentile and a careful writer. By Christian tradition he wrote the Gospel of Luke and Acts. The “we” in Acts 27 and 28 includes him: he was on the ship in the storm with Paul and he came to Rome with him.

He is still there at the end. In Paul's last letter, written from a cold Roman prison, he says: “Only Luke is with me.” A man who stays when it's easy to go is worth more than gold.

Be Luke for someone: the one who shows up on the bad day, doesn't say much, and is still there next month.

And Demas

  1. About AD 60 to 62Paul writes Colossians: “Luke, the beloved physician, and Demas, greet you.”Col. 4:14
  2. The same yearsPhilemon lists Demas among Paul's “fellowlabourers.”Philemon 24
  3. About AD 66 to 67Paul's last letter: “Demas hath forsaken me, having loved this present world.”2 Tim. 4:10

Here's the sad one. Demas worked beside Paul and was counted a co-worker, and later he drifted back to the world he'd left. It doesn't say he lost his salvation, and it doesn't tell us how his story ended. Only that he went.

It's a gentle warning. Nobody falls all at once. It starts with loving the present world a little more than the people who pray for you. If you feel that pull, tell a brother before you've gone, not after.

Part three

Pass it on

Verses 15–18

Share the letter, finish the job, and remember who is in chains.

A church in a house

Three towns in one valley, within a day's walk of each other. “Nymphas, and the church which is in his house”: the church met in somebody's living room. A church doesn't need a building. It needs people who show up.

Verse 16 is the first time a letter of Paul's was told to be passed round. Colosse reads Laodicea's letter, and Laodicea reads this one. We don't know what that other letter was. Many think Ephesians, but nobody knows.

Take heed. Fulfil it.

Archippus is named in Philemon 2 as “our fellowsoldier,” probably a leader in that church (some think Philemon's son). Nobody knows what exactly his “ministry” was, only that God handed him a job and he had to finish it.

“Fulfil” means fill to the full, like those dots. Starting is easy. The word is for men who finish. Whatever God has put in your hands (a job, a family, a few men to look after), take heed to it, and do it all the way.

In his own hand

Paul dictated most of his letters, and here he takes the pen himself. His hand was chained to a soldier's when he signed.

“Remember my bonds” isn't a plea for pity. It's: keep praying, keep standing with me. The man in chains wrote the most joyful letters in the New Testament.

And the last word is “Grace.” The letter began with it and ends with it. Everything in between rests on that word.

The whole letter, in four stops

  1. Christ over allThe image of the invisible God, who made peace by His cross.“Christ in you, the hope of glory”
  2. Complete in HimDon't trade Him for rules and clever ideas.“ye are complete in him”
  3. New clothesLook up, put off the old, put on the new, with love around it.“put on charity”
  4. Prayer and peoplePray for the door, salt your words, know your people.“Continue in prayer”

Notice the order. Colossians never says “try harder.” It begins with who Christ is and what He finished, and only then tells you how to live. Doctrine first, clothes second. The man in chains and the runaway slave both got that order right.

Five things to carry out the door

Sin

Named plainly in chapter 3: the temper, the lust, the lies. Not hidden, and not final.

Regret

Mark quit, and Paul said no. Years later: “profitable to me for the ministry.”

Grace

“Complete in him,” and “having forgiven you all trespasses.” Paul's last word is Grace.

Hope

“Your life is hid with Christ in God.” It will show.

Today

Pray for one door. Say one gracious thing. Tell one man the truth.

Points to remember

  1. Prayer“Continue in prayer, and watch in the same with thanksgiving” 4:2Keep at it, stay alert for what's coming, and thank God out loud. Pray for a door, not only for the chains to come off.
  2. Words“Let your speech be alway with grace, seasoned with salt” 4:6Honest talk with grace in it, for the people outside who are watching how you've changed.
  3. Onesimus“A faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you” 4:9The runaway comes home a brother, because Paul put the debt on his own account.
  4. Marcus“If he come unto you, receive him” 4:10A man who quit gets another chance, and ends up useful.
  5. Faithful friends“Epaphras … always labouring fervently for you in prayers” 4:12Be Epaphras and be Luke. Take Demas as a gentle warning: stay close to the people who pray for you.
  6. The last word“Take heed to the ministry which thou hast received in the Lord, that thou fulfil it” 4:17Finish what God gave you, remember those in chains, and rest on grace.

Question 1 of 6

Prayer

How does Paul tell believers to pray?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Keep at it, stay alert, and give thanks. Pray for open doors to speak about Christ, even from a bad spot, and pray hard for other people by name, the way Epaphras did.

Question 2 of 6

Words

How should a believer talk to people outside the church?

What verses tell us about this?

“A soft answer turneth away wrath: but grievous words stir up anger.”

Proverbs 15:1

Answer

With wisdom and with grace: honest, seasoned talk, the kind that makes a man curious instead of defensive. Buy up the opportunities you're given, and be ready to answer.

Question 3 of 6

Onesimus

What happened to the runaway slave Onesimus?

What verses tell us about this?

“For perhaps he therefore departed for a season, that thou shouldest receive him for ever; Not now as a servant, but above a servant, a brother beloved, specially to me, but how much more unto thee, both in the flesh, and in the Lord?”

Philemon 15–16

Answer

He met Christ in Paul's prison, and Paul sent him home with a letter and an offer to pay what he owed (Philemon 18). The runaway returned as “a brother beloved,” one of them.

Question 4 of 6

Marcus

Can a man who quit get a second chance?

What verses tell us about this?

“But Paul thought not good to take him with them, who departed from them from Pamphylia, and went not with them to the work.”

Acts 15:38

“Only Luke is with me. Take Mark, and bring him with thee: for he is profitable to me for the ministry.”

2 Timothy 4:11

Answer

Yes. Mark quit, and Paul refused to take him. Years later Paul told a church to receive him, and asked for him by name: “profitable to me for the ministry.” A failure is not a verdict.

Question 5 of 6

Faithful friends

Who stood by Paul, and who walked away?

What verses tell us about this?

“For Demas hath forsaken me, having loved this present world, and is departed unto Thessalonica; Crescens to Galatia, Titus unto Dalmatia.”

2 Timothy 4:10

“Only Luke is with me. Take Mark, and bring him with thee: for he is profitable to me for the ministry.”

2 Timothy 4:11

Answer

Luke stayed to the end, and Mark came back. Demas, who greeted the Colossians beside Luke, later “loved this present world” and left. We aren't told his end, only to take heed and keep close to the brothers.

Question 6 of 6

The last word

What does Paul ask of the church as he closes?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Pass the letter on, finish the work God gave you, and remember those in chains, which means keep praying. The last word is grace, the word the letter began with.
