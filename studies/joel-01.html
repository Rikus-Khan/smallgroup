Joel 1

When the locusts came

A plague strips Judah bare, and a prophet calls the whole nation to stop, weep, and cry out to the LORD.

The book of Joel

Who, when, and to whom

Joel's name means “the LORD is God.” All we know about him is his father's name, Pethuel. He spoke to Judah, the southern kingdom, and its capital, Jerusalem: he talks about Zion, the temple, and its priests. A swarm of locusts and a drought had just stripped the land bare.

  1. about 930 BCThe kingdom splits: Israel north, Judah south
  2. about 835 BC?Joel, in one view: a boy king, Joash, and the priests lead
  3. 722 BCAssyria takes Israel, the north
  4. 586 BCBabylon takes Jerusalem; the exile
  5. about 500–400 BC?Joel, in another view: back from exile, under Persia

Nobody knows for sure when Joel wrote. He names no king, so Bible teachers disagree, and good men hold both dates. The message doesn't depend on it: God sent a disaster as a warning, called His people back, and promised to restore them.

Part one

Nothing left

Verses 1–12

Wave after wave of locusts, until the vines are bare and the joy is gone.

Tell it down the line

Joel starts with the old men: Have you ever seen anything like this? Did your fathers? Nobody had. Then he says: make sure your children hear about it, and theirs, and theirs.

ye old mentellyour childrentelltheir childrentellanother generation

“We will not hide them from their children, shewing to the generation to come the praises of the LORD, and his strength, and his wonderful works that he hath done.”

Psalm 78:4

What God did in the hard years is meant to be passed on. That includes your story: what you went through, and how God met you in it. Somebody coming up behind you needs to hear it.

Wave after wave

  1. the fieldfull and green
  2. palmerworm“the cutter”
  3. locust“the swarmer”
  4. cankerworm“the licker”
  5. caterpiller“the finisher”: nothing left

These are four Hebrew words for locusts. Bible teachers aren't sure if they are four kinds, four stages of growth, or four waves of one swarm. The point is plain either way: what one wave left, the next one ate, until nothing was left. A big swarm can strip a field in hours.

Some men know losses that came in waves: the job, then the marriage, then the health, then the self-respect. Joel doesn't pretend it isn't bad. But hold on: these same four insects show up again in chapter 2, in one of the best promises in the Bible.

Wake up

Joel pictures the locusts as an invading army with a lion's teeth. They strip the vines and peel the bark off the fig trees until the branches are white. No grapes, no new wine. And the first men to notice are the drunkards.

What he ran to

Cut off

The thing a man leans on can be gone overnight. When it is, he finally sees how much it owned him.

What God offers

A better filling

Chapter 2 promises God's Spirit poured out. Paul puts the two side by side.

“And be not drunk with wine, wherein is excess; but be filled with the Spirit;”

Ephesians 5:18

The morning it's gone

“Awake” is a call, not a sneer. God isn't mocking the drunkard. He's shaking him. Jesus told about a young man who did the same thing:

“And when he had spent all, there arose a mighty famine in that land; and he began to be in want.”

Luke 15:14

“And when he came to himself, he said, How many hired servants of my father’s have bread enough and to spare, and I perish with hunger!”

Luke 15:17

The famine is what brought him “to himself.” If you've woken up with nothing left, that may be the morning God is calling you. The son went home, and the father ran to meet him.

Even the worship stopped

In 1611, “meat” meant food. The meat offering was a grain offering of flour and oil. The drink offering was wine poured out at the altar. Every morning and evening the priests offered them with a lamb. Look what the locusts took:

  1. The corngrain for bread and for the offering: “wasted”
  2. The new winefor the drink offering: “dried up”
  3. The oilfor the offering and for lamps: “languisheth”

With no harvest, there was nothing to bring to God. Joel says to grieve like a young woman whose bridegroom died before the wedding: she was promised to him, dressed in mourning sackcloth, and her whole future was gone.

Joy is withered away.

The farmers (“husbandmen”) and the men who kept the vineyards stand in dead fields. Vine, fig, pomegranate, palm, apple: everything that gave food, shade, and sweetness has dried up. And the joy dried up with it.

Plenty of men know those gray days: early sobriety, after a loss, when nothing feels good. That's real, and it's not the end. A later prophet faced the same empty fields and found a joy no locust could eat:

“Although the fig tree shall not blossom, neither shall fruit be in the vines; the labour of the olive shall fail, and the fields shall yield no meat; the flock shall be cut off from the fold, and there shall be no herd in the stalls: Yet I will rejoice in the LORD, I will joy in the God of my salvation.”

Habakkuk 3:17–18

Part two

Cry unto the LORD

Verses 13–20

Joel's answer to disaster isn't a plan. It's a nation on its face before God.

Getting low before God

  1. gird yourselveswith sackcloth
  2. lie all nightin sackcloth
  3. sanctify a fastset a day apart
  4. call an assemblygather everyone
  5. cry unto the LORDtogether

Sackcloth was rough goat-hair cloth worn for mourning. To “sanctify a fast” meant setting apart a day to go without food and seek God. A “solemn assembly” was a sacred gathering where everyone stopped work. The priests go first, then the elders, then all the people.

“Humble yourselves in the sight of the Lord, and he shall lift you up.”

James 4:10

Notice it's “gather.” Nobody gets low alone in a corner. They cry out together. A man doesn't climb out by himself either.

“The day of the LORD”

This is the big idea of Joel. The day of the LORD is a time when God Himself steps in to judge. Joel sees the locusts as a warning shot, a small picture of a greater day still to come. He uses the phrase five times:

  1. “at hand”Joel 1:15
  2. “nigh at hand”Joel 2:1
  3. “great and very terrible”Joel 2:11
  4. “the great and the terrible”Joel 2:31
  5. “near in the valley of decision”Joel 3:14

In Hebrew it's a play on words: shod (destruction) from Shaddai (the Almighty). But a warning is a kindness. God tells people before He acts, because He wants them to turn.

“The Lord is not slack concerning his promise, as some men count slackness; but is longsuffering to us-ward, not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance.”

2 Peter 3:9

When there's nothing left

After the locusts came a drought. The seed shriveled in the dry ground. The storehouses (“garners”) stood empty and the barns fell apart, because there was nothing to put in them. The cattle wandered, confused. Heat and fire burned the pastures, and the streams dried up.

  • give upJoel doesn't
  • run back to the old thingthe vines are bare anyway
  • “O LORD, to thee will I cry”even the animals cry out to God

“And call upon me in the day of trouble: I will deliver thee, and thou shalt glorify me.”

Psalm 50:15

The chapter ends with no rain yet, only a man praying. That's often where it starts. Chapter 2 is God's answer.

Points to remember

  1. Telling the story“Tell ye your children of it” 1:3What God did in the hard years is meant to be passed on, your story included.
  2. Wake-up calls“Awake, ye drunkards, and weep” 1:5God sometimes lets the thing we lean on be cut off, to wake us and bring us home.
  3. Joy“Joy is withered away” 1:12Joy built on the harvest dies with it. Joy in the Lord can't be eaten.
  4. Humility“Sanctify ye a fast, call a solemn assembly” 1:14Get low before God, and do it together.
  5. The day of the LORD“The day of the LORD is at hand” 1:15God will step in to judge. The warning is His mercy.
  6. Prayer“O LORD, to thee will I cry” 1:19When nothing is left, cry out to the Lord. That's where restoring starts.

Question 1 of 6

Telling the story

Why should we tell the next generation what God has done?

What verses tell us about this?

“We will not hide them from their children, shewing to the generation to come the praises of the LORD, and his strength, and his wonderful works that he hath done.”

Psalm 78:4

Answer

Because the people coming after us need to know that God is real and that He deals with sin and rescues sinners. Joel wanted this disaster remembered for generations so that no one would forget what it taught. Your own story of what God brought you through can be the thing that helps another man come home.

Question 2 of 6

Wake-up calls

What does it take to wake a man up?

What verses tell us about this?

“And when he came to himself, he said, How many hired servants of my father’s have bread enough and to spare, and I perish with hunger!”

Luke 15:17

“And be not drunk with wine, wherein is excess; but be filled with the Spirit;”

Ephesians 5:18

Answer

Sometimes it takes losing the thing we ran to. When the locusts ate the vines, the drunkards were the first to feel it. God's call to “awake” isn't mockery. It's mercy. Like the prodigal son in the famine, a man can come to himself at the bottom and turn home. And God offers something better to be filled with: His Spirit.

Question 3 of 6

Joy

What happens to joy when everything is stripped away?

What verses tell us about this?

“Although the fig tree shall not blossom, neither shall fruit be in the vines; the labour of the olive shall fail, and the fields shall yield no meat; the flock shall be cut off from the fold, and there shall be no herd in the stalls: Yet I will rejoice in the LORD, I will joy in the God of my salvation.”

Habakkuk 3:17–18

Answer

Joy that rests on the harvest withers when the harvest fails. Judah's joy went with its fields. But Habakkuk shows a different joy: “yet I will rejoice in the LORD.” Things can be taken. The Lord can't. Gray days are real, but they are not the end of the story.

Question 4 of 6

Humility

What does it look like to humble ourselves before God?

What verses tell us about this?

“Draw nigh to God, and he will draw nigh to you. Cleanse your hands, ye sinners; and purify your hearts, ye double minded.”

James 4:8

“Humble yourselves in the sight of the Lord, and he shall lift you up.”

James 4:10

Answer

It looks like stopping everything to seek God. Judah's leaders put on sackcloth, set apart a fast, gathered everyone, and cried out to the Lord. Today it means being honest about where we are, setting time apart for God, and doing it together with His people. God lifts up the man who gets low.

Question 5 of 6

The day of the LORD

What is “the day of the LORD”?

What verses tell us about this?

“For yourselves know perfectly that the day of the Lord so cometh as a thief in the night.”

1 Thessalonians 5:2

“The Lord is not slack concerning his promise, as some men count slackness; but is longsuffering to us-ward, not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance.”

2 Peter 3:9

Answer

It's the time when God Himself steps in to judge sin. The locust plague was a small version, a warning. The great day is still coming, and the New Testament says it will come suddenly. But God warns first because He is patient and wants men to repent, not perish.

Question 6 of 6

Prayer

Where do we turn when nothing is left?

What verses tell us about this?

“And call upon me in the day of trouble: I will deliver thee, and thou shalt glorify me.”

Psalm 50:15

Answer

To the Lord. With the fields burned and the rivers dry, Joel doesn't give up and doesn't go back to the old thing. He prays: “O LORD, to thee will I cry.” God invites us to call on Him in the day of trouble, and He promises to answer.
