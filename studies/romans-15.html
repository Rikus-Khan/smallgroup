Pick up the other end

Chapter 14 was about the “weak”: believers with a tender conscience about food, drink and holy days. Paul counts himself among the “strong,” and says strength comes with a job. “Bear” means carry.

It's what a sponsor or an accountability partner does. The man with five years doesn't laugh at the man with five days. He picks up one end of the load.

Carrying costs you time and patience. But you go further together. And pleasing your neighbour “for his good” isn't telling him what he wants to hear.

He took the blows meant for God

Paul quotes a psalm of David, a man hated for his love of God's house. The insults men threw at God landed on David. Paul says they landed, finally and fully, on Jesus.

“For the zeal of thine house hath eaten me up; and the reproaches of them that reproached thee are fallen upon me.”

Psalm 69:9

Jesus didn't spend His life pleasing Himself. He went to the cross and took the shame that belonged to us. Every ugly thing you've ever done, and every ugly thing done to you, He took on Himself. That's grace, and it's already done.

So when carrying a weaker brother costs you something (your evening, your patience, your pride), you're not doing anything Jesus didn't do first, and far more.

The Old Testament wasn't written just for people long ago. It was written “for our learning.” Joseph sold by his brothers. Moses the killer. David the adulterer. Jonah running the other way. God put their failures on the record so a man like you could see Him keep going with men like them.

Notice the order: patience first. Scripture doesn't usually work like a switch. It's slow and steady, a chapter at a time, until one day you notice you've got hope where you didn't before. So read today, even if nothing seems to happen.

How did Christ receive you? Before you'd cleaned up. Before you'd proved anything. He didn't make you earn a place at the table. That's the measure: “as Christ also received us.”

Which means the man who walks in late, smelling like last night, gets the same welcome you got. It's not just being nice. It's “to the glory of God.”

Part two

One song

Verses 8–13

Jews and Gentiles praising the same God, just as the Scriptures always said, and a prayer that fills you to overflowing.

It was always the plan

Jesus came as “a minister of the circumcision”: a Jew, serving the Jewish people, keeping the promises God made to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. But those promises always had the rest of the world in them. Paul proves it four times, from every part of the Old Testament.

  1. The Psalms“I will confess to thee among the Gentiles”
    v. 9, from Psalm 18:49 (David)
  2. The Law“Rejoice, ye Gentiles, with his people”
    v. 10, from Deuteronomy 32:43 (Moses)
  3. The Psalms“Praise the Lord, all ye Gentiles”
    v. 11, from Psalm 117:1
  4. The Prophets“There shall be a root of Jesse”
    v. 12, from Isaiah 11:10

Jesse was King David's father. By Isaiah's day the royal family tree looked like a stump. Out of that stump God promised a shoot, a King the nations would trust. That's Jesus. And “Gentiles” is everyone who isn't Jewish, which is almost certainly you. You were in God's plan from the beginning.

The God of hope

Look who does the work in this prayer. God fills. You don't squeeze joy and peace out of yourself. They come “in believing”: trusting Him, one day at a time.

And He doesn't stop at the line. He fills you until it runs over: “that ye may abound in hope.” Enough for you, and some for the man next to you.

It comes “through the power of the Holy Ghost,” who has lived in you since the day you believed. If your hope ran dry yesterday, the source didn't.

He brings God people who believed the gospel, made clean and holy “by the Holy Ghost.”

Paul is painting a picture, not starting a priesthood. The point is how God sees a Gentile who believes: not as a leftover or an outsider, but as a gift that pleases Him.

And look at verse 14. Paul trusts the believers to “admonish one another”: to set each other straight. You don't need a seminary degree to tell a brother the truth in love. That's your job in this room.

Full circle

Illyricum is the Adriatic coast across from Italy: today's Albania and the old Yugoslavia. Paul had carried the gospel around that whole curve of the world, well over a thousand miles as the crow flies, and many times that on foot and by ship.

And look how he talks about it: “I will not dare to speak of any of those things which Christ hath not wrought by me.” Tell your story the same way. Not your war stories. What Christ did.

The believers in Jerusalem were poor, likely made poorer by famine and by being pushed out for following Jesus. So the mostly Gentile churches of Greece took up a collection, and Paul was carrying it to Jerusalem himself.

“Their debtors they are.” The good news came to the Gentiles through Jewish believers. Sending help back was simply paying a debt of love. Somebody carried the message to you. You owe it forward, and sometimes you owe it back.

Plans and providence

The plan: the gift to Jerusalem, then Rome, then west to Spain, the far end of the known world, off the left edge of this map.

What happened: a plot sent him the long way round (Acts 20:3). In Jerusalem a mob nearly killed him, and soldiers took him away in chains.

Two years in a cell at Caesarea, then a shipwreck. He reached Rome about AD 60, a prisoner.

Spain? Nobody knows. God didn't cancel Paul's plan. He rewrote the route.

“Delivered from them that do not believe in Judaea”: yes. “As they went about to kill him,” the Roman captain ran down with soldiers (Acts 21:31–32). Rescued by the army.

“Come unto you with joy” and “with you be refreshed”: yes. The brethren walked out to meet him on the road, and “when Paul saw, he thanked God, and took courage” (Acts 28:15). He just came in chains.

“A man’s heart deviseth his way: but the LORD directeth his steps.”

Proverbs 16:9

Even Paul asked

“Strive together with me.”

The great apostle didn't pretend he had it handled. He told them exactly what he was afraid of (men in Judaea who wanted him dead) and asked them to fight it out in prayer beside him. “Strive” is a wrestler's word.

Plans are good. Paul made them. But you hold them with an open hand. Your plan for this year might get rewritten too: a setback, a court date, a door that shuts. That doesn't mean God has left. It may mean He's taking you the long way, to the same place.

One thing to do today: tell one man here the specific thing you're afraid of, and ask him to pray about it with you. Then ask what you can pray for him.

Question 1 of 6

Strong and weak

What do the strong owe the weak?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

To carry them. The strong are to “bear the infirmities of the weak,” not please themselves, and do what builds the other man up. That's what Christ did for us: He didn't please Himself, and He took the reproaches that were aimed at God.

Question 2 of 6

The Scriptures

Why were the old Scriptures written?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

“For our learning,” so that through the patience and comfort they give, we might have hope. Paul keeps quoting them in this chapter: the root of Jesse, the Gentiles who would hear. God kept every one of those promises in Jesus, and He'll keep His promises to you.

Question 3 of 6

Received

How are we to receive one another?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

“As Christ also received us”: freely, before we'd earned anything. And with one mind, so that together, with one mouth, we glorify God. The welcome we give the next man is meant to look like the welcome Jesus gave us.

Question 4 of 6

Hope

Where does real hope come from?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

From God Himself, “the God of hope.” He fills us with joy and peace as we believe, through the Scriptures and by the power of the Holy Ghost, until we “abound in hope.” It's His work in us, not something we have to manufacture.

Question 5 of 6

Plans and providence

What did Paul plan, and what did God do with his plans?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

He planned to take the gift to Jerusalem, visit Rome, and go on to Spain. God did bring him to Rome, but by way of an arrest, two years in prison, and a shipwreck. Paul arrived in chains and preached there for two years. God kept the goal and changed the route.

Question 6 of 6

Prayer

What did Paul ask the Romans to pray for?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

That he'd be delivered from the unbelievers in Judaea, that the gift would be welcomed in Jerusalem, and that he'd reach Rome with joy and be refreshed. God answered: Roman soldiers rescued him, and the brethren met him on the road to Rome.
