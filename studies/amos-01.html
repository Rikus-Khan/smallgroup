Amos 1

The lion roars

A herdman from the hills of Judah walks north with a message from God, and starts with the neighbors everyone already hates.

Who, when, and to whom

Who: Amos, a herdman and fig farmer from Tekoa, a hill town in Judah, the southern kingdom. He wasn't trained as a prophet. God called him off the job.

To whom: Israel, the northern kingdom. He walked about twenty miles north and preached at Bethel, the north's royal shrine.

When: about 760 BC, before the exile. Israel was rich, safe, and sure God was pleased with it.

Part one

The LORD will roar

Verses 1–2

A working man from the hills, and a God who is done staying quiet.

A herdman with a message

What Amos was

A working man

He raised sheep and tended sycomore figs, a cheap fruit poor people ate. No seminary, no famous father. Later he told a priest so himself (7:14).

What God made him

The LORD's mouthpiece

“The LORD took me as I followed the flock” (7:15). A man from Judah sent to preach in Israel's own backyard, to its king and its priests.

“But God hath chosen the foolish things of the world to confound the wise; and God hath chosen the weak things of the world to confound the things which are mighty;”

1 Corinthians 1:27

If you think your past or your lack of schooling rules you out of being used by God, look at Amos. God doesn't need a résumé. He needs a man who'll go where he's sent.

The LORD will roar.

Amos knew lions. A shepherd hears a lion roar, and he knows it has already found its prey. That's how this book opens: God is not making a suggestion.

Notice where He roars from: Zion, Jerusalem, where His temple was. Not Bethel, where the north had set up its own golden calf. And the roar carries all the way to Carmel, the green ridge by the sea in the far north. The best pasture in the land would dry up.

“Two years before the earthquake.” It was so bad people still talked about it more than two hundred years later:

“…yea, ye shall flee, like as ye fled from before the earthquake in the days of Uzziah king of Judah: and the LORD my God shall come, and all the saints with thee.”

Zechariah 14:5

Part two

Three, and four

Verses 3–10

Syria, the Philistines, Tyre: the crowd at Bethel would have loved this sermon.

“For three … and for four”

This is a Hebrew way of saying: sin after sin after sin, and then one more. God didn't strike at the first one. Or the second. Or the third. He waited. The fourth is the one that fills the cup.

God had said the same thing to Abraham about the people of Canaan, hundreds of years before He let judgment fall on them:

“But in the fourth generation they shall come hither again: for the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet full.”

Genesis 15:16

God is slow to anger. But slow isn't never. The same patience that waits for us is a reason to turn now, not a reason to wait.

Threshed like grain

Damascus was the capital of Syria, Israel's neighbor to the north-east. A threshing sledge was a heavy wooden board with sharp iron or stone teeth on the bottom, dragged over grain to tear it apart. Under King Hazael, Syria did that to the people of Gilead, Israel's land east of the Jordan.

“…for the king of Syria had destroyed them, and had made them like the dust by threshing.”

2 Kings 13:7

“The bar of Damascus” is the heavy beam that locked the city gate. God would break it. And He said exactly where Syria's people would end up:

  1. ✓the people of Syria shall go into captivity unto KirAmos 1:5, about 760 BC2 Kings 16:9

About thirty years later, Assyria took Damascus and “carried the people of it captive to Kir” (2 Kings 16:9). God's word doesn't miss.

People sold as cargo

Gaza and its sister cities were the Philistines, on the coast to the south-west. Their crime: raiding whole towns, everybody in them, and selling them to Edom as slaves. Not soldiers taken in battle. Families, sold for profit.

  • Gazathe trader in people
  • Ashdodcut off
  • Ashkelonits ruler cut off
  • EkronGod's hand against it
  • Gathnot named; Uzziah had broken its wall (2 Chronicles 26:6)

Four of the five Philistine cities are named, and God says even the remnant would perish. A whole people that made its money off other people.

God keeps the books on every person who has been used, sold, or traded. Nobody is cargo to Him.

A broken handshake

Tyrus (Tyre) was the great port city up the coast, the richest trading town of its day. Tyre did the same thing Gaza did, but worse: it broke its word to do it. Long before, Tyre's king and Israel's king had been partners and friends.

1 Kings 5:12

…and there was peace between Hiram and Solomon; and they two made a league together.

Amos 1:9

…they delivered up the whole captivity to Edom, and remembered not the brotherly covenant:

King Hiram even called Solomon “my brother” (1 Kings 9:13). Tyre forgot all of it when there was money to be made.

God notices when a man breaks his word, even when nobody else holds him to it. Keeping your word when it costs you is part of walking with Him.

Part three

Kin and cruelty

Verses 11–15

Edom and Ammon were Israel's own relatives. That made it worse.

A grudge that never ends

Edom came from Esau. Israel came from Jacob. They were twin brothers. Edom carried the old family feud for centuries, and when Israel was down, Edom came after them with the sword. Teman and Bozrah were Edom's chief towns.

Look at the words: “cast off all pity,” “tear perpetually,” “kept his wrath for ever.” A grudge you keep starts keeping you. Lots of us have drunk or used over a resentment we couldn't drop.

“Let all bitterness, and wrath, and anger, and clamour, and evil speaking, be put away from you, with all malice: And be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you.”

Ephesians 4:31–32

For a strip of land

The Ammonites lived east of the Jordan; Rabbah, their capital, is Amman, Jordan's capital today. In their wars over Gilead they cut open pregnant women, it seems to wipe out the next generation who would hold the land. All for a few more miles of border.

God names it, and He judges it. The weakest people in a war, a mother and a child not yet born, are not invisible to Him.

“For thou hast possessed my reins: thou hast covered me in my mother’s womb.”

Psalm 139:13

The circle is drawing in

Picture the crowd at Bethel. A preacher from Judah stands up and starts naming their enemies:

Syria in the north-east, who threshed Gilead. Amen!

The Philistines in the south-west, the slave traders. Amen!

Tyre up the coast, and Edom down south. Preach it!

Ammon in the east. Look at the map: the line is going round, and it's getting closer every time.

It's easy to cheer when God deals with other people's sins. Amos isn't finished. In chapter 2, the circle closes in on Judah, and then on Israel itself.

Points to remember

  1. God's call“The words of Amos, who was among the herdmen” 1:1God uses ordinary working men. He called Amos off the job.
  2. God's patience“For three transgressions … and for four” 1:3God waits a long time before He judges. Slow to anger isn't never.
  3. God's justice“I will not turn away the punishment thereof” 1:3Every nation answers to God, even those who never had His law.
  4. Human worth“To deliver them up to Edom” 1:6Nobody is cargo. God sees the sold, the used, and the unborn.
  5. Resentment“He kept his wrath for ever” 1:11A grudge you keep ends up keeping you. Put it away, and forgive.

Question 1 of 5

God's call

Who does God choose to speak for Him?

What verses tell us about this?

“Then answered Amos, and said to Amaziah, I was no prophet, neither was I a prophet’s son; but I was an herdman, and a gatherer of sycomore fruit: And the LORD took me as I followed the flock, and the LORD said unto me, Go, prophesy unto my people Israel.”

Amos 7:14–15

“But God hath chosen the foolish things of the world to confound the wise; and God hath chosen the weak things of the world to confound the things which are mighty;”

1 Corinthians 1:27

Answer

Ordinary men. Amos wasn't a priest's son or a trained prophet. He was a herdman and a fig farmer, and God took him right off the job. God doesn't look for impressive men. He looks for willing ones, and then He gives them the words.

Question 2 of 5

God's patience

What does “for three transgressions, and for four” tell us about God?

What verses tell us about this?

“But in the fourth generation they shall come hither again: for the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet full.”

Genesis 15:16

“The Lord is not slack concerning his promise, as some men count slackness; but is longsuffering to us-ward, not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance.”

2 Peter 3:9

Answer

That God is patient. He let sin pile up, one after another, before He acted. He isn't quick to destroy; He wants men to turn. But His patience has an end. The time to turn to Him is while He is still waiting, and He is waiting today.

Question 3 of 5

God's justice

Does God judge nations that never had His law?

What verses tell us about this?

“For when the Gentiles, which have not the law, do by nature the things contained in the law, these, having not the law, are a law unto themselves: Which shew the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience also bearing witness, and their thoughts the mean while accusing or else excusing one another;)”

Romans 2:14–15

Answer

Yes. Syria, Gaza, Tyre, Edom, and Ammon never had the law of Moses. God didn't judge them for missing the Sabbath. He judged them for cruelty they knew was wrong: slave raids, broken promises, murder. Every man has a conscience, and every nation answers to the God who made it.

Question 4 of 5

Human worth

How does God see people who are treated like property?

What verses tell us about this?

“He that oppresseth the poor reproacheth his Maker: but he that honoureth him hath mercy on the poor.”

Proverbs 14:31

Answer

He sees them. Whole towns sold as slaves, mothers and unborn children killed for land: the world shrugged, but God wrote it down and judged it. Every person bears the mark of his Maker. If you've ever been used or treated like you didn't matter, God took notice, and He counts you as His.

Question 5 of 5

Resentment

What does a grudge kept “for ever” do to a man?

What verses tell us about this?

“Dearly beloved, avenge not yourselves, but rather give place unto wrath: for it is written, Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord.”

Romans 12:19

“Let all bitterness, and wrath, and anger, and clamour, and evil speaking, be put away from you, with all malice: And be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you.”

Ephesians 4:31–32

Answer

It tears at him. Edom's anger “did tear perpetually” until it drove him to cruelty and brought God's judgment on him. A grudge goes round and round and never pays off. The way out is to hand the score-settling to God and forgive, the same way God in Christ has forgiven us.
