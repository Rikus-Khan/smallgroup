The Gospel of Luke

Who: Luke, “the beloved physician” (Colossians 4:14), who travelled with Paul. He wrote Acts too. He was probably not a Jew, the only Gentile writer in the Bible.

When: about AD 60, before he wrote Acts.

To whom: “most excellent Theophilus” (v. 3), and through him to anyone, Jew or Gentile, who wants to be sure about Jesus.

The chapter opens in the temple at Jerusalem (v. 9). Six months later the same angel goes north, to a small town in Galilee called Nazareth (v. 26).

Where Luke 1 fits

  1. about 430 BCMalachi, the last Old Testament prophet
  2. about 400 yearsno prophet, no word from heaven
  3. about 6 BCGabriel speaks: John and Jesus are promisedthis chapter
  4. about 5 BCJesus is born in Bethlehem
  5. about AD 30 or 33the cross and the empty tomb
  6. about AD 60Luke writes his Gospel, then Acts

The last book of the Old Testament ends with a promise: God will send Elijah before the day of the LORD (Malachi 4:5). Then heaven goes quiet for about four hundred years.

Luke 1 is where the silence breaks. And the very first thing the angel says picks up Malachi's promise word for word (v. 17).

Part one

A priest and a prayer

Verses 1–25

An old man burns incense, and God answers a prayer he had given up on.

Luke checked his facts

Eyewitnessessaw it “from the beginning”ministers of the word“delivered them unto us”
Luke the doctortraced it all “from the very first”Theophilus“that thou mightest know the certainty”you

Luke wasn't there himself. So he did what a careful doctor does: he went back to the people who were, and put it “in order.” Some of what follows (what Mary thought, what she said) most likely came from Mary herself, or from people close to her.

Your faith doesn't rest on a feeling or a legend. It rests on things that happened, told by men who saw them. You can be sure.

Herod, and a priest on duty

Herod here is Herod the Great, the king Rome set over the Jews. He rebuilt the temple in grand style, and he was a cruel and jealous man. He died about 4 BC.

King David split the priests into 24 courses, or teams (1 Chronicles 24). Each took its turn at the temple for a week. “The eighth to Abijah” (24:10): that's Abia, Zacharias' course.

Zacharias and Elisabeth both came from priestly families, and they both walked with God. And still they had no child. Trouble in your life doesn't prove God is against you.

Once in a lifetime

With thousands of priests, the honor went by lot. Tradition says a priest got it once in his life, if ever.

While the smoke went up inside, the people prayed outside. Incense was a picture of prayer: “Let my prayer be set forth before thee as incense” (Psalm 141:2).

On the biggest day of this old man's life, God showed up.

“Thy prayer is heard”

יוֹחָנָן said Yōḥanan, John “the LORD is gracious”

Which prayer? Most likely the one for a son, prayed when they were young, and probably given up on long ago. They were “well stricken in years” now (v. 7). God hadn't forgotten a word of it.

Some prayers you stopped praying years ago are still before Him. God's “not yet” is not the same as “no.”

Filled with something better

What John would leave alone

Wine and strong drink

Like a Nazarite, who “shall separate himself from wine and strong drink” (Numbers 6:3). John was set apart for God from birth.

What would fill him

The Holy Ghost

“Be not drunk with wine, wherein is excess; but be filled with the Spirit” (Ephesians 5:18).

Whatever fills a man runs him. For a man who knows what the bottle can do, this lands close to home. The answer isn't only to empty the glass. It's to be filled with God's Spirit instead.

John was filled from the womb, a rare gift for a special job. Every believer receives the Spirit when he's saved. Being filled is letting Him run your life, day by day.

The silence breaks right where it began

Malachi 4:6, the last page of the Old Testament

And he shall turn the heart of the fathers to the children, and the heart of the children to their fathers, lest I come and smite the earth with a curse.

Luke 1:17, about 400 years later

And he shall go before him in the spirit and power of Elias, to turn the hearts of the fathers to the children…

God picked up where He left off. John would come in the spirit of Elijah, to get the people ready for the Lord. Jesus later said John was that Elijah who was promised (Matthew 11:14).

And look what turning to God does: it turns fathers back to their children. Broken families are part of what God came to mend.

“Whereby shall I know this?”

ZachariasWhereby shall I know this? for I am an old man, and my wife well stricken in years.
prove it

The angelI am Gabriel, that stand in the presence of God… thou shalt be dumb… because thou believest not my words…

He had prayed for this, and when the answer came, he looked at his age instead of at God. So he got his sign: nine months without a word.

But God did not take the promise back. Zacharias' doubt cost him his voice, not the blessing. God is more faithful than our faith.

Shame taken away

“called barren” (v. 36) → looked on “the Lord … looked on me” (v. 25)

“Reproach” means shame, disgrace. In those days people whispered that a childless woman must have done something to deserve it. Elisabeth had carried that label for years.

God took it off her. Some of you carry names people put on you: drunk, junkie, loser, convict. When the Lord looks on you, those labels don't get the last word.

Part two

The angel and the virgin

Verses 26–38

The same angel goes to a nobody in a nowhere town, with the biggest news ever told.

“Espoused”

  1. Espouseda binding promise, sealed before witnessesMary is here
  2. Waitingoften up to a year, still living apart
  3. The weddinghe takes her home as his wife

Being espoused was more than our engagement. The couple were legally bound, and only a divorce could end it (Matthew 1:19). But they didn't live together yet, and Mary was still a virgin.

Joseph was “of the house of David,” the royal family line. God had promised David a son who would reign for ever. Watch for that promise in verse 32.

“Highly favoured”

κεχαριτωμένη from charis, grace: “graced,” one God has shown great kindness to

Mary was troubled by it (v. 29). She was a young woman from a small town with no name to speak of. Why would God pick her?

That's what grace is: favour you didn't earn. Mary received God's grace like any other believer, and she said so herself: “my spirit hath rejoiced in God my Saviour” (v. 47). God still loves to choose people nobody else would pick.

Five things about the baby

  1. JESUS“the LORD saves”: “he shall save his people from their sins” (Matthew 1:21)
  2. GreatJohn would be “great in the sight of the Lord” (v. 15). Jesus is simply great.
  3. Son of the HighestGod's own Son
  4. David's thronethe King God promised David
  5. No end“for ever” over the house of Jacob

“And thine house and thy kingdom shall be established for ever before thee: thy throne shall be established for ever.”

2 Samuel 7:16, God's promise to David

A thousand years after that promise, here is the King who keeps it.

Born of a virgin

Mary's question was honest. She was a virgin, and she knew how babies come. The answer: this child would have no human father. The Holy Ghost would bring it about.

“Therefore the Lord himself shall give you a sign; Behold, a virgin shall conceive, and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel.”

Isaiah 7:14, about 700 years earlier

That's why Jesus is both. Born of Mary, He is truly a man. Conceived by the Holy Ghost, He is “the Son of God,” and “holy,” without sin. Only someone like that could take our place.

For with God nothing shall be impossible.

Genesis 18:14, to old, barren Sarah

Is any thing too hard for the LORD? At the time appointed I will return unto thee, according to the time of life, and Sarah shall have a son.

Luke 1:36–37, about old, barren Elisabeth

She hath also conceived a son in her old age… For with God nothing shall be impossible.

The angel gave Mary a sign she could check: go and see Elisabeth. An old woman pregnant, and a virgin with child. Both impossible, both done.

A man free after twenty years of drinking. A family put back together. A heart made new. Don't tell God what can't be done.

Two questions, two answers

An old priest, in the temple, in Jerusalem

“Whereby shall I know this?”

Asked for proof, because he didn't believe it (v. 20). He went home unable to speak.

A young woman, at home, in a little town

“How shall this be?”

Asked how, not whether. Then: “Be it unto me according to thy word” (v. 38). Elisabeth called her blessed because she “believed” (v. 45).

God doesn't mind honest questions. Mary asked one. The difference is the heart behind it: “prove it to me” or “I believe You; show me how.”

And her yes cost her. An unmarried girl with child faced gossip, and the chance of losing Joseph. She said yes anyway.

Part three

Two mothers and a song

Verses 39–56

An unborn baby jumps for joy, and a young woman sings about a God who lifts the low.

Mary goes south

From Nazareth to the hills of Judaea is about 80 miles, several days on foot. Luke doesn't name the town. It was somewhere in the hill country near Jerusalem.

She went “with haste.” The angel had pointed her to Elisabeth, and she went straight to the one other woman on earth who would understand.

When God does something big in you, find the people who will rejoice with you.

John's first witness

Before either baby was born, John jumped for joy at the sound of Mary's voice. His whole life would be pointing to Jesus. He started early.

Elisabeth, filled with the Holy Ghost, calls Mary “the mother of my Lord.” She calls the tiny child in Mary's womb her Lord. Jesus was Lord from the start.

Luke, the doctor, uses the same Greek word, brephos, “babe,” for John in the womb here and for Jesus in the manger: “Ye shall find the babe wrapped in swaddling clothes” (Luke 2:12). To God, a child in the womb is a child.

“My soul doth magnify”

To magnify is to make great. A magnifying glass doesn't make a thing bigger. It makes it look as big as it really is.

Mary's song makes God look big and herself small: “the low estate of his handmaiden” (v. 48). She calls Him “God my Saviour.” Even the mother of Jesus needed saving.

When your problems look huge and God looks small, you're holding the glass the wrong way round.

God turns things upside down

Up highon the seat

Down lowat the bottom

The proud and the mightythe rich, sent “empty away”
The low and the hungry“filled … with good things”

Mary sings as if it's already done. God puts down the ones who think they don't need Him, and lifts up the ones who know they do.

If you came to Jesus at the bottom, broke and hungry, you're in good company. That's exactly who He fills. The only thing that keeps a man empty is thinking he's already full.

Part four

His name is John

Verses 57–80

The silent priest writes one line, gets his voice back, and sings about the sunrise.

The eighth day

“And he that is eight days old shall be circumcised among you, every man child in your generations…”

Genesis 17:12

Circumcision was the mark of God's covenant with Abraham. On that day a Jewish boy was also named. Everyone expected “Zacharias, after the name of his father.” That was the custom.

Elisabeth said no. “None of thy kindred” had this name (v. 61). This boy wasn't going to carry on the old family pattern. God had named him. Your family's past doesn't have to be your future.

One line on a tablet

Zacharias, after his father → John “the LORD is gracious,” the name God gave (v. 13)

A “writing table” was a small board coated with wax, written on with a point. Nine months earlier Zacharias wouldn't take God at His word. Now he writes it down in front of everybody: His name is John.

The moment he obeyed, his tongue was loosed, and the first thing out of his mouth was praise. The man who doubted got a second chance. So do we.

“An horn of salvation”

A horn was a picture of strength, like the horns of a bull. David used it of God: “my buckler, and the horn of my salvation, and my high tower” (Psalm 18:2).

Zacharias sings about God raising up a strong Saviour “in the house of his servant David.” That's not John, who was from Aaron's line. It's Mary's baby, from David's line, not even born yet.

And notice the tense: God “hath visited and redeemed.” When God promises it, it's as good as done.

Listen to the song: God remembers His covenant (v. 72). He keeps the oath He swore to Abraham (v. 73), and it's all His tender mercy (v. 78). The father, the mother, and the boy: the LORD remembered His oath, and He was gracious.

Why did He save them? “That we … might serve him without fear” (v. 74). Not to live scared, but free.

The Greek word, aphesis, means a sending away, a release, like a debt written off. John's job was to tell people how to be saved: their sins could be sent away.

Not by working it off. “Through the tender mercy of our God” (v. 78), paid for by the One who was coming.

The dayspring: sunrise

v. 79Sitting in darkness

“In the shadow of death.” Not walking, sitting: stuck, with no way out.

v. 78The dayspring

The first light over the hills. God's Son, coming “from on high” to visit us.

v. 79The way of peace

Light enough to see the road, and feet guided onto it.

“The people that walked in darkness have seen a great light: they that dwell in the land of the shadow of death, upon them hath the light shined.”

Isaiah 9:2

Sunrise doesn't wait for you to climb out of the dark first. It comes to where you are.

The quiet years

  1. about 6 BCJohn is born
  2. about 30 yearsgrowing strong, out of sight in the deserts
  3. about AD 27“the word of God came unto John the son of Zacharias in the wilderness” (Luke 3:2)

One verse covers some thirty years. No crowds, no preaching, just growing. God was getting His man ready.

Recovery has quiet years too: the slow days when nobody's clapping and you're just growing strong. They're not wasted. God does some of His best work out of sight.

Question 1 of 6

God's Word

Can we be sure what we believe is true?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Yes. Luke didn't write down rumors. He went back to the eyewitnesses, traced everything from the start, and set it out in order, so that we could know “the certainty” of what we've been taught. Our faith rests on things that really happened.

Question 2 of 6

Prayer

Does God still hear prayers we have stopped praying?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Yes. Zacharias and Elisabeth were too old to hope any more, and God said, “Thy prayer is heard.” He answers in His time, not ours. A prayer you gave up on may still be before Him.

Question 3 of 6

Faith and doubt

What is the difference between doubting God and asking Him?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Zacharias wanted proof because he didn't believe. Mary asked how, because she did. God welcomes honest questions. What matters is whether we take Him at His word: “be it unto me according to thy word.”

Question 4 of 6

Who Jesus is

Who did the angel say Mary's son would be?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Jesus, “the LORD saves.” Born of a virgin by the Holy Ghost, He is the Son of God, holy, and truly a man. He is the promised King, and His kingdom will never end.

Question 5 of 6

God's power

Is anything too hard for God?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

No. An old barren woman had a son, and a virgin conceived. “With God nothing shall be impossible.” Whatever looks hopeless in your life is not too hard for Him.

Question 6 of 6

Salvation (soteriology)

What did God send Jesus to do for His people?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

To redeem them: to buy them back. To send their sins away, by God's tender mercy and not by their own work. To bring light to people sitting in darkness, and to guide their feet into the way of peace.
