Genesis 31

Running from Laban, and the heap of witness

After twenty years of being cheated, Jacob hears the LORD say go home. He slips away without a word, Rachel steals her father's household gods, and Laban chases him for seven days, until God steps in. It ends with a heap of stones and a prayer: the LORD watch between me and thee.

Part one

Time to go

Verses 1–16

Twenty years in, the signs all say the same thing. Then the LORD says it too.

Three signs it was time

  1. The talk (verse 1)Laban's sons say Jacob has taken everything that was their father's. Jealous talk in the family.
  2. The face (verse 2)Laban's look had gone cold: “it was not toward him as before.” Jacob could read it.
  3. The word (verse 3)The LORD: “Return unto the land of thy fathers, and to thy kindred; and I will be with thee.”

The first two were only pressure. Jacob had been under pressure for years. What settled it was the third: the LORD said. Before you leave a job, a town or a church because things got tense, ask what God has said, not just what people have said.

“And, behold, I am with thee, and will keep thee in all places whither thou goest, and will bring thee again into this land; for I will not leave thee, until I have done that which I have spoken to thee of.”

Genesis 28:15

He heard those words twenty years earlier, running from his brother, with a stone for a pillow. Now God says the same thing again: I will be with thee. The man who left home with nothing is being walked home by the One who promised.

Twenty years, by the numbers

  1. 14 yearsfor the two daughtersLeah and Rachel (verse 41)
  2. 6 yearsfor the flockhis own wages (verse 41)
  3. 10 timesLaban changed his wagesverses 7 and 41
  4. 20 yearsin Laban's houseand God never left him

Jacob was the man who once tricked his own father. Now he has been tricked by his own uncle, again and again. The Bible does not say that makes it fair, or that it was all a lesson. It does say what stood between Jacob and ruin: “God suffered him not to hurt me.”

If you have worked for a boss who kept moving the goalposts, you know how Jacob felt. The man with power over your pay does not have the last word. God sees the work.

Old words in verses 8–12

  1. ringstrakedstriped, with bands or rings of colour
  2. speckledspotted
  3. grisledmottled grey, speckled with grey
  4. hirewages, pay
  5. cattlelivestock: here, sheep and goats

Chapter 30 told how Jacob used striped sticks and clever breeding to build his flock. Here he tells it the way he now sees it: “God hath taken away the cattle of your father, and given them to me.” Whatever the sticks did, it was God who saw what Laban did to him, and God who set it right.

Jacob had been a clever man all his life, and cleverness was never what he lived on.

“I am the God of Bethel”

Old words: anointedst and vowedst are old verb endings: “you anointed”, “you vowed.” At Bethel Jacob poured oil on a stone and made a promise, while he was running from the mess he made at home.

He had probably half forgotten it. God had not. He keeps track of promises: His, and the ones we made to Him in a bad hour. That is good news for the man who made big promises and broke them. He has not forgotten you, and He is still calling you home.

What Rachel and Leah said

JacobYour father has changed my wages ten times. But the God of my father hath been with me. God said, get thee out.

Rachel and LeahIs there yet any portion or inheritance for us in our father's house? Are we not counted of him strangers? we are with you

Rachel and LeahNow then, whatsoever God hath said unto thee, do.

“Quite devoured also our money” is the sisters' complaint. Many Bible teachers read it this way: the years Jacob worked were, in effect, the price of the marriage, which should have been set aside for the daughters. Laban kept it for himself. Their own father had treated them like strangers.

Notice what two rivals did. Leah and Rachel spent chapters 29 and 30 competing. Now they stand together, because a father wounded both of them. And they ended with one of the best lines in Genesis: whatsoever God hath said unto thee, do.

Part two

Out the back door

Verses 17–24

Jacob does the right thing the wrong way, and Rachel does the wrong thing.

Haran to Gilead

Jacob had been in Haran about twenty years (verse 41). Now he leaves with wives, sons, camels, and every animal he owns. The line is the way he would have gone: over “the river” (the Euphrates, verse 21), south past Damascus, and into the hill country of Gilead, east of the Jordan.

Notice “Isaac his father” in verse 18. Isaac is still alive, and he is the one Jacob is going home to.

The images

תְּרָפִים said teraphim “household gods”: small figures kept in the home

Why did Rachel take them? The chapter does not say. Some teachers think she wanted the gods' protection on the road. Others think they were a sign of who would inherit the family goods. What the text does say is that she took them, and that her father called them “my gods” (verse 30).

Rachel said whatsoever God hath said unto thee, do, and still carried an idol in her baggage: a believer with a divided heart. Many men know what it is to follow Jesus with something from the old life tucked in the saddle. Later, Jacob buries them:

“And they gave unto Jacob all the strange gods which were in their hand, and all their earrings which were in their ears; and Jacob hid them under the oak which was by Shechem.”

Genesis 35:4

“Stole”, “stolen”, “steal”

Count the word in verses 19 to 39:

19×1 20×1 21  22  23  24  25  26×1 27×1 28  29  30×1 31  32×1 33  34  35  36  37  38  39×2

8 times in about twenty verses. Laban took Jacob's pay, ten times over. Jacob slipped away without a word. Rachel took her father's gods. Nobody in this tent was clean, and the Bible says so with one word, over and over.

Jacob had God's word to go. But he went by stealth, and he says why later: “Because I was afraid” (verse 31). Fear can make a man do the right thing the wrong way. A hard, honest conversation, with God's promise behind him, was the harder road. Some of us have slipped out of a job, a home or a church the same way: no word, no goodbye. Naming it is the first step to doing better next time.

The night before

“Take heed that thou speak not to Jacob either good or bad.” Neither threats nor sweet talk. Hands off.

Old words: the Syrian means the Aramean: a man from Aram, the country around Haran. Mount means hill country. His brethren are his relatives.

Jacob never saw this happen. He was camped in the hills, frightened, and the night before the showdown the LORD had already spoken to the man chasing him.

Part three

The search

Verses 25–42

Laban wants his gods. Jacob makes a rash promise. Rachel sits very still.

The send-off that never was

  1. mirthjoy, a party
  2. songssinging
  3. tabreta small hand drum, like a tambourine
  4. harpa stringed instrument
  5. tarriedstayed, waited (verse 54)
  6. captives taken with the swordprisoners of war

Laban says he would have thrown a farewell party. Maybe. He also changed Jacob's wages ten times. A man's story about what he would have done is not always the truth.

But the hurt in verse 28 sounds real: “hast not suffered me to kiss my sons and my daughters?” Laban was a hard and crooked man. He was also a grandfather who did not get to say goodbye. People are rarely only one thing.

“It is in the power of my hand”

LabanIt is in the power of my hand to do you hurt:

Laban, in the same breathbut the God of your father spake unto me yesternight. held back

That is the whole chapter in two lines. A man with the power to hurt, and a God who said no. Laban does not say he has become kinder. He says God spoke.

Old words: yesternight is last night. Wouldest needs be gone means you had to go. Sore longedst means you longed very much.

A rash word

Jacob swears“With whomsoever thou findest thy gods, let him not live”
→
But he did not know“that Rachel had stolen them”
→

Jacob was angry, sure of himself, and wrong about what was in his own camp. The Bible does not say this oath caused Rachel's early death in chapter 35, and we should not say it did. But it shows how fast a man's big words can point at the people he loves most.

If your mouth has got you into trouble, you are not alone. Slow down before you swear, threaten, or promise something in anger.

A god you have to carry, and hide, and sit on.

Old words: the camel's furniture is the camel's saddle and baggage. The custom of women is the old, polite way of saying a woman's monthly period: Rachel's reason for not getting up.

Rachel lied to her father's face, with the stolen gods under her. The Bible reports it without a smile; do not make her a hero. And do not miss the picture: the gods Laban chased for seven days were sitting under a tired woman in a saddle bag, and they could not say a word.

“They bear him upon the shoulder, they carry him, and set him in his place, and he standeth; from his place shall he not remove: yea, one shall cry unto him, yet can he not answer, nor save him out of his trouble.”

Isaiah 46:7

“Little children, keep yourselves from idols. Amen.”

1 John 5:21

Twenty years of swallowed anger

  1. wrothvery angry
  2. chodequarrelled, scolded (the old past tense of chide)
  3. trespassa wrong, an offence
  4. hotlywith heat, in a rage
  5. stuffbelongings, goods

Jacob had been quiet for twenty years. Then it all comes out in one speech. He had a fair case: Laban searched everything and found nothing. But the Bible says plainly that he was wroth, and chode.

“Wherefore, my beloved brethren, let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath: For the wrath of man worketh not the righteousness of God.”

James 1:19-20

Men who stuff their anger for years do not get calmer. They get a moment when it all comes out at once. Tell a brother or your pastor about the thing you are swallowing, while it is small.

What a shepherd's twenty years cost

  1. By day“the drought consumed me”: the heat
  2. By night“the frost”: the cold
  3. No sleep“my sleep departed from mine eyes”
  4. The losses“I bare the loss of it”: he paid for what the wild beasts tore and what thieves took

Laban's flock came through those years in better shape than it went in. Jacob's body paid for it. If you have worked hard for a man who never said thank you, God has still been watching the whole time.

“And whatsoever ye do, do it heartily, as to the Lord, and not unto men; Knowing that of the Lord ye shall receive the reward of the inheritance: for ye serve the Lord Christ.”

Colossians 3:23-24

“I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep.”

John 10:11

And the One who really watches the sheep is no hired man.

Whose God is He?

  1. The God of my fatherverses 5 and 42: Isaac's God, handed down
  2. The God of Abrahamthe One who called a man out of Ur
  3. The fear of Isaacthe One Isaac stood in awe of
  4. “My God”?Not yet. Jacob has never quite said it.

Jacob has seen God at Bethel. God has kept him for twenty years. But still he talks about the God of my father. He is borrowing his faith from Abraham and Isaac. Many of us start there: our grandmother's God, our pastor's God.

Keep an eye on this. At the end of chapter 33, Jacob will build an altar and name it for himself and his God together.

And do not skip the last line of verse 42: “God hath seen mine affliction and the labour of my hands, and rebuked thee yesternight.” Seen. That is the word to carry.

Part four

The heap of witness

Verses 43–55

Two men who cannot trust each other put God between them.

“All that thou seest is mine.”

That is Laban to the end: the daughters, the children, the cattle, everything. And in the same breath: “what can I do this day unto these my daughters?” He claims it all, and he cannot touch any of it.

A covenant is a sworn agreement made before God, with witnesses. Neither man trusts the other, so they will make a promise bigger than both of them. Jacob sets up a stone pillar to mark the spot.

English

Laban spoke the language of his country, Aramaic, and Jacob the Hebrew of his fathers. Same pile of stones, two names, one meaning. Galeed gave its name to the hill country of Gilead.

They sat down and ate on the heap. A shared meal sealed a deal. It is hard to stay enemies over bread.

Mizpah

מִצְפָּה said Mitspah “the watch-tower”: a lookout

Today “Mizpah” is on friendship jewellery as a loving goodbye. Read it where it was said. Laban says it to a son-in-law he does not trust, after twenty years of cheating each other. It means: “God sees what we cannot see when we are apart. So behave.” Verse 50 says it outright: “no man is with us; see, God is witness.”

“The eyes of the LORD are in every place, beholding the evil and the good.”

Proverbs 15:3

That is a harder meaning, and a better one. When you are alone, God is not. Put a brother in Christ in your life who can also ask you the hard questions when you are apart.

A line neither would cross

“I will not pass over this heap to thee, and thou shalt not pass over this heap and this pillar unto me, for harm.” They could still travel and trade. What neither would do was cross it to hurt the other. The stones are not a wall. They are a witness.

Notice the clause in verse 50: “If thou shalt afflict my daughters, or if thou shalt take other wives beside my daughters.” Laban is a crooked man, but here is a father making his last move to protect his girls. He has no power to enforce it, so he hands it to God.

Whom do you swear by?

Laban

“The God of Abraham, and the God of Nahor”

Two names, for two families. Some teachers hear him treating them as two gods, which fits a man who kept household idols.

Jacob

“The fear of his father Isaac”

One God, the only One. Then he offered a sacrifice and shared a meal with all the men.

Old word: sware is swore. Who a man swears by shows who he serves.

A kiss, and a blessing

Look at how this ends. The man who rode seven days with a grudge “rose up, and kissed his sons and his daughters, and blessed them: and Laban departed, and returned unto his place.”

“When a man’s ways please the LORD, he maketh even his enemies to be at peace with him.”

Proverbs 16:7

Laban did not turn into a saint overnight, and Jacob had no power to change his heart. God did it. When your way pleases the LORD, He has ways to quiet the enemies you cannot.

Jacob got to put one road behind him. The next road is harder: his brother Esau is waiting, and Jacob wronged him far worse than Laban ever wronged Jacob. That is chapter 32.

Question 1 of 6

The call

What did the LORD tell Jacob to do, and what did He promise?

What verses tell us about this?

“And, behold, I am with thee, and will keep thee in all places whither thou goest, and will bring thee again into this land; for I will not leave thee, until I have done that which I have spoken to thee of.”

Genesis 28:15

Answer

To go home, to the land of his fathers and his family, and the LORD promised, “I will be with thee.” It was the same promise He made at Bethel twenty years earlier, when Jacob was running. God does not forget the promise or the man. If He has told you to go, He goes with you.

Question 2 of 6

God sees

Who was really taking care of Jacob while Laban cheated him?

What verses tell us about this?

“And whatsoever ye do, do it heartily, as to the Lord, and not unto men; Knowing that of the Lord ye shall receive the reward of the inheritance: for ye serve the Lord Christ.”

Colossians 3:23-24

Answer

God. Laban changed the wages ten times, and the flock grew anyway. Jacob says it himself: God suffered Laban not to hurt him, and God saw his affliction and his labour. If someone with power over your pay has been unfair, work for the Lord and trust the One who sees.

Question 3 of 6

Sin

How did Jacob, Rachel, and Laban each go wrong in this chapter?

What verses tell us about this?

“If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness.”

1 John 1:9

Answer

Laban cheated Jacob on his wages, ten times over. Jacob slipped away without a word, out of fear. Rachel stole the household gods and lied to her father about them. No one in this chapter is clean, and God held them all anyway. If you have sinned in secret, confess it: He is faithful and just to forgive.

Question 4 of 6

False gods

What good were Laban's household gods?

What verses tell us about this?

“They bear him upon the shoulder, they carry him, and set him in his place, and he standeth; from his place shall he not remove: yea, one shall cry unto him, yet can he not answer, nor save him out of his trouble.”

Isaiah 46:7

Answer

None. They could be stolen, hidden in a camel saddle, and sat on, and they never said a word. Laban chased his gods for seven days and never found them. A god you have to carry cannot carry you. Whatever you have been leaning on that cannot answer, let it go and take it to the LORD.

Question 5 of 6

Protection

Why did Laban not hurt Jacob when he caught up with him?

What verses tell us about this?

“What shall we then say to these things? If God be for us, who can be against us?”

Romans 8:31

Answer

Because God spoke to Laban in a dream the night before. Laban says it himself: he had the power to hurt Jacob, but the God of Jacob's father said no. If God is for you, nobody can finish you. Jacob did not even know it was happening.

Question 6 of 6

Mizpah

What does Mizpah really mean?

What verses tell us about this?

“The eyes of the LORD are in every place, beholding the evil and the good.”

Proverbs 15:3

Answer

It was a watch-tower: a warning between two men who did not trust each other. The LORD sees what we do when we are apart. Today it is often a sweet goodbye, but the real meaning is sharper and better: God sees you when no one else does, so live as a man who is seen, and keep a brother in Christ close who can see too.
