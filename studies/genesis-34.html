Genesis 34

Dinah, and the sword at Shechem

A young woman is wronged, her brothers answer with a lie and a massacre, and nobody in the chapter asks God a thing. Here is where anger and revenge lead.

Things to watch for

A hard chapter, told in few words. Keep these questions in mind. We will come back to them at the end.

Part one

A wrong done at Shechem

Verses 1–12

A young woman is wronged by the prince of the land, and then the prince wants to make her his wife.

Camped outside Shechem

Jacob was home. He had come back from Haran with two wives, two maids, eleven sons and a daughter, and he pitched his tent outside Shechem, bought a field there from Hamor's sons, and built an altar (33:18–20).

Hamor ruled the town, a Hivite, one of the peoples who lived in Canaan. His son Shechem was “prince of the country,” and carried the same name as the town.

Bethel, where God had first met Jacob, was about twenty miles south. Keep that in mind: chapter 35 opens with God calling him on to Bethel.

Say it plainly

What the verse says (2)

He took her, and defiled her

Shechem forced himself on Dinah. The Bible names it in plain words and gives no detail, and none is needed. It was a sin, and it was his.

What came after (3–4)

Then he loved her

His soul “clave” to her, which means it stuck, held fast. He “spake kindly” and asked his father to get him the girl. Sweet words and a wedding do not wash out what was already done.

Notice what the verse does not say. It does not say Dinah did wrong by going out to see the daughters of the land. The blame is Shechem's. If anything like this was ever done to you, it was not your fault either, and you do not have to carry it alone. Tell your pastor or a brother you trust.

And for the man who took what he wanted and talked about love afterward: a hunger that takes first and asks later has ruined plenty of lives. Shechem's story shows where it ends.

Three men, three answers

  1. Jacobheard it and held his peace, said nothing until his sons came home from the field
  2. The sonsgrieved and very wroth, with good reason
  3. Hamorcame to commune with Jacob, to talk it over, as if it were a deal to be settled

The sons were right to be angry. Verse 7 says what Shechem did was “folly in Israel,” an outrage, a disgrace, “which thing ought not to be done.” Anger at a wrong like this is not the sin. What a man does with it can be.

Jacob's silence is worth a look. He said nothing at all for Dinah. The one time he speaks in this chapter (verse 30), he is worried about himself. A father's silence can be as hard on a daughter as a stranger's shout.

An offer, but not what was owed

  • a marriage“give her him to wife”
  • land and trade“dwell and trade ye therein, and get you possessions”
  • any price“Ask me never so much dowry and gift”
  • but not one “I was wrong”he asks for grace, and never says sorry

Dowry is the gift a man paid for his bride. Shechem will pay any amount. What he never offers is the thing that matters most: an honest word that says I did wrong.

Money and kind words are easy. A man in recovery knows this one. It is easier to promise a family the world than to look them in the eye and say I did that, and it was wrong. Grace starts with that sentence.

Old words in this part

  1. damsela girl, a young woman
  2. clavestuck to, held fast
  3. wrothvery angry
  4. wrought follydid an outrage, a shameful thing
  5. communetalk it over, discuss

The KJV says “brethren” for brothers (verse 11), and “soul” for the whole inner man: “the soul of my son Shechem longeth.”

Part two

The sign turned into a trap

Verses 13–24

Jacob’s sons answer with a lie, and they use God’s own mark on Abraham’s family to carry it out.

God's sign, used as a weapon

What God made it (Genesis 17)

A sign of a promise

God gave Abraham circumcision as the mark of His covenant, the sign that this family belonged to Him and that His promises were for them.

What the brothers made it (13–17)

A trap

They said: become like us, and we will become one people. They did not mean it. The Bible says plainly they spoke “deceitfully.”

“This is my covenant, which ye shall keep, between me and you and thy seed after thee; Every man child among you shall be circumcised. And ye shall circumcise the flesh of your foreskin; and it shall be a token of the covenant betwixt me and you.”

Genesis 17:10–11

There is a warning in this for every man who has ever used religious talk as cover. It is easy to say it's for the Lord, it's for honour, it's for family when the real motive is to hurt somebody. God is not fooled by the words.

Everybody had a reason

  1. Shechemwanted Dinah. He did not wait, and then he could not wait.
  2. Hamorwanted peace, and a deal with a rich family
  3. The men at the gatewanted the cattle: “Shall not their cattle … be ours?”
  4. The brotherswanted payback, and had planned how to get it

The gate was where a town's elders sat to decide business and settle quarrels. That is where Hamor made his case, and he made it with profit. A whole city said yes to a painful thing because they thought it would make them richer.

Read all thirty-one verses again. Dinah never says a word. Everyone decides about her, and nobody asks her. And “deferred” in verse 19 means put off, delayed: the young man did not delay. He had not delayed at the start, either.

Part three

The sword

Verses 25–31

On the third day, while the men of the city lay sore, two brothers pick up their swords.

How it went down

  1. A real wrongDinah was hurt
  2. A cold planwait three days
  3. A liewearing God's sign
  4. A sworda whole city of men
  5. A plundered citywomen and children taken

Look at the top step. The anger began in the right place, with a real wrong done to their sister. That is what makes this chapter hard. Every step down felt like justice to the men taking it.

The Bible does not dwell on it, and neither will we. Spoiled means plundered. Two brothers began it with the sword, and then all the sons took the flocks, the wealth, the wives and the little children of the city. A wrong done to one girl became wrongs done to a whole city.

The last word

JacobYe have troubled me to make me to stink among the inhabitants of the land.

Simeon and LeviShould he deal with our sister as with an harlot? no answer

“To stink” is the old way of saying to be hated, to have a bad name. Jacob is afraid of what the neighbours will do, “I being few in number.” He says nothing about the city, the dead, or the lie. A harlot is a prostitute, and the brothers' question has a point: Dinah was wronged. But it is not an answer to what they did.

And that is where the chapter ends. Nobody says I was wrong. Nobody builds an altar.

Not once does anyone say God.

Count it: thirty-one verses and the word is missing. Jacob does not pray. The sons do not ask. Hamor and Shechem bargain, the brothers plan, the men at the gate vote with their wallets. When people leave God out, it tends to go this way.

Jacob had wrestled God at the Jabbok not long before (chapter 32) and been given a new name. Here he is back to being the man who keeps quiet and lets things happen. Even a man with a promise can drift into a chapter where he does not seek God.

If you have had a chapter like that, a few months or a few years when God was not part of the plan, you are in good company. And notice what God does next.

Jacob did not forget

Many years later, dying in Egypt, Jacob blessed his sons one by one. When he came to Simeon and Levi, he went back to Shechem:

Cursed be their anger, for it was fierce;

and their wrath, for it was cruel:

“Simeon and Levi are brethren; instruments of cruelty are in their habitations. O my soul, come not thou into their secret; unto their assembly, mine honour, be not thou united: for in their anger they slew a man, and in their selfwill they digged down a wall. Cursed be their anger, for it was fierce; and their wrath, for it was cruel: I will divide them in Jacob, and scatter them in Israel.”

Genesis 49:5–7

Sin has a long memory, and consequences do not always go away when God forgives. But look at what became of Levi. His tribe was never given a block of land, only cities scattered among the others, and the work of serving at God's house. A man with a sword in Genesis 34 has descendants who carry the tabernacle in Exodus. God does not cancel what happened. He can still use what is left.

James 1:19–20

What God does next

Nobody in Genesis 34 asked God a thing. Chapter 35 opens with God speaking first, to the man who had stayed quiet:

“And God said unto Jacob, Arise, go up to Bethel, and dwell there: and make there an altar unto God, that appeared unto thee when thou fleddest from the face of Esau thy brother.”

Genesis 35:1

He does not say you and your sons are finished. He says arise, go up, back to the place where He met Jacob as a runaway with nothing. The brothers never owned what they did. You can. Your sin may be different, but the answer is the same:

“If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness.”

1 John 1:9

Today: if there is anger you are carrying, or something you did and never owned up to, call or text a brother in Christ, or your pastor. Say it plainly. That is where God starts.

Points to remember

  1. The sin“he had wrought folly in Israel in lying with Jacob’s daughter; which thing ought not to be done” 34:7Shechem’s sin was his, and the Bible calls it an outrage. Dinah is not blamed.
  2. The trap“answered Shechem and Hamor his father deceitfully” 34:13They wrapped a lie in God’s own sign. Religious words are no cover for revenge.
  3. Anger“took each man his sword, and came upon the city boldly, and slew all the males” 34:25A real wrong became a massacre, one justified step at a time. Take anger to a brother before it grows a plan.
  4. Nobody asks God“Ye have troubled me to make me to stink among the inhabitants of the land” 34:30Not once is God asked. Jacob worries about himself, and the sons about their honour.
  5. Hope“Should he deal with our sister as with an harlot?” 34:31The chapter ends with no sorry and no altar. Then chapter 35 opens with God speaking: “Arise, go up to Bethel.” He does not give up on His people.

Question 1 of 5

The sin

What was done to Dinah, and what does the Bible call it?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Shechem, the prince of the land, took Dinah and defiled her. The Bible calls it folly, an outrage that “ought not to be done.” It does not blame Dinah. His kind words and his offer to marry her afterward did not undo it, and he never said he was wrong.

Question 2 of 5

The trap

How did Jacob’s sons use the sign of God’s covenant?

What verses tell us about this?

“This is my covenant, which ye shall keep, between me and you and thy seed after thee; Every man child among you shall be circumcised. And ye shall circumcise the flesh of your foreskin; and it shall be a token of the covenant betwixt me and you.”

Genesis 17:10–11

Answer

God gave circumcision to Abraham as the sign of His promise. The brothers offered it to Shechem’s city as the price of peace and marriage, but they were lying. The Bible says plainly they answered “deceitfully.” They used what was holy as bait.

Question 3 of 5

Anger

Where did the brothers’ anger and revenge take them?

What verses tell us about this?

“Cursed be their anger, for it was fierce; and their wrath, for it was cruel: I will divide them in Jacob, and scatter them in Israel.”

Genesis 49:7

“Dearly beloved, avenge not yourselves, but rather give place unto wrath: for it is written, Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord.”

Romans 12:19

Answer

Their anger began in the right place, with a real wrong. It ended with a lie, a sword, a city of men dead, and women and children carried off. Jacob never forgot it. Anger at a wrong is not a sin, but taking vengeance into your own hands is. Take it to a brother and to the Lord before it makes a plan.

Question 4 of 5

Nobody asks God

In all of this chapter, who do Jacob and his sons ask for guidance?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Nobody. The name of God is not in the chapter. Jacob is silent, then afraid for himself. The sons justify themselves with their honour. Hamor and the men of the city count cattle. When a man leaves God out of the plan, the plan goes the way this one did.

Question 5 of 5

Hope

Did God give up on this family?

What verses tell us about this?

“And God said unto Jacob, Arise, go up to Bethel, and dwell there: and make there an altar unto God, that appeared unto thee when thou fleddest from the face of Esau thy brother.”

Genesis 35:1

“Moreover the law entered, that the offence might abound. But where sin abounded, grace did much more abound:”

Romans 5:20

“If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness.”

1 John 1:9

Answer

No. The very next chapter opens with God speaking to Jacob: “Arise, go up to Bethel.” He did not wipe out the consequences, but He did not drop the family either. Where sin abounds, grace abounds more, and the way back starts with owning it.
