Genesis 47

“Few and evil have the days of my life been”

Jacob stands before Pharaoh and tells him the truth about his life. A famine takes everything from Egypt, one thing at a time, while Israel settles and grows. And an old man asks to be buried in the land God promised.

Things to watch for

Keep these questions in the back of your mind as we read. We'll come back to every one of them at the end.

  1. BlessingWho blessed whom when Jacob stood before Pharaoh?
  2. HonestyHow did Jacob describe his life to Pharaoh?
  3. HungerWhat did the starving Egyptians give up to get bread?
  4. GoshenWhile Egypt sold itself for bread, what happened to Israel?
  5. HopeWhere did Jacob ask to be buried, and why?

Part one

Before Pharaoh

Verses 1–12

A family of shepherds stands in front of a king, and the oldest man there speaks plainly.

Old words in verses 1–12

  1. occupationwork, the way a man earns his living
  2. sojournstay for a while as a stranger (not to settle for good)
  3. pasturegrazing land for flocks
  4. men of activitycapable men, men who are good at the work
  5. pilgrimagea journey as a stranger passing through
  6. attainedreached (“have not attained unto the days”: haven't lived as long)
  7. nourishedfed and looked after

Notice how the brothers answer. They say plainly, “Thy servants are shepherds”, and they ask only “to sojourn”, to stay for a while. They don't ask for a country. Pharaoh gives them “the best of the land”.

Who is the greater man in the room?

An old foreigner, a refugee from a famine. On the other side, the most powerful ruler of his world. And the Bible says Jacob blessed him. Twice.

“And without all contradiction the less is blessed of the better.”

Hebrews 7:7

That is the rule: the one who blesses is the greater. Jacob had no army and no palace, but he carried the promise of the living God, and Pharaoh did not.

“I exhort therefore, that, first of all, supplications, prayers, intercessions, and giving of thanks, be made for all men; For kings, and for all that are in authority; that we may lead a quiet and peaceable life in all godliness and honesty.”

1 Timothy 2:1-2

You may not be anybody's idea of important either. You can still bless the man who has power over you: pray for your boss, your probation officer, the judge, the mayor. It does not need a title.

Not as long as my fathers

“Have not attained unto the days of the years of the life of my fathers.” Abraham lived 175 years, Isaac 180, and Jacob, at the end, 147 (verse 28). At 130 he is already behind them, and he knows it.

He is not boasting about his troubles, and he is not asking for pity. He is just telling the truth about where he stands.

What “few and evil” looked like

  1. ran for his lifefrom his own brother, after cheating him (chapter 27)
  2. twenty yearsunder a cheating uncle, Laban (chapter 31)
  3. buried Rachelthe wife he loved, on the road (chapter 35)
  4. a daughter wrongedand sons who answered with violence (chapter 34)
  5. lost Josephand mourned him twenty-two years (chapter 37)
  6. a faminethat sent his sons begging for grain (chapter 42)

“Evil” here means hard, full of trouble, not wicked. Some of it came to Jacob from others. Some of it he brought on himself, the grabbing and deceiving of his early years, and he does not hide that either. He just lays it on the table.

Many of you can say the same: few and evil. Be honest about it, with God and with your brothers in Christ. Don't dress it up, and don't hide it. And then keep reading, because the story does not end there.

Hard years, and a pilgrim

Jacob does not call his life a “disaster”. He calls it a pilgrimage: a journey as a stranger who is on his way somewhere. The writer of Hebrews takes it up:

“These all died in faith, not having received the promises, but having seen them afar off, and were persuaded of them, and embraced them, and confessed that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth.”

Hebrews 11:13

That is hope with its eyes open. The days may be few and hard, but a pilgrim does not live as if this road is the whole story. If you have wrecked years behind you, they are part of the road, not the destination.

Rameses is Goshen

Genesis 47:11

…gave them a possession in the land of Egypt, in the best of the land, in the land of Rameses, as Pharaoh had commanded.

Genesis 47:27

And Israel dwelt in the land of Egypt, in the country of Goshen…

Two names for the same ground. The name “Rameses” is probably the one Moses' readers knew, from the store city that Israel was forced to build there centuries later (Exodus 1:11). Genesis is telling you where this is.

And “Joseph nourished his father, and his brethren, and all his father's household, with bread, according to their families.” The same Joseph who was sold for twenty pieces of silver is now feeding the family by head count, one household at a time.

Part two

Bread for land

Verses 13–26

The famine takes Egypt's money, then its cattle, then its land, then the people themselves.

Three rounds of trading

  1. FirstTheir moneyverses 14–15
  2. That yearTheir cattleverses 16–17: horses, flocks, herds, asses
  3. The next yearTheir land, and themselvesverses 18–20: “our bodies, and our lands”

Joseph gathered the money into Pharaoh's house (verse 14). The Bible says he did what a ruler does in a famine: he stored the grain in the good years, and now he sold it. We don't have to decide whether every part was fair. What the text does show is the price of hunger.

Old words in verses 13–26

  1. faintedwore out, gave out (“fainted by reason of the famine”: starved and weak)
  2. very sorevery severe, very bad
  3. money faileththe money runs out
  4. not ought leftnothing left at all
  5. desolateempty, deserted
  6. the fifth partone share in five (twenty percent)
  7. portiona share, a fixed allowance

What a hunger costs

  1. the moneyspent first, the easy part (verse 14)
  2. the cattlewhat they lived on (verse 17)
  3. the landwhat they were leaving their children (verse 20)
  4. themselves“our bodies”: servants unto Pharaoh (verse 19)

Every man here knows this staircase. First the cash. Then the car, the tools, the things that kept the house going. Then the family's trust. And then the man himself. A craving never asks for it all at once. It takes a little more each time, and always for “bread”, for something you really feel you need.

“Jesus answered them, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Whosoever committeth sin is the servant of sin. And the servant abideth not in the house for ever: but the Son abideth ever. If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed.”

John 8:34-36

Joseph fed them, and they were grateful. But look at the difference. The one who feeds you can also own you. Jesus is the one who comes and offers the opposite: he does not buy your freedom and keep you. He makes you free “indeed”.

The fifth part

1 part in 5to Pharaoh: one fifth, twenty percent
4 parts in 5for seed, food and the family
nonefrom the priests' land, which Pharaoh already supplied

Joseph “made it a law over the land of Egypt unto this day”, which tells us that when Moses wrote, this was still the rule. Whatever else you think of the arrangement, the people were given seed and had four-fifths for themselves.

The priests keep their land: they “had a portion assigned them of Pharaoh”. Joseph's own wife was the daughter of a priest of On (41:45). The Bible simply records it, without comment.

“Thou hast saved our lives”

What it did

It kept Egypt alive

People who would have starved were fed, given seed, and left with four parts in five. “Thou hast saved our lives: let us find grace in the sight of my lord.”

What it cost

It made Pharaoh enormous

One man now owned the land and the people who worked it. A later king, “which knew not Joseph” (Exodus 1:8), would use that power on Joseph's own family.

The Bible does not praise or blame Joseph's policy here, and we won't either. It records that a hungry people were fed, and that hunger sold them into Pharaoh's hand.

There is one thing to take from it. “Let us find grace in the sight of my lord” is a hungry man's request. A believer has found grace in the sight of a Lord who bought him with his own blood, and who then hands him the keys and calls him a son.

Bought, but not owned

“What? know ye not that your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost which is in you, which ye have of God, and ye are not your own? For ye are bought with a price: therefore glorify God in your body, and in your spirit, which are God’s.”

1 Corinthians 6:19-20

Egypt was bought with bread and became Pharaoh's servants. A believer is bought too, “with a price”, and the price is the blood of Christ. But the buyer is different.

“Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free, and be not entangled again with the yoke of bondage.”

Galatians 5:1

Pharaoh bought them to keep them. Christ bought us to free us. If you are still in the bread-for-your-body staircase, call your brothers in Christ today. The yoke can come off.

Part three

An old man's last request

Verses 27–31

Israel grows in Goshen, and Jacob asks to go home when he dies.

Egypt sold. Israel grew.

Egypt

Money, cattle, land, bodies

They went out of the famine owning nothing but the seed they were given, and servants to Pharaoh.

Israel in Goshen

“Possessions… grew, and multiplied exceedingly”

Fed in the famine, settled on good ground, and the family getting bigger. Exactly what God told Jacob: “a great nation.”

Don't read this as “God makes his people rich.” Israel did well because of a promise, not because they were better than the Egyptians, and the next book shows how soon that changes. God keeps his word, even in a famine.

Jacob lived seventeen years in Egypt, and Joseph was seventeen when he was sold (37:2): seventeen years with him, twenty-two without, seventeen more with him.

Old words in verses 27–31

  1. hand under my thighthe most solemn oath of the time, taken with a hand under the thigh of the one asking
  2. deal kindly and trulytreat me with loyal love, and keep your word
  3. buryingplacea family burial ground (here the cave at Machpelah, near Hebron)
  4. swareswore, made an oath (“Swear unto me”: promise me, with an oath)
  5. nighnear (“the time drew nigh”: the time came near)
  6. found grace in thy sightto be treated with favour by someone

The thigh oath is the most solemn promise there is. The thigh is where a man's descendants come from, so a man who puts his hand there is swearing on the future of his whole family. Abraham asked his servant for the same (24:2). Jacob asked it of Joseph, so that Joseph could not forget.

“Bury me not in Egypt”

Jacob is comfortable, safe and well fed, with his son the ruler of the land. And he asks to be taken out of it.

“For they that say such things declare plainly that they seek a country. And truly, if they had been mindful of that country from whence they came out, they might have had opportunity to have returned. But now they desire a better country, that is, an heavenly: wherefore God is not ashamed to be called their God: for he hath prepared for them a city.”

Hebrews 11:14-16

This is the pilgrim's hope. Egypt was good to him, but it was not the land God promised, and he wanted his body laid where that promise would be kept.

One small thing: Hebrews 11:21 adds what Genesis leaves out. He “worshipped, leaning upon the top of his staff.” An old man sitting up at the bed's head, leaning on his staff, bowing in worship: that is Jacob's last act here.

Points to remember

  1. Blessing“Joseph brought in Jacob his father, and set him before Pharaoh: and Jacob blessed Pharaoh” 47:7A foreign old shepherd blessed the most powerful man on earth, because he carried God's promise.
  2. Honesty“few and evil have the days of the years of my life been” 47:9He did not dress it up. He was honest about the hard years, and he still called himself a pilgrim headed somewhere.
  3. Hunger“there is not ought left in the sight of my lord, but our bodies, and our lands” 47:18First their money, then their cattle, then their land, then themselves. A hunger takes a little at a time.

Question 1 of 5

Blessing

Who blessed whom when Jacob stood before Pharaoh?

What verses tell us about this?

“And without all contradiction the less is blessed of the better.”

Hebrews 7:7

“I exhort therefore, that, first of all, supplications, prayers, intercessions, and giving of thanks, be made for all men; For kings, and for all that are in authority; that we may lead a quiet and peaceable life in all godliness and honesty.”

1 Timothy 2:1-2

Answer

Jacob did. A homeless old shepherd spoke good over the ruler of the greatest country of his day. “The less is blessed of the better”: Jacob had no throne, but he carried God's promise. A believer can do the same: pray for the boss, the judge and the officials over you.

Question 2 of 5

Honesty

How did Jacob describe his life to Pharaoh?

What verses tell us about this?

“These all died in faith, not having received the promises, but having seen them afar off, and were persuaded of them, and embraced them, and confessed that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth.”

Hebrews 11:13

Answer

Honestly: “few and evil.” At a hundred and thirty he told the king his years had been hard. He didn't put on a show. But he called his life a “pilgrimage”, a journey as a stranger passing through, which is how Hebrews says men of faith saw themselves. You can be honest about hard years and still be heading somewhere.

Question 3 of 5

Hunger

What did the starving Egyptians give up to get bread?

What verses tell us about this?

“Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free, and be not entangled again with the yoke of bondage.”

Galatians 5:1

Answer

Everything: their money, their cattle, then their land and, in their own words, “our bodies”. A famine, like a craving, takes a little at a time. They thanked Joseph for the bread, but Christ offers the opposite deal: not to own us, but to make us free.

Question 4 of 5

Goshen

While Egypt sold itself for bread, what happened to Israel?

What verses tell us about this?

“And all the souls that came out of the loins of Jacob were seventy souls: for Joseph was in Egypt already. And Joseph died, and all his brethren, and all that generation. And the children of Israel were fruitful, and increased abundantly, and multiplied, and waxed exceeding mighty; and the land was filled with them.”

Exodus 1:5-7

Answer

They were fed by Joseph, settled in Goshen, and grew “exceedingly”. That was God keeping his word (“a great nation”, 46:3), not Israel earning a reward, and trouble was coming: a king “which knew not Joseph”. But in a famine that bankrupted a country, God provided.

Question 5 of 5

Hope

Where did Jacob ask to be buried, and why?

What verses tell us about this?

“For they that say such things declare plainly that they seek a country. And truly, if they had been mindful of that country from whence they came out, they might have had opportunity to have returned. But now they desire a better country, that is, an heavenly: wherefore God is not ashamed to be called their God: for he hath prepared for them a city.”

Hebrews 11:14-16

Answer

In Canaan, with Abraham and Isaac, not in Egypt. Egypt was comfortable, but it was not home, and he wanted his body laid in the land God promised. A believer can work, love his family and live in this world, and still know where he belongs.
