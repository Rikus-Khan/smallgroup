Genesis 42

Ten brothers bow, and a conscience wakes

A famine sends Jacob's sons to Egypt for grain, and the man who sells it is the brother they sold. He knows them. They do not know him. Twenty years of buried guilt come up in three words: “We are verily guilty.”

Part one

Down to Egypt for corn

Verses 1–17

Ten hungry men go shopping, and meet their brother without knowing it.

Hebron to Egypt, for bread

Canaan is in famine. Egypt has grain, because Joseph stored it up (41:47–57).

Jacob's first words: “Why do ye look one upon another?” Ten grown men stood staring at each other while their children went hungry. Shame and fear can freeze a man like that. Jacob points at the road: get you down thither, and buy.

If you are frozen in front of a mess today (a debt, a court date, an apology), do the next small step, and tell a brother in Christ you're taking it.

The brother kept at home

  • Jacobcalled Israel
    • Ten brothersgo down for corn
    • Josephgoverns Egypt
    • Benjaminkept at home

Benjamin is Joseph's only full brother, Rachel's second son, born as she died (35:16–18). Jacob holds him back: “Lest peradventure mischief befall him.” Jacob lost Rachel, he believes he lost Joseph, and he cannot stand to lose the last piece of her.

Fear is not wicked. But Jacob's fear is about to decide what ten men must do, and it will cost them months of hunger. A man who has been hurt can end up running his whole house by the hurt. If you know that man, it may be you. Say it out loud to your pastor.

The dream, and the day

Joseph's dream, about twenty years before

“For, behold, we were binding sheaves in the field, and, lo, my sheaf arose, and also stood upright; and, behold, your sheaves stood round about, and made obeisance to my sheaf.”

Genesis 37:7

The day itself

“And Joseph was the governor over the land, and he it was that sold to all the people of the land: and Joseph’s brethren came, and bowed down themselves before him with their faces to the earth.”

Genesis 42:6

No one planned it. The brothers walked in for bread and, without knowing, did what they had said would never happen. The sin that tried to stop the dream (the pit, the sale) became the road that carried it out.

Why didn't they know him? Twenty years, and an Egyptian lord's dress and speech, with an interpreter at his side (verse 23). They were looking for a slave or a dead man. They never thought to look up.

“He made himself strange”

וַיִּתְנַכֵּר said vayitnakkar “made himself strange”: acted like a stranger, kept himself unrecognized

Joseph kept the mask on and spoke harshly (“roughly”). Why? The text does not say at this point. But watch what he does next: he tests them again and again, and by chapter 45 you can see what he was looking for. Was there any change in these men? Would they treat the youngest brother as they treated him?

One more thing. He was not acting from revenge. He weeps in secret in verse 24, and later he says plainly, “God did send me before you to preserve life” (45:5). A man with power and a grudge would have finished them in minute one.

“Ye are spies”

Joseph (as the lord of Egypt)You are spies. You have come to see where the land is weak.

The brothersNo, my lord. We are all one man's sons. We are true men.

JosephNo. You have come to see where the land is weak.

The brothersTwelve sons. The youngest is with our father, and one is not. said to Joseph's face

“The nakedness of the land” means the thin places where a country is open to attack. And “one is not”: they say it casually to Joseph's own face. They have said it so long it has stopped feeling like a lie.

A pit for him, a ward for them

Joseph, years ago (37:24)

A pit

An empty cistern. No water. His brothers above him, and he “besought” them (42:21), begging for his life. Then they sat down to eat.

The brothers, now

A ward, three days

“Ward” means custody: a guarded lock-up. They are shut in with nothing to do but think, and the thing they think about is not Egypt. It is the pit.

Joseph does not throw them in a hole. A ward is not a pit. But for the first time, the brothers are on the inside of a locked door, wondering if they will be killed. It is a small taste of what they did, and it works. Three days of quiet is enough to bring a buried thing to the top.

“By the life of Pharaoh” was how an Egyptian swore an oath. Joseph speaks like an Egyptian, because that is who they think he is.

Part two

A conscience wakes

Verses 18–28

Twenty years of silence, and then one sentence.

He changed the terms

Day one (verse 16)

One goes, nine stay

Send one man for Benjamin. Everyone else sits in prison. Their families, back home, are still starving.

Day three (verses 19–20)

Nine go, one stays

Carry corn home for the famine of your houses. One brother is held until you bring Benjamin. Everyone else goes free.

Between the two offers, one sentence: “This do, and live; for I fear God.” A governor of Egypt says it, and means it. Joseph cannot do whatever he wants with these men, because he answers to God. A man who fears God is safe to be under, because he stops where he ought to.

It also tells the brothers something they are not hearing yet: this strange ruler thinks about right and wrong the way their own father's God does.

Buried, but not dead

For twenty years the brothers carried one lie and told it every day. And the conscience stayed quiet. Then came trouble, and out it came: “We are verily guilty.” “Verily” means truly.

They name it. “The anguish of his soul, when he besought us, and we would not hear.” No excuse, no softer word. Just what they did, and what they heard.

That is where mercy can start. A sin that is out in the open can be dealt with. Keep it buried and it only gets heavier.

What a conscience says, and what to do with it

David knew that weight, and so did the brothers. A conscience that wakes up is not your enemy. It is telling you the truth, and the truth is where healing begins.

“I acknowledged my sin unto thee, and mine iniquity have I not hid. I said, I will confess my transgressions unto the LORD; and thou forgavest the iniquity of my sin. Selah.”

Psalm 32:5

“If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness.”

1 John 1:9

The brothers admitted it only to each other, and only when afraid. The next step is David's: bring it to God, and to a brother or your pastor. A man in Christ does not need twenty years of trouble to do it. Today works.

Reuben: “I told you so”

ReubenDidn't I say, Do not sin against the child, and you would not listen? verse 22

Reuben, in 37:21–22Let's not kill him. Throw him in the pit. a secret plan to rescue him later

Reuben was right that he spoke up. He also wasn't there when it mattered. He had planned to get Joseph out of the pit, and while he was gone, the others sold him. His “I told you so” has a thread of defence in it: my hands are clean, it was you.

“His blood is required” means a debt of life is being collected: God holds men to account for a brother's blood. They think Joseph is dead. They are afraid the bill has come.

When you finally see your sin, do not spend the energy on who was more to blame. Say your part and leave the rest. That is where a man in recovery starts.

He turned away and wept

The brothers, in HebrewWe are verily guilty. The anguish of his soul… his blood is required. they think no one understands

Joseph, who understood every wordHe turned himself about from them, and wept.

An “interpreter” stood between them. The brothers spoke Hebrew to each other, thinking he couldn't follow. So he heard, for the first time, what they really thought about the pit, and left the room to cry.

These are the first of many tears (43:30; 45:2, 14–15; 46:29; 50:1, 17). A ruler of a nation, and a man who weeps. The Bible shows both without shame.

Why Simeon? The text does not say, so we should not guess. He was bound before their eyes and stays in Egypt until they return.

One sack, two readings

What Joseph did

A gift

He filled their sacks with grain, gave them food for the road, and quietly put every man's silver back in his sack. He paid for it all himself.

What the brothers heard

A charge

“Their heart failed them, and they were afraid, saying one to another, What is this that God hath done unto us?” Not what a kindness. They think they are being set up.

A guilty conscience cannot receive a gift. Everything that happens sounds like the bill coming due. They had just said “We are verily guilty,” and the first unexpected thing that happens, they read it as God's hand coming down.

If you have been guilty a long time, you may find you are suspicious of kindness. When a brother is good to you, you wait for the catch. Hear it from the other side: the silver was a gift. It was put there on purpose, by someone who loved them. That is how Christ's forgiveness sits in a man's sack: paid, and put in, before he knew to ask.

Part three

Home to Jacob

Verses 29–38

Every sack tells the same story, and an old man says no.

“The man, the lord of the country”

They tell Jacob everything, but notice the name. They never say Joseph, because they don't know it's him. To them he is only “the man, who is the lord of the land.” Chapter 43 keeps the same phrase: “the man.” The brothers are talking about their brother in the third person for three chapters.

“Traffick” means to trade: buy and sell freely. The offer is good news: bring the youngest, and you can come and go, buying and selling, in Egypt.

The sacks have not been opened yet at home. Notice what they say to Jacob about Simeon, and about the spy charge. They tell all of it but their guilt in verse 21, and that part they kept to themselves.

Reuben's offer, and Jacob's answer

ReubenSlay my two sons if I do not bring him back. a bold offer

JacobMy son shall not go down with you. not even listened to

Reuben offers the lives of his two sons, as a pledge. It is a big gesture, and it sounds loyal. But it is not his own life he puts up; it is two boys who have no say. And Jacob says no. A promise that costs someone else is not much of a promise.

Keep that in mind. In chapter 43 another brother makes a pledge for Benjamin, and he puts himself on the line. That brother is Judah.

“All these things are against me”

  1. Joseph is nothe thinks he is dead
  2. Simeon is notheld in Egypt
  3. Benjamin to gothe last of Rachel's sons
  4. “All these things are against me”the sum he reaches

Jacob adds up every fact he has, and the total is despair. But he is missing the biggest fact of all: Joseph is alive, and he is the one holding the corn. Simeon is safe. The money in the sacks was a kindness. Every one of the things Jacob counts “against” him is God working for him.

“And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose.”

Romans 8:28

A man can have every true fact and still reach the wrong total, because he is reading the middle of the story. If it looks like everything is against you today, you may be missing a fact too. Do not decide the ending alone. Talk to your brothers in Christ and your pastor, and let them add what you cannot see.

Where the bread was

Ten men bowed to a stranger who had everything they needed. The stranger was the brother they had rejected. They could not eat without him.

“And Jesus said unto them, I am the bread of life: he that cometh to me shall never hunger; and he that believeth on me shall never thirst.”

John 6:35

The Bible does not call Joseph a picture of Christ in so many words, and Joseph was a sinner like us. But the pattern is there: the one rejected by his brothers ends up the one who holds the bread, and the hungry have to come to him. The difference is this. Joseph made them wait and prove themselves. Jesus says to all of us, come, as you are.

Twenty years in the middle

  1. about 1898 BCSold at seventeenthe pit at Dothan
  2. about 1885 BCRuler of Egypt at thirty41:46

Twenty years or more. The brothers lived with it all that time: the lie, the father's grief, the silence. It was not hidden from God, and it was not too late. Twenty years is long, but the conscience woke, and the way back opened.

If you have a long-buried thing behind you, this chapter says it can still come to light, and that is not the end of you. In the next two chapters the same men get the chance to do differently.

Question 1 of 6

Action

What did Jacob tell his sons to do when they stood staring at the famine?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Go down to Egypt and buy grain. Jacob will not let ten men stand around looking at each other while the family starves. And God had already put grain in Egypt for them. If you are stuck, do the next small thing today, and tell a brother in Christ you are doing it.

Question 3 of 6

Regret

What did the brothers' consciences say when trouble came?

What verses tell us about this?

“If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness.”

1 John 1:9

Answer

“We are verily guilty.” Twenty years of silence did not kill it. A buried sin comes up, usually in trouble. The better road is to bring it out on purpose, to God and to a brother or your pastor, where it can be forgiven and does not have to keep costing.

Question 4 of 6

Grace

What did Joseph do when he heard his brothers admit their guilt?

What verses tell us about this?

“But God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us.”

Romans 5:8

Answer

He turned away and wept. The man they wronged was moved before they had asked anything of him. That is a small picture of the God who loved us “while we were yet sinners.” Mercy begins on His side, before we do anything right.

Question 5 of 6

Guilt

How did the brothers hear the kindness of the money in their sacks?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

As a charge, not a gift. Their hearts failed them and they said, “What is this that God hath done unto us?” A guilty conscience hears a kindness as a trap. If a brother is kind to you and you wait for the catch, say thank you. It may just be a gift.

Question 6 of 6

Hope

Was everything really against Jacob?

What verses tell us about this?

“And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose.”

Romans 8:28

Answer

No. Joseph was alive and in charge of the grain, Simeon was safe, and the money in the sacks was a kindness. Jacob had every fact but the biggest one. If it feels like everything is against you, you may be missing a fact too. Call or text your brothers in Christ, and your pastor, and ask what they can see.
