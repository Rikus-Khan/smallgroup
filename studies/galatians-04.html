No more a servant, but a son

God sent His Son to buy us out and sent His Spirit into our hearts to call Him Father. So why would a free son walk back to the old chains?

Teachers had come to the churches of Galatia saying a Gentile believer had to be circumcised and keep the law of Moses to be right with God. In chapter 3 Paul answered: we are children of God by faith, and the law was only the slave who walked us to Christ.

Chapter 4 finishes the argument. First with a picture from family life: a boy who owns everything but isn't grown yet. Then from the heart, as a father to his sons. Then with a story from Genesis: two mothers and two sons.

Part one

No more a servant

Verses 1–7

A rich man's boy owns it all, but he still takes orders. Until the day his father sets.

Lord of all, and still under orders

In a wealthy home the son owned the estate on paper, but while he was a boy, household servants ran his days. He had no more say than they did. The father chose the day he would come of age.

That's where God's people were under the law, Paul says: heirs, but kept like children under the elements, the ABCs of religion, rules about what to touch and which days to keep. Then the Father's day came.

The fulness of the time

  1. The promiseto Abrahamabout 2000 BC
  2. The lawat Sinai430 years later
  3. Silenceno prophet after Malachiabout 400 years
  4. One worldRoman roads, Greek spoken everywhereRome rules
  5. God sent forth His Sonmade of a womanabout 6–4 BC

God wasn't late. He waited until everything was ready: the promise, the law that showed men their need, and a world where the news could travel fast.

“Made of a woman”: truly a man, born like us. “Made under the law”: He lived under it and kept every word of it. He came under the law to buy out the men who were stuck under it, and to make them sons.

A son's word

Abba Aramaic, the language of Jesus' home Father: what a son calls his own dad

It's the word Jesus prayed in the garden the night before the cross:

“And he said, Abba, Father, all things are possible unto thee; take away this cup from me: nevertheless not what I will, but what thou wilt.”

Mark 14:36

God sent His Son for you (4:4), then sent the Spirit of His Son into you (4:6). Now the same word is in your mouth. You get to talk to God the way Jesus did.

For the man whose father wasn't there

The father some of us had

Gone, or hard

Never around. Or around and drunk, angry, quick with his hands. Maybe the only thing he ever said about you was what you'd never be.

The Father you have now

“Abba”

He sent His own Son to get you. He put His Spirit in you. He calls you son and heir (4:7), and He's not going anywhere.

“When my father and my mother forsake me, then the LORD will take me up.”

Psalm 27:10

“For ye have not received the spirit of bondage again to fear; but ye have received the Spirit of adoption, whereby we cry, Abba, Father.”

Romans 8:15

Adopted, with everything that comes with it

servant → son Many Bible teachers point out that a Roman adopted son took his new father's name, his old debts were wiped away, and he became a full heir, just as if he'd been born into the family. “If a son, then an heir.”

A servant works for a wage and can be let go. A son belongs. You don't stay in the family by earning your keep. You're in because the Father brought you in.

Part two

Back to the old chains?

Verses 8–20

Paul can't understand it: free men walking back to the cell. Then he stops arguing and speaks to them like a mother.

Why walk back in?

For the Galatians, the old chains were religious: special days, months and years, kept to stay right with God. Paul calls them weak (no power to save) and beggarly (nothing to give you).

For us it might be a bottle, a pipe, an old crowd, or rules we keep to feel good enough. A son of God doesn't stop being a son. But he can walk back into a cell he holds the key to.

“Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free, and be not entangled again with the yoke of bondage.”

Galatians 5:1

When they first met

On his first journey Paul came to these towns sick, through “infirmity of the flesh.” We aren't told what it was. Many think it was his eyes (4:15). At Lystra he was stoned and left for dead (Acts 14:19).

They didn't turn him away. They took him in “as an angel of God,” and they'd have given him their own eyes.

Now he tells them the truth and they treat him like an enemy. The brother who tells you the truth when you're sliding isn't your enemy. He's the one who loves you.

My little children

The false teachers courted the Galatians to cut them off from Paul, so they'd need them instead (4:17). An old crowd does the same: flattery first, then you can't leave.

Paul talks like a mother in labour. He went through the pain once to bring them to birth; now he goes through it again, until Christ is formed in them, until they look like Him.

That takes time, and it hurts. If you're not who you want to be yet, you're not finished. You're being formed. “He which hath begun a good work in you will perform it” (Philippians 1:6).

Part three

Two mothers, two sons

Verses 21–31

You want to be under the law? Then listen to what the law itself says about Abraham's two sons.

The story behind it

God promised Abraham a son. Years went by, and Sarah had no children. So they made a plan of their own (Genesis 16):

“And Sarai said unto Abram, Behold now, the LORD hath restrained me from bearing: I pray thee, go in unto my maid; it may be that I may obtain children by her. And Abram hearkened to the voice of Sarai.”

Genesis 16:2

Ishmael, Hagar's son

After the flesh

The natural way, by a man's own plan to help God out. It brought years of trouble into the family.

Isaac, Sarah's son

By promise

Born when Abraham was a hundred and Sarah ninety. Only God could do it, so only God got the credit.

We know that move: when God seems slow, we fix it ourselves. Paul says that's the difference between the flesh and the promise.

Two mothers, two cities

An allegory uses a true story as a picture. Genesis really happened; Paul shows what it pictures. Mount Sinai, down in Arabia, is where the law was given. Earthly Jerusalem, still under the law, is Hagar's family. Every believer belongs to the free city.

Cast it out

At the feast for young Isaac, Ishmael was caught mocking him. Sarah said:

“Wherefore she said unto Abraham, Cast out this bondwoman and her son: for the son of this bondwoman shall not be heir with my son, even with Isaac.”

Genesis 21:10

Bondage and freedom can't share the house. The voice of the law still mocks the son of promise: “You? After what you did? Earn it first.” Don't negotiate with that voice. Cast it out. You're not on probation in God's house. You're a son and an heir.

Question 1 of 6

The heir

How is an heir who is still a child like a servant?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

He owns everything on paper, but while he's a boy he lives under tutors and governors with no more say than a servant, until the day his father sets. God's people under the law were like that: heirs, but kept in bondage to the basic rules.

Question 2 of 6

Sent

What did God do when the fulness of the time was come?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

He sent His Son, born of a woman and born under the law, to buy back those who were under the law, so that we could be adopted as sons. God's timing was exactly right.

Question 3 of 6

Abba

What does it mean to call God Abba, Father?

What verses tell us about this?

“For ye have not received the spirit of bondage again to fear; but ye have received the Spirit of adoption, whereby we cry, Abba, Father.”

Romans 8:15

Answer

It means we belong to God as sons, not servants. The Spirit of His Son lives in us and calls God by the same close word Jesus used. A son is also an heir: whatever our earthly father was, God is a Father who stays.

Question 4 of 6

Going back

Why would a free man go back to his chains?

What verses tell us about this?

“Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free, and be not entangled again with the yoke of bondage.”

Galatians 5:1

Answer

Because the old ways feel familiar and promise something. But they are weak and beggarly: they can't save and they have nothing to give. Paul begs them to stand in the freedom Christ gave, not to be tied up again.

Question 5 of 6

Little children

How did Paul feel about these struggling believers?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

He loved them like a mother in labour. He told them hard truth, but he called them his little children and was willing to go through the pain again until Christ was formed in them.

Question 6 of 6

Two sons

What do the two sons of Abraham teach us?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

One son was born of a slave, by Abraham's own plan; the other was born free, by God's promise. They picture the law that brings bondage and the promise that brings freedom. Believers are children of promise, free sons, and the bondage must be cast out.
