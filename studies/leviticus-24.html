Leviticus 24

Oil and bread, always; and a man who cursed the name

Lamps that never go out and twelve cakes that are never missing from the table. Then, in the camp, a quarrel, a man who curses the name of the LORD, and a LORD who says what is to be done, with one law for every man.

Part one

“To cause the lamps to burn continually”

Verses 1–9

The oil for the lamps and the bread on the table, before the LORD.

Light in a room with no window

The holy place had no window. The only light inside the tent came from the candlestick, a lampstand of seven lamps, and the lamps burned olive oil.

Israel brought the oil, and Aaron kept the lamps. It was a shared job: the people gave and the priest tended.

Oil for the lamps

  • pure oil oliveclear olive oil
  • beatencrushed out of the olives, the clearest oil
  • orderarrange and tend
  • continuallywithout stopping: “from the evening unto the morning”

Four times in these verses the word continually comes (2, 3, 4, 8): three times for the lamps and once for the bread. The LORD asks for something that does not stop.

The light that does not go out

“Then spake Jesus again unto them, saying, I am the light of the world: he that followeth me shall not walk in darkness, but shall have the light of life.”

John 8:12

Jesus says it of himself. The man who follows him “shall not walk in darkness.” Like the lamps, he is not a light that comes on when we feel good and goes off when we fail. There is a Keeper. “From the evening unto the morning,” the night is the lamp's own time.

Some of you know what the nights are like. The night is not the time the lamp goes out. It is the time it was lit for.

Twelve cakes before the LORD

  • cakesflat loaves of fine flour, “two tenth deals” each, two tenths of an ephah
  • frankincensea sweet resin burned for its smell
  • a memoriala reminder, a sign that he is remembered
  • everlasting covenanta promise that does not end

New bread every sabbath, set in order “before the LORD continually.” Twelve is the number of Israel's tribes: the whole people on the table, in front of him, every week. And the old bread was taken away to be eaten: “it shall be Aaron’s and his sons’; and they shall eat it in the holy place.” It is called the shewbread, the bread set before his face.

Bread only for the priests?

The rule is plain: it was “most holy,” and it belonged to Aaron and his sons. When David was running for his life, he ate it. And Jesus used it to teach the men who were criticising his disciples:

“But he said unto them, Have ye not read what David did, when he was an hungred, and they that were with him; How he entered into the house of God, and did eat the shewbread, which was not lawful for him to eat, neither for them which were with him, but only for the priests?”

Matthew 12:3-4

Jesus does not rebuke David. A hungry man was fed from the bread of God's house, and it is the Lord of the sabbath who tells the story. The bread points past itself to him:

“And Jesus said unto them, I am the bread of life: he that cometh to me shall never hunger; and he that believeth on me shall never thirst.”

John 6:35

The bread that was always on the table before the LORD, and the lamps that were always lit: Jesus is the one, and he is the other. In him there is no empty table and no dark room. And all believers are now “a royal priesthood” (1 Peter 2:9): we eat in the holy place.

Part two

“The son of an Israelitish woman”

Verses 10–16

A fight in the camp, a curse, and a wait for the LORD's word.

What happened, in order

  1. a quarrelin the campverse 10
  2. a cursehe blasphemed the nameverse 11
  3. brought to Mosesby those who heardverse 11
  4. put in warduntil the LORD saysverse 12
  5. the LORD speaksand the judgmentverses 13–16

Strove means fought, quarrelled, with fists or words. Blasphemed means spoke against God with contempt, and cursed means he did it with a curse. Ward is custody: they held him, and they waited. In the heat of a quarrel, a man's worst words come out.

Who he was

  • his motherShelomith, the daughter of Dibri, of the tribe of Dan
  • his fatheran Egyptian
  • his namenot given: the Bible calls him only “the son of an Israelitish woman”

When Israel left Egypt, “a mixed multitude went up also with them” (Exodus 12:38). Egyptians had married in and come out with them. This man lived in the camp, among the tribes, with a mother of Dan and a father from the land they had left. We do not know what the quarrel was about, and Moses does not say.

Notice what the story does not say. It does not say he was judged for his father's blood. It says he “blasphemed the name of the LORD, and cursed.” It was the words, and the name they were aimed at. The third commandment was already written on the tables of stone:

“Thou shalt not take the name of the LORD thy God in vain; for the LORD will not hold him guiltless that taketh his name in vain.”

Exodus 20:7

They did not decide it themselves

The men who heard him did not drag him out and stone him on the spot. They did not guess the penalty. They brought him to Moses, and “they put him in ward, that the mind of the LORD might be shewed them.” They waited for God's word before anyone acted.

The Bible says the same at another time, of a man who broke the sabbath: “And they put him in ward, because it was not declared what should be done to him” (Numbers 15:34). In a hot moment, with a fresh outrage and people shouting, the right thing was to hold him and ask the LORD.

That is worth a man's attention in his own life. When you are angry, or when someone has wronged you, the first move is not to act. It is to wait, and ask.

The sentence, plainly

  • without the campoutside the camp, not in the middle of it
  • lay their hands upon his headthe ones who heard him, as witnesses, put their hands on him
  • all the congregationthe whole people, not one man
  • shall bear his sinshall carry the guilt of it

This was the civil law of Israel, handed to Moses for the courts of that nation. It was calm and public: a hearing, a word from the LORD, witnesses, and the whole congregation. A man's hands were laid on the head of the one he had heard curse God, as witnesses, and the sentence was carried out outside the camp. It was not a lynching.

Why so heavy? Because the LORD himself dwelt in the middle of that camp, in the tent. His name is himself, and cursing it was cursing the one who was among them. And in Israel “as well the stranger, as he that is born in the land” (16) was bound by the same law.

Taken outside the camp

The man who blasphemed was taken “without the camp.” Centuries later, another man was condemned for blasphemy:

“Then the high priest rent his clothes, saying, He hath spoken blasphemy; what further need have we of witnesses? behold, now ye have heard his blasphemy.”

Matthew 26:65

Jesus was accused of the very sin this chapter punishes. He was not guilty: he was the Son of God, and he said so. And he too was taken outside:

“Wherefore Jesus also, that he might sanctify the people with his own blood, suffered without the gate. Let us go forth therefore unto him without the camp, bearing his reproach.”

Hebrews 13:12-13

He bore the curse

Paul says what that meant:

“Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree:”

Galatians 3:13

“Whosoever curseth his God shall bear his sin.” The blasphemer bore it. The One who never cursed anyone, and prayed for those who hurt him, bore the curse of the ones who do. That is the reason a man who has used the name of God for a curse does not have to be turned out of the camp for good.

Is there a way back for a blasphemer?

Paul was one. He says it himself:

“Who was before a blasphemer, and a persecutor, and injurious: but I obtained mercy, because I did it ignorantly in unbelief. And the grace of our Lord was exceeding abundant with faith and love which is in Christ Jesus. This is a faithful saying, and worthy of all acceptation, that Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners; of whom I am chief.”

1 Timothy 1:13-15

“A blasphemer, and a persecutor, and injurious: but I obtained mercy.” The chief of sinners, by his own word, and he was mercifully taken in. If your mouth has been the worst thing about you, with God's name in it, with curses at home, you are not past Christ's reach. He came into the world to save sinners, and that includes the one who cursed.

The mouth is a hard thing to bring under. Many of you cursed from habit before you knew him. The answer is not to hold your tongue by force. It is to hallow the name, and ask:

“After this manner therefore pray ye: Our Father which art in heaven, Hallowed be thy name.”

Matthew 6:9

Part three

“One manner of law”

Verses 17–23

Life for life, loss for loss, and the same law for the stranger and the born.

Fair, and no more than fair

  • a man killed“shall surely be put to death” (17, 21)
  • a beast killed“make it good; beast for beast”: pay it back, like for like (18)
  • a blemish caused“as he hath done, so shall it be done to him” (19)
  • breach for breacha break for a break, “eye for eye, tooth for tooth” (20)

Look at the word in verse 19: blemish. It is the same word as in chapters 21 and 22, where a blemish kept a priest from the altar and an animal from the offering. Here it is something a man does to a neighbour. A man who hurts his neighbour makes a blemish in him.

The rule here is a limit and a measure for a judge: no more than the harm done, and the same for everyone, rich or poor. It was written for Israel's courts. It was never an order to a private man to go and get even.

For the court

“as he hath done, so shall it be done to him”

A fair measure for a judge: the penalty matches the wrong, no more.

For your own hand

“resist not evil”

Jesus takes the revenge out of a man's own hands.

“Ye have heard that it hath been said, An eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth: But I say unto you, That ye resist not evil: but whosoever shall smite thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also.”

Matthew 5:38-39

Jesus does not scrap the measure. He tells his own men that the measure belongs to the judge, and not to them. If someone has done you wrong, you are not the one who evens it. And if you have wronged someone, here is what the law gave: “shall make it good.” Say sorry, and pay it back.

“Make it good”

The killing of a beast is a small thing beside the killing of a man, and the law does not let it pass. “He that killeth a beast shall make it good; beast for beast.” Verse 21 says it again: “he that killeth a beast, he shall restore it.”

Restoring is the point. A man who has done damage, to a neighbour's property or to a neighbour's heart, owes more than a sorry face. For the men in this room that is a map: write down the people you harmed, and with the help of your pastor and your brothers in Christ, make it right wherever you can, and do not make things worse.

Some things cannot be put back. Make good the part you can, and bring the rest to the Lord who forgives.

One law for everyone

the strangerone of your own country“one manner of law” (22). The man with an Egyptian father stood under the same law as the man with two Israelite parents.
the man in the middle of the campthe man at the edgeThe law was the same, and so was the mercy.

The chapter began with Israel bringing oil and bread for the LORD, and it ends with Israel doing “as the LORD commanded Moses” (23). The same law covered the born Israelite and the stranger, the same “I am the LORD your God.” There was no second law for the man who did not belong.

Real world: a light, a table, and a mouth

  • SinA quarrel, a hot moment, and a name taken in vain. Many of us have done it, and done worse.
  • Regret“Whosoever curseth his God shall bear his sin.” Someone bore it for you. A man who has cursed God is not beyond the Lord who prayed for those who killed him.
  • GraceThe lamps burn “continually” and the bread is always on the table. You are not a light that must keep itself lit.
  • HopePaul was a blasphemer and obtained mercy. So can you.
  • TodayWhen anger comes, hold it, like the men who put the man in ward, and ask what the LORD says before you answer. And if you owe someone a wrong made good, begin with one name today.

Points to remember

  1. The oil“pure oil olive beaten for the light, to cause the lamps to burn continually” 24:2Israel brought it, Aaron kept the lamps. The light that does not go out is Christ, the light of the world.
  2. The bread“bake twelve cakes thereof… set them in two rows, six on a row, upon the pure table before the LORD” 24:5–6Twelve cakes, new every sabbath, then eaten by Aaron and his sons in the holy place.
  3. The quarrel“the Israelitish woman’s son blasphemed the name of the LORD, and cursed” 24:11His mother was Shelomith, the daughter of Dibri, of the tribe of Dan; his father was an Egyptian. He cursed in a quarrel in the camp.
  4. The wait“they put him in ward, that the mind of the LORD might be shewed them” 24:12They held him and waited for the LORD's word before anyone acted.
  5. The sentence“Bring forth him that hath cursed without the camp; and let all that heard him lay their hands upon his head” 24:14Outside the camp, with the witnesses' hands on his head. Jesus also suffered without the gate, and bore the curse.
  6. One law“Ye shall have one manner of law, as well for the stranger, as for one of your own country” 24:22The same law for the stranger and the born, because “I am the LORD your God.”
