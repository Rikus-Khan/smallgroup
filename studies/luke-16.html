Part one

The unjust steward

Verses 1–13

A manager about to be fired cooks the books on his way out, and his boss is impressed.

A steward caught

A steward ran a rich man's estate for him: the land, the crops, the accounts. He could make deals and sign for his master. This one was reported for wasting his master's goods, the same word Luke used for the younger son who “wasted his substance” (15:13).

Jesus is still talking, the same day. The publicans, the Pharisees and the disciples are all listening (15:1–2, 16:14). This story is aimed at the disciples, and it's about money.

“Give an account”: hand in the books, you're done. Every one of us will give account to God for what He put in our hands.

What shall I do?

  • “I cannot dig”Too soft for hard labour in the fields.
  • “To beg I am ashamed”Too proud to sit by the road with his hand out.
  • Make friends while he still can“that … they may receive me into their houses”

He had a few hours left with the books and the master's name. He knew his future depended on what he did with them before the door shut. He didn't waste the time feeling sorry for himself. He moved.

Cooking the books

The first debtor's bill

10050 measures of oil

hundreds of gallons of olive oil
The second debtor's bill

10080 measures of wheat

hundreds of bushels of wheat

A bill was a written note of what a man owed. The steward had each debtor rewrite his own bill in his own hand, quickly, before word got out. Fourscore is eighty.

Each man walked away owing his master far less and owing the steward a big favour. He was spending his master's money to buy himself friends.

Praised for what?

The lord here is the rich man in the story, not Jesus. He still calls the steward unjust. He didn't praise the cheating. He admired the man's sharpness: “he had done wisely,” shrewdly. He saw his day coming and got ready for it.

The children of this world

Plan for next year

They work every angle to set themselves up for the future they can see.

The children of light

Forget forever

God's people often give less thought to eternity than a crook gives to his retirement.

Friends with mammon

Mammon is money and possessions. It's “of unrighteousness” because it belongs to this crooked world, and it so easily pulls men into sin.

Here's the point. Money won't last. One day it runs out, or you do: “when ye fail.” So use it now for what lasts, which is people. Help a brother, feed the hungry, support the work that brings men to Christ. Some of the people you help will be in heaven to welcome you, in everlasting habitations.

That's not buying a way into heaven. Salvation is a free gift by faith. It's spending wisely, because you already know where you're going.

Little things show big things

Ifyou can't be trusted with money, the smallest thing God gives you,
→
thenwho will trust you with the true riches?

Money is the least thing God trusts us with. The true riches are His Word, His people, the souls of men. And it's all another man's: we only manage what is God's.

Many of us burned through money and trust. Trust is rebuilt in little things: pay the small bill, return what you borrowed, show up when you said you would.

One master

“Ye cannot serve God and mammon.”

A slave in that world belonged to one owner, all day, every day. Two owners would pull him in two directions until he loved one and hated the other. Jesus doesn't say you shouldn't serve both. He says you can't.

Money makes a good servant and a terrible master. So does anything a man starts living for: the bottle, the pipe, the paycheck, the next win. Whatever runs your life is your master. There's room for only one.

Part two

God knoweth your hearts

Verses 14–18

The Pharisees love money and laugh at Jesus. He answers with the law they claimed to keep.

They laughed

Covetous means greedy, always wanting more. They derided Jesus: sneered at Him. Many of them taught that wealth proved God was pleased with you. A poor teacher telling rich men how to use money was a joke to them.

Before men

Highly esteemed

They looked good, prayed in public, gave where people could see, and grew rich.

In the sight of God

Abomination

He saw the greed and pride underneath. God looks at the heart.

“For the love of money is the root of all evil: which while some coveted after, they have erred from the faith, and pierced themselves through with many sorrows.”

1 Timothy 6:10

Until John

  1. Mosesthe law given
  2. The prophetsIsaiah to Malachi
  3. Silenceabout four hundred years
  4. John the Baptistthe last of the old prophets
  5. The kingdom of Godpreached by Jesus, now

The law and the prophets pointed ahead. John pointed at Jesus. Now the good news is out, and all kinds of men are pressing in, forcing their way in like a crowd at a gate: publicans, sinners, men like us.

But the new day doesn't throw out the old law. A tittle is the tiny stroke that tells one Hebrew letter from another. Heaven and earth will go before the smallest stroke of God's Word fails. And the Pharisees, who claimed to keep it, were bending it.

An example: marriage

Here's one place the Pharisees bent the law. Some of their teachers let a man put away, divorce, his wife for almost any reason, then marry someone else, all properly on paper. Jesus says God sees it as adultery. Marriage is meant to last for life.

Matthew records Jesus giving one exception, “except it be for fornication” (Matthew 19:9). Luke gives the short form of the teaching. Jesus held marriage higher than the Pharisees did, not lower.

Some men in this room have broken marriages behind them. This verse isn't here to crush you, and we're not ruling on anyone's situation today. If it hits home, talk with the pastor. The Father who ran to the prodigal forgives every sin we confess, this one too.

Part three

A great gulf fixed

Verses 19–31

Two men, one gate. Then death, and the gate between them is gone, and something far wider takes its place.

Two men at one gate

The rich man

No name

Purple, the costliest dye there was, and fine linen. He fared sumptuously: feasted in luxury, not now and then, but every day.

The beggar

Lazarus

Too sick to walk, laid at the gate each day, covered in sores, hoping for the scraps. Street dogs licked his sores, and he couldn't drive them off.

This isn't the Lazarus of Bethany from John 11. His name means “God helps,” and he's the only person in any of Jesus' stories who gets a name. The world knew the rich man's name and ignored the beggar. Jesus did the opposite.

Everything turned over

Comfort“good things”

Misery“evil things”

The rich manpurple, linen, feasting; then a funeral
Lazarussores, dogs, crumbs; then carried by angels

At death they trade places. Lazarus is carried to Abraham's bosom, the place of honour at a feast, leaning beside father Abraham. The rich man “was buried,” probably in style, and wakes up in torment.

Money didn't damn the rich man, and poverty didn't save Lazarus. The rich man lived for himself and never turned to God (16:30). Lazarus, “God helps,” had nothing to lean on but God.

No crossing over

Jesus speaks plainly about hell, more than anyone else in the Bible. Here it's real and conscious. The rich man can see, feel, speak and remember. He asks for one drop of water and can't have it. And the great gulf is fixed: after death, nobody crosses either way.

This side of the gulf

Jesus didn't say this to scare men for sport. He said it so nobody would end up there. He went to the cross to carry our sin, so whosoever believes in Him can be on Abraham's side of that gulf.

The time to settle where you'll spend forever is on this side of the gulf, while you can still hear and turn. Not someday. Today.

“(For he saith, I have heard thee in a time accepted, and in the day of salvation have I succoured thee: behold, now is the accepted time; behold, now is the day of salvation.)”

2 Corinthians 6:2

“Send Lazarus”

Notice how he talks. Even in torment he's giving orders: send Lazarus with water, send him to my brothers. He still sees the beggar as a servant. He never once speaks to Lazarus himself.

But he does care about his five brethren, his brothers, still living the way he lived. He doesn't want them to come where he is.

Abraham's answer: they already have what they need. Moses and the prophets, the Scriptures. God had already spoken. They only had to listen.

Though one rose from the dead

Ifa man won't listen to what God has said,
→
thena miracle won't change his mind either.

It came true. Lazarus of Bethany rose from the dead, and the chief priests plotted to kill him too (John 12:10). Then Jesus rose, and many still refused. We don't need a voice from the grave. We have the Bible and a risen Saviour.

“For had ye believed Moses, ye would have believed me: for he wrote of me.”

John 5:46

  3. Faithful“Faithful in that which is least” 16:10Little things show what a man is, and rebuild trust.
  4. Masters“Ye cannot serve God and mammon” 16:13Whatever runs your life is your master. Only one fits.

Question 1 of 6

Shrewd

Why did the master praise a dishonest steward?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Not for cheating. He still called him unjust. He praised him for being sharp: he saw his end coming and used what he had to get ready. Jesus wants His people to think that hard about eternity.

Question 2 of 6

Money

How should a believer use money?

What verses tell us about this?

“But lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where neither moth nor rust doth corrupt, and where thieves do not break through nor steal:”

Matthew 6:20

“For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also.”

Matthew 6:21

Answer

As a tool, not a master. Money won't last, so we use it now for what will: helping people and supporting the work that brings men to Christ. That's treasure in heaven.

Question 3 of 6

Faithful

What does faithfulness in little things show?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

What a man really is. The way he handles small things, like money, is the way he'll handle big things. God trusts the true riches to men who are faithful with the least.

Question 4 of 6

Masters

Why can't a man serve God and mammon?

What verses tell us about this?

“For the love of money is the root of all evil: which while some coveted after, they have erred from the faith, and pierced themselves through with many sorrows.”

1 Timothy 6:10

Answer

Because a servant can only truly belong to one master. Two will pull him apart until he loves one and despises the other. Whatever we live for becomes our master, and God won't share the throne.

Question 5 of 6

Eternity

What does the rich man and Lazarus teach about life after death?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

It's real and conscious, and it starts at death: comfort for one, torment for the other. Men remember, feel and speak. And the gulf is fixed, so there's no changing sides afterward. The time to turn to God is now.

Question 6 of 6

The Word

Why wouldn't the brothers believe even if one rose from the dead?

What verses tell us about this?

“For had ye believed Moses, ye would have believed me: for he wrote of me.”

John 5:46

Answer

Because the problem wasn't a lack of evidence. God had already spoken in the Scriptures. A heart that won't hear God's Word won't be moved by a miracle either. Jesus did rise, and many still refused.
