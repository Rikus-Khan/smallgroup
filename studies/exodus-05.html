Who is the LORD?

Moses and Aaron ask Pharaoh to let Israel go, and Pharaoh says he has never heard of the LORD. The work gets harder, the people turn on Moses, and Moses takes it to God: “why is it that thou hast sent me?”

Part one

“Who is the LORD?”

Verses 1–5

Moses brings God's word to Pharaoh, and Pharaoh has never heard of Him.

“Who is the LORD?”

Who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice?

Pharaoh's answer is honest: I know not the LORD. Egypt had many gods, and counted its king one. A God of slaves was nobody he had ever heard of.

It was a refusal in the form of a question. But the LORD takes it seriously, and answers it over the chapters that follow:

“And the Egyptians shall know that I am the LORD, when I stretch forth mine hand upon Egypt”

Exodus 7:5

Most of us have said it in some form. He answers by who He is and what He does, and He is patient with the asking.

A small request

Moses and AaronThe God of the Hebrews hath met with us: let us go, we pray thee, three days’ journey into the desert, and sacrifice unto the LORD our God.

PharaohWherefore do ye, Moses and Aaron, let the people from their works? get you unto your burdens.

They asked for only three days, to worship: not for freedom, not yet. Pestilence is a deadly disease. Burdens are the loads of forced labour.

Worship heard as rebellion

Pharaoh heard worship as rest, and rest as rebellion. “The people of the land now are many, and ye make them rest from their burdens.” He had feared their numbers from the start (1:9–10).

“Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest.”

Matthew 11:28

Pharaoh's word to a slave is get you unto your burdens. Jesus' word is I will give you rest. Two masters, two words.

Part two

“Straw”

Verses 6–14

The answer to “let my people go” is more work, with fewer tools.

The same bricks, no straw

Before

Straw was given

The people made their daily number of bricks, and the straw came to them.

The same day

Go and find your own

“Let them go and gather straw for themselves.” The number of bricks does not change.

Mud bricks were mixed with chopped straw to hold them together. Without it they crumble. Pharaoh took the one thing that made the work possible and kept the number the same.

And look at his reason: “they be idle; therefore they cry, saying, Let us go and sacrifice to our God.” He treats a request to worship as laziness, and answers it with more work so they will have no time for “vain words.” To him, the words of God were vain.

Words from the brickyard

  • taskmastersEgyptian overseers who drove the work
  • officersIsraelite foremen, answerable to the taskmasters (verse 14)
  • the tale of the bricksthe count of bricks: tale here means number
  • oughtanything: “not ought of your work shall be diminished” means not a thing
  • minishto make less (verse 19). Diminish means the same
  • stubblethe short stalks left standing after the grain is cut
  • heretoforeuntil now, before this
  • thereofof it

So the people “were scattered abroad throughout all the land of Egypt” hunting for stubble, and the taskmasters “hasted them,” hurrying them: fulfil your works, your daily tasks, as when there was straw. Fulfil means finish.

It is the oldest cruelty there is: demand the same result, take the tools. A man who has been told to produce with nothing knows how it feels to fail every day, and to be told it is his fault.

Who took the blows

Pharaoh“No straw”Egyptian taskmasterspressedIsraelite officerswere beatenthe people

Pharaoh's order went down a chain. The Egyptian taskmasters made the Israelite officers answer for the shortfall, and when the bricks were short, the officers were the ones “beaten.” Their own people were set over them, and their own backs paid.

These men stood between the crowd and the whip. They were not the problem, and they paid first. Next, they go to find someone to appeal to.

Part three

“Why hast thou sent me?”

Verses 15–23

Everyone has someone to blame. Moses takes it to God.

“Ye are idle, ye are idle”

The officers went to Pharaoh himself and called themselves “thy servants” three times. They said plainly that the fault was not theirs: no straw was given. And Pharaoh said it twice: Ye are idle, ye are idle.

That is what power says to the people it is crushing: lazy. It is easier than listening. If they are idle, he need not ask why they are tired.

Some men here were called idle, useless, a waste, by someone who should have known better. It stuck. God never calls the crushed idle. His words at the bush were: “I have seen… I have heard… I know their sorrows.”

“I have surely seen the affliction of my people which are in Egypt, and have heard their cry by reason of their taskmasters; for I know their sorrows”

Exodus 3:7

The messenger gets the blame

Out of Pharaoh's court, the officers meet Moses and Aaron, “who stood in the way.” They did what Moses had asked, and now they said: The LORD look upon you, and judge.

Savour is a smell. They said Moses had made them stink in Pharaoh's nose, and given him “a sword in their hand to slay us”: an excuse to kill them. To abhor is to hate and shrink from.

Moses had done exactly what God sent him to do, and things got worse. Obedience is not always followed by an easier road. Often, when a man turns toward God, the first thing he meets is more resistance: old friends, old habits, old debts, and sometimes his own people.

That does not mean God has left. It does not mean you took a wrong step. Do not quit at the first “worse.” Tell a brother how it is going, and keep walking.

Moses takes it to God

MosesLord, wherefore hast thou so evil entreated this people? why is it that thou hast sent me? … neither hast thou delivered thy people at all.

the LORD, at the start of the next chapterNow shalt thou see what I will do to Pharaoh: for with a strong hand shall he let them go.

It is a hard thing to say to God, and the LORD does not scold him. Evil entreated means treated badly.

He took it to the right place

“I poured out my complaint before him; I shewed before him my trouble.”

Psalm 142:2

God does not scold him. He answers in the very next verse, from the very next chapter. Moses said, “neither hast thou delivered thy people at all.” God said, Now shalt thou see.

Real world: if you have been asking God “why,” say it out loud to Him today, and tell your brothers what you said. It is a prayer, and He hears it.

Points to remember

  1. Pharaoh“Who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice?” 5:2A proud man cannot obey a God he will not know. The LORD answers by what He does.
  2. The request“let us go, we pray thee, three days’ journey into the desert, and sacrifice unto the LORD our God” 5:3A small request: time to worship. Pharaoh heard it as rebellion.
  3. The bricks“Ye shall no more give the people straw to make brick” 5:7Cruelty asks for the same result and takes away the tools.
  4. The officers“thy servants are beaten; but the fault is in thine own people” 5:16They stood between the people and the whip, and were called idle.
  5. Blame“The LORD look upon you, and judge” 5:21The right step can make things worse first, and the messenger takes the blame.
  6. The complaint“wherefore hast thou so evil entreated this people? why is it that thou hast sent me?” 5:22Moses took his hardest question to the LORD. So can you.

Pharaoh

Answer

“Who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice? I know not the LORD.” He would not obey a God he did not know. The LORD answered him over the next chapters by what He did, until the Egyptians knew. Many of us once said the same, and He was patient with us.

Question 2 of 6

The request

What exactly did Moses and Aaron ask for?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Three days' journey into the desert, to hold a feast and sacrifice to the LORD. Not yet their freedom, only time to worship. Pharaoh called it a reason to be idle, and refused.
