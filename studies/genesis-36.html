Part one

Esau, who is Edom

Verses 1–8

The family of the brother who lost the blessing, and where he went.

Esau, who is Edom

Esau → Edom “Edom” means red, from the red stew Esau sold his birthright for, and from the red hair he was born with.

The chapter says it over and over: “Esau, who is Edom” (1), “Esau is Edom” (8), “Esau the father of the Edomites” (9, 43). A nickname from a bad bargain stuck to a whole nation:

“And Esau said to Jacob, Feed me, I pray thee, with that same red pottage; for I am faint: therefore was his name called Edom.”

Genesis 25:30

It is a quiet warning. One careless trade for a bowl of food, and the name followed him, and his family, for the rest of the Bible.

Esau's three wives

  1. Adahdaughter of Elon the Hittite. Mother of Eliphaz.
  2. Aholibamahdaughter of Anah, a Hivite. Mother of Jeush, Jaalam and Korah.
  3. BashemathIshmael's daughter, sister of Nebajoth. Mother of Reuel.

Two of these were Canaanite women, and the Bible tells us what Isaac and Rebekah thought of that:

“And Esau was forty years old when he took to wife Judith the daughter of Beeri the Hittite, and Bashemath the daughter of Elon the Hittite: Which were a grief of mind unto Isaac and to Rebekah.”

Genesis 26:34–35

The names do not line up exactly with chapters 26 and 28 (Judith, Bashemath, Mahalath). The usual explanation is that the women carried two names. The text does not say. What it does say is that this was Esau's household, and that Esau, who married Ishmael's daughter to try to please his father (28:9), was still at odds with the family God had chosen.

Esau makes room

Mount Seir is the rugged mountain country south-east of the Dead Sea. Their riches (their substance, all they owned) were too much for one land, so Esau took his wives, children and flocks and moved there.

The words sound like Abram and Lot (13:6). Abram let Lot choose; here Esau is the one who steps aside. The man who once said in his heart that he would kill his brother (27:41) now goes “from the face of his brother Jacob” without a fight.

Peace sometimes means making room

Esau was not the chosen son, and he knew it. He had lost the birthright and the blessing, and he had once wanted Jacob dead. Yet when they both had too much to share one country, he moved. There is a bright spot in that, and a lesson for any man with a brother, a father, or an ex-friend he cannot stand:

God had a hand in it too, as He reminded Israel centuries later:

“Meddle not with them; for I will not give you of their land, no, not so much as a footbreadth; because I have given mount Seir unto Esau for a possession.”

Deuteronomy 2:5

“If it be possible, as much as lieth in you, live peaceably with all men.”

Romans 12:18

If it be possible, as much as lieth in you. You cannot control the other man. You can control whether you keep fighting. Sometimes the peaceful thing is a longer drive, a different church pew, or letting the last word go. Today, think of one person you are still at war with, and ask God what your part in peace is.

Bare means gave birth to. A concubine was a lesser wife; Timna was Eliphaz's, and she bore him Amalek. The people who came from him, the Amalekites, would be the first to attack Israel after the Exodus (Exodus 17:8). Temanites show up later too: one of Job's friends was Eliphaz the Temanite.

Notice that the Bible gives Esau a full family tree of his own. He is not erased. God keeps track of him as carefully as He keeps track of Jacob.

A “duke” in Edom

אַלּוּף said alluf a chief, the head of a clan

The KJV's word duke comes from the Latin for leader. It does not mean a lord in a castle. These were the heads of the clans, the fathers of the families that came from Esau, each over his own people and territory. Verses 15–19 name more than a dozen, and verses 40–43 list them again by their places.

Edom grew up fast: clan chiefs, then kings, while Jacob's family was still a family of shepherds. Isaac had said Esau would live by his sword and in time break his brother's yoke (27:40). This chapter shows his family on its way.

The people who were there first

Seir the Horite and his family lived in these mountains before Esau came. Esau's children married into them (Aholibamah was Anah's daughter, verses 2 and 25), and in time the Edomites took over. The Horites are named here so that the story of the land is not lost.

Then, in the middle of a list, one small story: Anah found something while he fed his father's donkeys. The word “mules” is rare and nobody is sure of it; some teachers, going back to the old Latin Bible, think it means hot springs. Whatever he found, somebody remembered that Anah found it, minding the family's animals on an ordinary day.

Not every name in the Bible is a king or a prophet. Most are men who worked and left a family. God put the name of a man who watched donkeys in His book.

Read it as it is written: “And Bela died, and Jobab the son of Zerah of Bozrah reigned in his stead.” In his stead means in his place. Not one king is the son of the one before him. Each comes from a different town. (This Saul is not the Saul who was Israel's first king.)

Verse 31 says these reigned “before there reigned any king over the children of Israel.” God had promised Jacob that kings would come from him (35:11). Edom got its kings first. Israel's came later, and the promise ends in a King who reigns forever, from Judah's line.

He calleth them all by their names.

“He telleth the number of the stars; he calleth them all by their names.”

Psalm 147:4

Esau lost the birthright and the blessing, and he is not the hero of this book. He still has a whole chapter. His wives, his sons, his clan chiefs, the donkey-herd Anah, the kings of Edom: God wrote all of them down. He keeps account of people the world has long forgotten.

“To him the porter openeth; and the sheep hear his voice: and he calleth his own sheep by name, and leadeth them out.”

John 10:3

That includes you. Not the man you were pretending to be, and not the man with the record. He calls His own by name. Today, pick one man in this group, say his name to God in prayer, and tell him you did.

Points to remember

  1. Esau is Edom“Now these are the generations of Esau, who is Edom” 36:1Esau’s nickname from the red stew stuck to a whole nation. A careless trade can follow a man a long way.
  2. Making room“their riches were more than that they might dwell together” 36:7Esau stepped away from Jacob without a fight. Peace sometimes means making room.
  3. Kings first“And these are the kings that reigned in the land of Edom, before there reigned any king over the children of Israel” 36:31Edom’s eight kings came first, and none was a son of the last. The promised King would come later, through Judah.
  4. Every name“he is Esau the father of the Edomites” 36:43God keeps a full family tree for the brother who lost the blessing. He knows every man by name.

Question 1 of 4

Esau is Edom

Who is Edom, and where did Esau go to live?

What verses tell us about this?

“And Esau said to Jacob, Feed me, I pray thee, with that same red pottage; for I am faint: therefore was his name called Edom.”

Genesis 25:30

Answer

Edom is Esau. The name means red, from the red stew he sold his birthright for. After Canaan became too crowded for his flocks and Jacob’s, he moved to the mountain country of Seir, south-east of the Dead Sea, and his family became the Edomites.

Question 2 of 4

Making room

Why did Esau move away from Jacob?

What verses tell us about this?

“If it be possible, as much as lieth in you, live peaceably with all men.”

Romans 12:18

Answer

The two brothers were too rich to share one land, and Esau took his whole household to Mount Seir, away from his brother Jacob. The man who once wanted Jacob dead made room instead of fighting. God had given Seir to Esau as a possession (Deuteronomy 2:5).

Question 3 of 4

Kings first

Which family had kings first, Esau’s or Jacob’s?

What verses tell us about this?

“And God said unto him, I am God Almighty: be fruitful and multiply; a nation and a company of nations shall be of thee, and kings shall come out of thy loins;”

Genesis 35:11

Answer

Esau’s. Eight kings reigned in Edom before Israel had any king, and none was the son of the king before. God had promised Jacob kings from his own body, and that promise took longer. It ended in Jesus, the King from Judah’s line.

Question 4 of 4

Every name

Why would God put Esau’s whole family in the Bible?

What verses tell us about this?

“He telleth the number of the stars; he calleth them all by their names.”

Psalm 147:4

“To him the porter openeth; and the sheep hear his voice: and he calleth his own sheep by name, and leadeth them out.”

John 10:3

Answer

Because every family matters to Him. Esau lost the blessing and was not the chosen line, yet God kept his sons, his clan chiefs and his kings written down. He calls the stars by name, and He calls His own sheep by name. He knows you too.
