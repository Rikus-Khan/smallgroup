Genesis 35

Back to Bethel

God speaks first, Jacob cleans his camp of idols, and the family goes home. On the way a nurse dies, Rachel dies, and a father dies. Through all of it the promise holds.

Part one

Arise, go up to Bethel

Verses 1–15

After the mess at Shechem, God speaks first, and Jacob finally cleans out his own camp.

Clean house first

FirstPut away the strange gods
→
Thenbe clean
→
Thenchange your garments

Strange gods are the idols of other nations. Jacob's camp had some. Years before, Rachel had stolen her father's household gods (31:19), and a camp that has just plundered a city may have picked up more. The text does not say where they all came from. It says Jacob knew they were “among you.”

Jacob is about to go to the place where God first met him, and he will not take the idols along. The order matters: put away first, then be clean, then change your garments. A clean coat on top of a hidden idol is just a coat.

Every man here has something he has kept around just in case: the number in the phone, the bottle in the truck, the old friend. Today, name it to a brother, and ask him to help you get rid of it.

What Jacob feared, and what God did

What Jacob said (34:30)

“They shall gather themselves together against me”

He had good reason. His sons had wrecked a city. The neighbours had every reason to come for his family, and he was “few in number.”

What God did (35:5)

The terror of God

“The terror of God was upon the cities that were round about them, and they did not pursue after the sons of Jacob.” Nobody came. God protected the very family that had caused the trouble.

“He hath not dealt with us after our sins; nor rewarded us according to our iniquities.”

Psalm 103:10

This is grace, and it comes before Jacob cleaned house, not after. God did not wait for the family to deserve it. If you are waiting to be good enough before you go back to him, look at how this chapter starts.

The road home

Four stops, all south, about fifty miles in all.

Shechem to Bethel, about twenty miles. Luz was the old name of the town (28:19). Now Jacob builds an altar and calls it El-beth-el, “the God of Bethel.” He names the altar for God, not the place.

Bethel to Ephrath, about twelve miles. Ephrath is the old name for Bethlehem (verse 19). This is where Rachel dies.

Bethlehem to Hebron, about twenty miles more, to Mamre and his father Isaac (verse 27).

An oak for a servant

אַלּוֹן בָּכוּת said Allon-bachuth the oak of weeping

Deborah (not the judge of a later book) was Rebekah's nurse. She came with Rebekah from Haran, all those years ago (24:59), and is buried here. A nurse in that day was a woman who raised the children, and lived with the family for life.

Her name is in the Bible, and so is the name of the oak where she was buried. She was a servant, and nobody wrote her a long story. God had someone write down where she rests. Plenty of faithful people in your own life are not remembered the way they should be. He knows their names.

A new name, again

Jacob → Israel God had given the name at the Jabbok (32:28): “as a prince hast thou power with God and with men.” Now he says it again, at Bethel.

Read the rest of the chapter and you will see both names. “Israel journeyed” (21), and in the next verse “Israel dwelt”, but also “the sons of Jacob.” The old name and the new name run side by side. That is how it goes with a new believer too. God keeps calling him by the new name while the old habits still answer to the old one.

“I am God Almighty” is El Shaddai, the name God used with Abraham when he promised a son at ninety-nine (17:1). Then the old promise again: a nation, a company of nations, kings from his own body, and the land (11–12).

“And he said, Thy name shall be called no more Jacob, but Israel: for as a prince hast thou power with God and with men, and hast prevailed.”

Genesis 32:28

Back where it began

The first time (chapter 28)

A runaway with a stone

Alone, running from his brother, with a stone for his pillow. He set the stone up and poured oil on it, and made a vow: if God will be with me and keep me.

This time (35:14)

A family, and a drink offering

A household of his own and a flock. He sets up a pillar again, pours oil on it, and a drink offering, wine poured out to God as worship.

“And, behold, I am with thee, and will keep thee in all places whither thou goest, and will bring thee again into this land; for I will not leave thee, until I have done that which I have spoken to thee of.”

Genesis 28:15

God had said I will bring thee again into this land, and here Jacob stands, twenty years or more later, a long road behind him. God kept every word. The man was a mess, and the promise held anyway.

Part two

Two names, one grave

Verses 16–26

On the road to Bethlehem, Rachel dies giving birth. Her last word names her son, and his father changes it.

Ben-oni becomes Benjamin

Ben-oni → Benjamin “Son of my sorrow” becomes “son of the right hand.”

Rachel named him as she was dying: Ben-oni, son of my sorrow. Jacob changed it. Benjamin means son of the right hand, the place of strength and favour. He had just lost the wife he loved most, and he picked a name for hope.

The grief was real, and Jacob did not pretend otherwise. But the child was going to grow up under a name that said something good. If your worst day has a name on it, you are allowed to decide what the next chapter is called.

“The LORD is nigh unto them that are of a broken heart; and saveth such as be of a contrite spirit.”

Psalm 34:18

Ephrath, which is Bethlehem

Rachel is buried by the road to Ephrath, which is Bethlehem. Jacob set a pillar on her grave, “unto this day”, meaning the day Moses wrote it down. The place matters, because this is the town Micah names:

“But thou, Bethlehem Ephratah, though thou be little among the thousands of Judah, yet out of thee shall he come forth unto me that is to be ruler in Israel; whose goings forth have been from of old, from everlasting.”

Micah 5:2

Nearly two thousand years later, Herod's soldiers killed the baby boys of Bethlehem, looking for the child born there. Matthew hears Rachel weeping again:

“In Rama was there a voice heard, lamentation, and weeping, and great mourning, Rachel weeping for her children, and would not be comforted, because they are not.”

Matthew 2:18

Rachel's sorrow, and the sorrow of Bethlehem's mothers, is not hidden from God. And it is on Bethlehem's road that he brings the Child who will deal with death itself.

One verse, and a stop

The Bible gives Reuben's sin one line. He “lay with Bilhah his father's concubine.” A concubine was a lesser wife; Bilhah was Rachel's maid, who had given her to Jacob to bear sons (30:3–4). It was sin against his father, against Bilhah, and against God. The sentence stops there: “and Israel heard it.” We will stop there too.

What Reuben was (49:3)

Firstborn, “my might”

The oldest son, “the beginning of my strength, the excellency of dignity.”

What it cost him

“Unstable as water”

Jacob's last verdict: “thou shalt not excel; because thou wentest up to thy father's bed.” The birthright went to Joseph's sons (1 Chronicles 5:1).

Notice that sin has a price, and it can last. But notice, too, that Reuben is still in the list of twelve. In chapter 37, you will find him the one trying to talk his brothers out of killing Joseph. A man can fail badly and still be counted in the family.

Four mothers, twelve sons. Verse 26 says they “were born to him in Padan-aram”, which is true of eleven; Benjamin, just born, was the exception, and the list gives the family as a whole.

Look at who is in this tree: Simeon and Levi, who did the sword work at Shechem; Reuben; Judah, whose own story is coming in chapter 38; Joseph, whose story fills the rest of the book. And through Judah's line, a King will come. Not one of the twelve was a spotless man, and God put his name on all of them: the tribes of Israel.

Part three

Isaac is gathered to his people

Verses 27–29

Jacob comes home to Hebron in time to bury his father.

A hundred and eighty years

  1. BornAbraham is a hundred21:5
  2. On the altarand the ram in the thicket22
  3. Fortymarries Rebekah25:20
  4. Sixtytwins, Esau and Jacob25:26
  5. Oldhis eyes dim, blesses Jacob27
  6. A hundred and eightydies at Hebron35:28–29

“Gave up the ghost” means breathed his last. “Gathered unto his people” is how the Bible speaks of a believer's death: not the end, but a gathering. “Full of days” means he lived a full life and was satisfied. Fourscore is eighty, so a hundred and fourscore is a hundred and eighty.

One thing to notice: going by the ages in Genesis, Isaac outlived Joseph being sold into Egypt by about a dozen years. The writer closes Isaac's story here, in this chapter of Jacob's homecoming, before turning to Joseph.

Two brothers at one grave

Look at who buried Isaac: “his sons Esau and Jacob.” Esau first. These two had once been enemies: one stole a blessing, the other planned to kill him (27:41). Then, years later, when Jacob came home:

“And Esau ran to meet him, and embraced him, and fell on his neck, and kissed him: and they wept.”

Genesis 33:4

Now they stand together at their father's grave. Not every broken family gets this. Some of you have a brother or a father you may never be able to bury beside. But where the door is open, walk through it. Reconciliation can take twenty years and a funeral, and it is still worth it.

  1. God speaks first“Arise, go up to Bethel, and dwell there” 35:1God spoke first, to a man who had drifted. He still calls his own back.

Question 1 of 5

God speaks first

After the trouble at Shechem, who tells Jacob where to go, and where?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

God did, and he spoke first. “Arise, go up to Bethel,” back to the place where he had met Jacob as a runaway. This is right after the shame of chapter 34, when nobody in the family had asked God anything. Jacob built an altar there and named it El-beth-el, the God of Bethel.

Question 2 of 5

Clean house

What did Jacob tell his household to put away before they went up?

What verses tell us about this?

“For they themselves shew of us what manner of entering in we had unto you, and how ye turned to God from idols to serve the living and true God;”

1 Thessalonians 1:9

Answer

The strange gods, the idols of other nations, and his people gave up their earrings too. He told them to be clean and change their garments. Jacob hid the idols under the oak and left them behind. A man cannot carry his old gods and the Lord up the same road.

Question 3 of 5

A name and a promise

What did God say to Jacob when he appeared to him again at Bethel?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

He said the name Israel again, told him “I am God Almighty,” and repeated the promises: a nation and a company of nations, kings from his own body, and the land he gave Abraham and Isaac. It is the same promise he made at Bethel when Jacob was running away, and he had kept it.

Question 4 of 5

Two names

What happened to Rachel, and what did her baby get called?

What verses tell us about this?

“In Rama was there a voice heard, lamentation, and weeping, and great mourning, Rachel weeping for her children, and would not be comforted, because they are not.”

Matthew 2:18

Answer

Rachel died giving birth, on the road to Ephrath, which is Bethlehem. As she was dying she called her son Ben-oni, son of my sorrow. His father called him Benjamin, son of the right hand. He chose a name of hope in the middle of his grief.

Question 5 of 5

Heavy news

What sorrows and failures does this chapter hold, and did God still keep his promise?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Deborah the nurse dies, Rachel dies, Reuben sins with his father’s concubine, and Isaac dies. Yet God’s promise stands: “the land which I gave Abraham and Isaac, to thee I will give it.” Sorrow and failure are in the chapter, and so is the God who keeps his word through them.
