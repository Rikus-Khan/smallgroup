Micah 1

The LORD comes down

A country preacher sees the LORD step out of heaven to judge His own people, and he weeps over every town on the road.

The book of Micah

Who: Micah, a countryman from Moresheth, about twenty miles southwest of Jerusalem.

When: about 740 to 700 BC, under Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah, before the exile. Assyria is rising.

To whom: Samaria and Jerusalem, the capitals of Israel and Judah, but mostly Judah.

He saw Samaria fall (722) and Assyria reach Jerusalem's gates (701). He named Bethlehem as the Ruler's birthplace (5:2, Matthew 2:6).

Part one

The LORD cometh

Verses 1–7

Heaven opens a courtroom, and the Judge steps down onto the mountains.

A name that asks a question

מִיכָה said Mee-kah, short for Mikayahu “Who is like the LORD?”

“The Morasthite” means the man from Moresheth. His name asks the question his book answers. Chapter 1 is hard, but listen to how he ends:

“Who is a God like unto thee, that pardoneth iniquity, and passeth by the transgression of the remnant of his heritage? he retaineth not his anger for ever, because he delighteth in mercy.”

Micah 7:18

Court is in session

Verse 2 sounds like a courtroom. The whole earth is called in to listen, and the Lord GOD Himself takes the stand as witness against His people. He saw it all.

Micah 1:4

“And the mountains shall be molten under him, and the valleys shall be cleft, as wax before the fire…”

Psalm 97:5

“The hills melted like wax at the presence of the LORD, at the presence of the Lord of the whole earth.”

“High places” were hilltop shrines where people worshipped idols. When the LORD comes, He walks right over them. The mountains men thought were solid run like candle wax. Nothing we build to hide behind will stand up when He shows up.

The rot starts at the top

“Jacob” is the whole nation, named after its father. God asks where its sin lives, and answers with an address:

“What is the transgression of Jacob?”SamariaThe capital of the north, home of its kings, its court and its golden calves.
“What are the high places of Judah?”JerusalemThe capital of the south, the city of God's own temple. Even there, idols had moved in. King Ahaz put up altars “in every corner of Jerusalem” (2 Chronicles 28:24).

The sickness didn't start in the small towns. It started in the capitals, with the men in charge, and flowed downhill. Whatever a man allows at the head of his life, his home picks up.

“The hire of an harlot”

“Hire” means wages. Idol worship came with temple prostitution, and the money covered Samaria's idols in silver and gold (v. 7).

Assyria would haul it all home to deck out their own idol temples. What sin pays, sin takes back.

Every man in recovery knows the math. What did the habit ever give you that it didn't take back with interest?

It happened

  1. about 740 BCMicah begins to preach
  2. 722Samaria falls to Assyriaverse 6
  3. 701Assyria takes Judah's walled townsverses 9–16
  4. 701Jerusalem is spared2 Kings 19

“In the ninth year of Hoshea the king of Assyria took Samaria, and carried Israel away into Assyria, and placed them in Halah and in Habor by the river of Gozan, and in the cities of the Medes.”

2 Kings 17:6

Samaria sat on a hill covered with vineyards. Within a few years of Micah's warning it was a pile of stones, and the hill went back to being farmland. God gave the north some two hundred years of prophets first. His judgment came late, and it came true.

Part two

I will wail

Verses 8–16

The trouble reaches Micah's own back yard, and he walks the road town by town.

A prophet who wept

Micah doesn't preach judgment with a smirk. He goes barefoot and stripped down like a captive, crying out loud. “Dragons” here are most likely jackals, and the “owls” desert birds, both known for their eerie cries at night.

Micah 1:8–9

“I will wail and howl”

The wound has spread from Samaria to the gate of his own people. He feels it as his own.

Luke 19:41

“He beheld the city, and wept over it”

Seven hundred years later, Jesus looked at the same Jerusalem and wept too.

God's men don't enjoy other people's ruin. When we see a man going down, even one who earned it, the right response is tears and prayer, not “told you so.”

Every town's name is a sermon

Micah plays on the sound of each town's name. In English it's lost, so here's what the Hebrew ear heard:

  1. Gathsounds like “tell”: tell it not
  2. Aphrah“house of dust”: roll in the dust
  3. Saphir“pretty town”: led off naked and ashamed
  4. Zaanansounds like “come out”: came not forth, too scared
  5. Beth-ezel“house next door”: no help from the neighbors
  6. Maroth“bitterness”: waited for good, got bitter news

“Tell it not in Gath” echoes David's lament when King Saul died (2 Samuel 1:20). Gath was a Philistine city. Don't let the enemy hear of it and laugh.

“Evil” in verse 12 means disaster, not wickedness. God doesn't do wrong. But He does send hard things, and He is the One who says when it stops: unto the gate, and no farther.

Down the road to Jerusalem

  1. Lachishsounds like “horse”: hitch up and run
  2. MoreshethMicah's home: given away like a bride
  3. Achzib“a lie”: like a creek that runs dry
  4. Mareshah“the heir”: a new owner is coming

In 701 Sennacherib of Assyria besieged Lachish, Judah's biggest fortress after Jerusalem.

From there he sent his army to Jerusalem (2 Kings 18:17). “Unto the gate of my people”, but not through it.

Trusting the chariots

Lachish was a chariot city. Many think that is why God calls her “the beginning of the sin” to Jerusalem: she taught Judah to trust in horses and walls instead of the LORD. When the enemy came, the only use left for her horses was running away.

“Now in the fourteenth year of king Hezekiah did Sennacherib king of Assyria come up against all the fenced cities of Judah, and took them.”

2 Kings 18:13

“Fenced cities” are walled towns. Every one of them fell. The things we lean on in place of God can look strong for years. They fail on the day we need them.

Back to the cave

Judah's leading men, “the glory of Israel,” will end up hiding in the caves of Adullam. “Make thee bald, and poll thee” means shave your head, a sign of mourning. The “eagle” is likely the bald-headed vulture.

But Adullam is the cave where David hid when Saul was hunting him (1 Samuel 22:1), and look who came to him there:

“And every one that was in distress, and every one that was in debt, and every one that was discontented, gathered themselves unto him; and he became a captain over them: and there were with him about four hundred men.”

1 Samuel 22:2

That crew became David's mighty men. Rock bottom is a bad place to live, but a good place to meet the King. Jesus, the Son of David, still takes in men like that.

Points to remember

  1. God's holiness“Let the Lord GOD be witness against you” 1:2He saw it all. A holy God won't wink at sin, even in His own people.
  2. Sin“Is it not Samaria?” 1:5The rot started at the head and flowed down. Guard what you allow at the top.
  3. Sin's wages“They shall return to the hire of an harlot” 1:7What sin pays, sin takes back.
  4. God's heart“Therefore I will wail and howl” 1:8God's men weep over judgment. They don't gloat.
  5. Hope“He shall come unto Adullam” 1:15Rock bottom is where many men first meet the King.

Question 1 of 5

God's holiness

Why does the LORD come down against His own people?

What verses tell us about this?

“The Lord is not slack concerning his promise, as some men count slackness; but is longsuffering to us-ward, not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance.”

2 Peter 3:9

Answer

Because of their sin, and because He is holy. Being God's people never meant they could do as they pleased. He had been patient for generations, sending prophet after prophet. His delay was mercy, giving them room to turn. When they wouldn't, He came, just as He said.

Question 2 of 5

Sin

Where did the rot in Israel and Judah start?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

At the top: in the capital cities, with the kings and leaders. From there it ran downhill into every town. The same is true of a man's life. Whatever sits on the throne of your heart sets the course for everything under it. That's why the first thing to settle is who is King.

Question 3 of 5

Sin's wages

What happens to what we gain by sin?

What verses tell us about this?

“What fruit had ye then in those things whereof ye are now ashamed? for the end of those things is death.”

Romans 6:21

“For the wages of sin is death; but the gift of God is eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord.”

Romans 6:23

Answer

It doesn't last. Samaria's gold went back to the idol temples it came from. Sin always takes back what it gives, and more. The good news is in the second half of Romans 6:23: God doesn't pay wages. He gives a gift, eternal life in Jesus, and that gift can't be taken back.

Question 4 of 5

God's heart

How should we feel when judgment falls on others?

What verses tell us about this?

“And when he was come near, he beheld the city, and wept over it,”

Luke 19:41

Answer

Heartbroken, not smug. Micah preached hard truth, and he cried while he did it. Jesus did the same over Jerusalem. When a brother falls, or a man pays for what he did, we tell the truth, and we grieve and pray for him. We were not better. We were rescued.

Question 5 of 5

Hope

Where can a man run when everything falls apart?

What verses tell us about this?

“And every one that was in distress, and every one that was in debt, and every one that was discontented, gathered themselves unto him; and he became a captain over them: and there were with him about four hundred men.”

1 Samuel 22:2

“Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest.”

Matthew 11:28

Answer

To the King. Judah's proud men would end up in the caves of Adullam, but that's where broken, indebted, fed-up men once found David and became his mighty men. Jesus, David's greater Son, says the same thing today: come to Me. Rock bottom can be the start of the story.
