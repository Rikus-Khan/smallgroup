Deuteronomy 24

The bill of divorcement, the poor man's coat, and the sheaf left in the field

A law that limits divorce, which Jesus read for us. A year at home for the new husband. No taking a man's millstone or keeping his coat overnight. Pay the working man before sundown. Every man for his own sin. And leave something in the field for the stranger, the fatherless and the widow.

Part one

“A bill of divorcement”

Verses 1–5

What Moses allowed, why he allowed it, and the year a new husband stayed home.

What the law did

  1. 1“some uncleanness”
  2. 2a written bill, in her hand
  3. 3“she may go and be another man's wife”
  4. 4no taking her back

A bill of divorcement is a written paper of divorce. Uncleanness here is something shameful the husband found; Moses doesn't say more. The latter husband is the second one.

Read it slowly: Moses does not command anyone to divorce. He says if a man does it, it must be in writing, put in her hand, so she is free and not left hanging; and he may not take her back after she has been another man's wife. The law put a fence around something already going on, so a wife could not be sent off and fetched back like a tool.

Jesus reads this chapter

“The Pharisees also came unto him, tempting him, and saying unto him, Is it lawful for a man to put away his wife for every cause?”

Matthew 19:3

“Wherefore they are no more twain, but one flesh. What therefore God hath joined together, let not man put asunder.”

Matthew 19:6

“They say unto him, Why did Moses then command to give a writing of divorcement, and to put her away? He saith unto them, Moses because of the hardness of your hearts suffered you to put away your wives: but from the beginning it was not so.”

Matthew 19:7–8

They said Moses commanded it. Jesus said Moses suffered it, that is, allowed it, “because of the hardness of your hearts”. Then he went back past Deuteronomy to the beginning: God joined them, one flesh. Moses' law dealt with men as they were. Jesus showed what God meant marriage to be.

If divorce is in your story

“And I say unto you, Whosoever shall put away his wife, except it be for fornication, and shall marry another, committeth adultery: and whoso marrieth her which is put away doth commit adultery.”

Matthew 19:9

“For the LORD, the God of Israel, saith that he hateth putting away:”

Malachi 2:16

Many of us have a divorce behind us. Some did the leaving; some were left. Jesus does not soften what God thinks of it, and his blood reaches every sin in it.

“And such were some of you: but ye are washed, but ye are sanctified, but ye are justified in the name of the Lord Jesus, and by the Spirit of our God.”

1 Corinthians 6:11

Own your part before God, without excuses and without hating her. Pay what you owe. Don't run down your children's mother in front of them. If you are married now, guard that marriage with everything you have. Hard questions about your own case are for your pastor, face to face.

A year at home

In the same breath as the law about divorce comes a law to keep a marriage from ever getting there. A new husband was not to go to war, nor be charged with any business (given public duties). For one year he was to be “free at home”, and to cheer up his wife: to make her glad.

  • Before the warIsrael's army could wait. The marriage came first.
  • Before the businessThe nation's work could wait. She came first.
  • Her joyThe point of the year is not his rest, but “his wife which he hath taken”.

Most of us did it the other way: the job, the buddies, the bottle, and the wife got what was left. God built the first year of a marriage around her. It is never too late to put her ahead of the work and the war today.

Part two

“He taketh a man's life to pledge”

Verses 6–15

The millstone, the man-stealer, Miriam, the coat, and the day's pay.

The millstone and the pledge

A pledge is something a borrower handed over to show he would pay the loan back. Bread began with grain ground between two stones: the nether (lower) one, and the upper one turned on top of it.

Take either stone, and the family has no bread that day. That is why Moses says it: “he taketh a man’s life to pledge.” A lender may protect his loan. He may not take the thing a man lives by.

Stealing a man

Verse 7 is about kidnapping: stealing a man to make merchandise of him, to sell him as a slave. Joseph's brothers did it to him (Genesis 37:28). God's law put it beside murder.

“And he that stealeth a man, and selleth him, or if he be found in his hand, he shall surely be put to death.”

Exodus 21:16

“For whoremongers, for them that defile themselves with mankind, for menstealers, for liars, for perjured persons, and if there be any other thing that is contrary to sound doctrine;”

1 Timothy 1:10

Paul still names menstealers in his list. Anyone who has seen men or women bought and sold, for work, for drugs, or for the street, knows this is no old-time sin. God sees every one of them as a man or woman, never as merchandise.

“Remember… Miriam”

With leprosy, Israel was to do just what the priests taught (Leviticus 13–14). Then Moses says: remember Miriam. We read her story in Numbers 12: she spoke against Moses, and the LORD struck her.

“And the cloud departed from off the tabernacle; and, behold, Miriam became leprous, white as snow: and Aaron looked upon Miriam, and, behold, she was leprous.”

Numbers 12:10

“And Miriam was shut out from the camp seven days: and the people journeyed not till Miriam was brought in again.”

Numbers 12:15

Remember both halves. God took her sin seriously, even in Moses' own sister. And he healed her, and the whole camp waited for her to come back. A brother put out for his sin is to be brought in again, and nobody leaves without him.

Stand outside, and give back the coat

  1. Lendto your brother
  2. Stand abroadoutside his door
  3. He brings it outhis own choice
  4. Sundowngive back the coat
  5. He sleeps“and bless thee”

Abroad means outside. The lender was not to walk into a poor man's house and pick what he wanted. The man kept his dignity. And if the poor man's pledge was his raiment, the coat he slept in, it went back to him every night.

“For that is his covering only, it is his raiment for his skin: wherein shall he sleep? and it shall come to pass, when he crieth unto me, that I will hear; for I am gracious.”

Exodus 22:27

Paid before sundown

The hired servant worked by the day, and his hire (his wages) fed his family that night. “He is poor, and setteth his heart upon it”: he has been counting on it all day. Israelite or stranger, it made no difference.

“Behold, the hire of the labourers who have reaped down your fields, which is of you kept back by fraud, crieth: and the cries of them which have reaped are entered into the ears of the Lord of sabaoth.”

James 5:4

Wages held back cry out, and the Lord hears them. If you run a crew, pay them on time. If you owe a man for work, settle it. And if you are the one working, God sees your sweat and your pay.

Part three

“Every man shall be put to death for his own sin”

Verses 16–22

No son punished for his father, and a sheaf left behind on purpose.

Not for your father's sin

In Israel's courts, a son was never to be put to death for his father's crime, nor a father for his son's. Years later a king kept this very verse:

“But the children of the murderers he slew not: according unto that which is written in the book of the law of Moses, wherein the LORD commanded, saying, The fathers shall not be put to death for the children, nor the children be put to death for the fathers; but every man shall be put to death for his own sin.”

2 Kings 14:6

“The soul that sinneth, it shall die. The son shall not bear the iniquity of the father, neither shall the father bear the iniquity of the son: the righteousness of the righteous shall be upon him, and the wickedness of the wicked shall be upon him.”

Ezekiel 18:20

Some of us had a father who drank, fought, or left. His sin is not your sentence. And your sons do not have to carry yours. The line can stop with you.

The one who died for another's sin

The law, 24:16

Every man for his own

No man may die for another man's sin. That is justice, and God keeps it.

The gospel

The just for the unjust

One Man had no sin of his own to die for, and he chose to die for ours.

“For Christ also hath once suffered for sins, the just for the unjust, that he might bring us to God, being put to death in the flesh, but quickened by the Spirit:”

1 Peter 3:18

By this law every one of us stands for his own sin. That is the bad news. The good news is that Christ, who owed nothing, stood in our place of his own free will, and “he might bring us to God”.

Remember where you came from

To pervert the judgment is to twist a court case against someone who can't fight back: the stranger, the fatherless, the widow. And the widow's coat was not to be taken in pledge at all.

Why? Not “because they deserve it,” but because of what you were: “thou wast a bondman in Egypt, and the LORD thy God redeemed thee thence.” We met these words in chapters 5 and 15; here Moses ties them to the court and the pledge (18), and again to the harvest (22).

A court was where a poor man was easiest to cheat, because nobody would take his side. Be the man who takes it: speak up for the one with no family, no money and no voice in the room.

Leave some behind

The fieldhast forgot a sheafdon't go back for it
The olive treethou beatest thine olive treedon't go over the boughs again
The vineyardthou gatherest the grapesdon't glean it afterward

Olives were knocked down by beating the boughs (branches). To glean is to go back over the field or vine for the bits that were missed. The farmer did one pass, and what was left was “for the stranger, for the fatherless, and for the widow”. The poor did the gathering themselves, with dignity. And God promised to bless “all the work of thine hands”.

A widow in the field

“And Ruth the Moabitess said unto Naomi, Let me now go to the field, and glean ears of corn after him in whose sight I shall find grace. And she said unto her, Go, my daughter.”

Ruth 2:2

“And let fall also some of the handfuls of purpose for her, and leave them, that she may glean them, and rebuke her not.”

Ruth 2:16

Years later a widow, a stranger from Moab, went out to glean under this very law. Boaz did more than the law asked: he had handfuls dropped “of purpose for her”. He married her, and she became the great-grandmother of David, and is named in the line of Christ (Matthew 1:5).

God has a way of meeting people in the leftovers. Ruth came looking for scraps, and found a family and a place in his story. Grace always gives more than the law requires.

Real world: pay what you owe

  • SinTreating people as things: a wife sent off, a man's millstone taken, a worker's pay held back, the poor man's coat kept overnight.
  • RegretA broken marriage, debts, wages we cheated or were cheated of. Own your part before God, without excuses.
  • Grace“Ye are washed” (1 Corinthians 6:11). Christ, the just for the unjust, died for sins that were not his (1 Peter 3:18).
  • HopeYour father's sin is not your sentence (Ezekiel 18:20), and Ruth found a family in the leftovers.
  • TodayPay one thing you owe, a debt, a wage or an apology, before the sun goes down. And leave something for someone who has less than you.

Points to remember

  1. Divorce“Moses because of the hardness of your hearts suffered you to put away your wives: but from the beginning it was not so.” Matthew 19:8Moses allowed it and fenced it in; Jesus points back to what God joined.
  2. The new husband“he shall be free at home one year, and shall cheer up his wife which he hath taken” 24:5His wife before the war and the work.
  3. The pledge“No man shall take the nether or the upper millstone to pledge: for he taketh a man’s life to pledge” 24:6Not the millstone, not the poor man's coat overnight, not the widow's raiment at all.
  4. The hired servant“At his day thou shalt give him his hire, neither shall the sun go down upon it” 24:15Pay the working man on the day, for he is counting on it.
  5. His own sin“every man shall be put to death for his own sin” 24:16No man for his father, and Christ, the just, for the unjust.
  6. The forgotten sheaf“it shall be for the stranger, for the fatherless, and for the widow” 24:19Leave some behind, and remember you were a bondman in Egypt.

Question 1 of 6

Divorce

Why did Moses allow a bill of divorcement, according to Jesus?

What verses tell us about this?

“He saith unto them, Moses because of the hardness of your hearts suffered you to put away your wives: but from the beginning it was not so.”

Matthew 19:8

Answer

Because of the hardness of their hearts. Moses allowed it and put limits on it, but from the beginning it was not so: what God joins, man is not to put asunder.

Question 2 of 6

The new husband

What was a newly married man to do for his first year?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Stay at home, free from war and public business, and cheer up his wife.

Question 3 of 6

The pledge

What was a lender not to take, or not to keep overnight?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

Not a man's millstone, for that is his life. Not the poor man's pledge overnight: it went back at sundown so he could sleep in it. And never a widow's raiment.

Question 4 of 6

The hired servant

When was the hired servant to be paid, and why?

What verses tell us about this?

“Behold, the hire of the labourers who have reaped down your fields, which is of you kept back by fraud, crieth: and the cries of them which have reaped are entered into the ears of the Lord of sabaoth.”

James 5:4

Answer

On the same day, before sundown, because he is poor and is counting on it, and so that he does not cry to the LORD against you.

Question 5 of 6

His own sin

For whose sin was a man to be put to death?

What verses tell us about this?

“For Christ also hath once suffered for sins, the just for the unjust, that he might bring us to God, being put to death in the flesh, but quickened by the Spirit:”

1 Peter 3:18

Answer

For his own sin, not his father's or his son's. Only Christ, who had no sin, died for the sins of others.

Question 6 of 6

The forgotten sheaf

Who was the forgotten sheaf left for, and why?

What verses tell us about this?

Answer

For the stranger, the fatherless and the widow, because Israel was to remember that they had been bondmen in Egypt. And God promised to bless the work of their hands.
